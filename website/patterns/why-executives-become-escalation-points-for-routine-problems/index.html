Why Executives Become Escalation Points for Routine Problems

Discounts, refunds, supplier delays and staff clashes all end up on the managing director's desk. Why routine problems escalate upward, and how to stop it.

Mellorca Patterns2026-11-16

Observable condition

The managing director's phone does not stop. A sales rep wants to know whether a long-standing customer can have an extra three percent. The warehouse wants to know whether to dispatch to a customer who is slightly over their limit. A customer is unhappy and has asked for the owner by name. Two team leaders disagree about who should cover a shift. None of these is a strategic decision. All of them end up with the most senior person in the business.

The executive spends the day resolving problems that someone else could have resolved, and the important work, such as pricing strategy, key accounts, hiring and investment, waits for evenings and weekends.

An illustrative example: the founder of a Western Cape food manufacturer that has grown to about ninety staff. Every credit note above a small amount, every price exception and most customer complaints still go to her, because that is how it worked when the business had fifteen people. Her managers are capable, but none of them has been told what they may decide.

Realisation

The realisation is that routine problems escalate to executives not because managers are weak, but because escalation to the top is the only route that is certain to work. When nobody has been given a clear right to decide, and there is no defined next step for a problem they cannot solve, staff go to the one person whose decision will not be questioned.

Decision frameworks exist to close exactly this gap. Bain's description of its RAPID tool sets out distinct roles for who recommends, who must agree, who gives input, who decides and who performs, and argues that clear roles reduce ambiguity and help teams move faster. The specific framework matters less than the principle: for each recurring type of decision, someone is named as the decider.

Realisation prompt: of the last twenty issues that reached you, how many could someone else have decided if they had been told they were allowed to?

Identification

This is a pattern of undefined decision rights. The visible symptom is senior leaders handling routine operational issues. The common assumption is that staff lack judgement or that the executive is simply better at these calls. The structural cause is that the business has not set who may decide what, within what limits, or where an issue goes when it is outside those limits. In the absence of a route, everything flows to the top.

It is closely linked to when routine decisions all wait for one manager, and to why the CEO is still chasing operational updates, which looks at the information side of the same operating model.

Diagnosis

  • Limits of authority are unwritten. Managers do not know how large a discount, refund or write-off they may approve, so they approve none.
  • Escalation routes are undefined. When a problem is outside someone's authority, there is no named next level below the executive.
  • Past overrules taught caution. Managers who made decisions that were later reversed learned that it is safer to ask first.
  • Customers have learned the route too. Key customers phone the owner directly because that has always produced results.
  • Approvals travel informally. Requests arrive by phone, chat and corridor conversation, so there is no record of what was decided and no way to see patterns.
  • The executive enjoys being useful. Solving problems is satisfying and visible, so the habit is reinforced from both sides.

Commercial impact

The commercial value at stake is leadership capacity, speed of response and the resilience of the business.

Executive time is the scarcest resource in a growing business. Every hour spent on a routine discount is an hour not spent on pricing strategy, major customers or planning. Response times suffer because every escalated issue waits for one person's availability. The Impact note on work stalling when a decision-maker is unavailable describes how that waiting adds up.

There is a development cost: managers who never decide never build the judgement to decide well, which keeps the business dependent on the executive. And there is a resilience cost. If the executive is ill, travelling or leaves, the business loses not only its leader but also the only place routine problems can be resolved.

Common misidentification

The common response is to hire a senior operations manager to absorb the escalations. Without written decision rights, the new person often becomes a second bottleneck, or problems continue to bypass them. Another response is for the executive to declare that they will no longer handle small issues, without saying who will. The issues then wait, or find their way back.

Possibility

In a better state, the business has a short, written set of decision rights for its common operational decisions: who decides, up to what limit, and who is next if the limit is exceeded. Approvals for those decisions are requested and recorded through one channel, so the executive can see patterns without handling each case. Approval tools can route requests to defined approvers in sequence; Microsoft's guide to approval flows describes options such as first-to-respond and sequential approval. Customers are introduced to the managers who now own their issues. The executive reviews exceptions in aggregate and steps in only when a decision is genuinely strategic.

Intervention

Start by logging what actually reaches the executive for two weeks. The log will show which decisions to delegate first.

Practical next actions

  1. Keep an escalation log for two weeks. Record each issue that reaches the executive, its type, its value and who raised it.
  2. Group the issues by type. Discounts, refunds, credit decisions, supplier problems, complaints and staffing usually account for most of them.
  3. Write decision rights for the top five types. For each, name the decider, the limit and the next level of escalation.
  4. Route requests through one channel. Use a simple form or approval tool so each decision is recorded and visible.
  5. Back the decisions that are made. Agree that decisions within limits will not be reversed casually, and discuss disagreements privately as coaching.
  6. Review the log monthly. Check whether escalations to the executive are falling, and raise limits where managers are deciding well.

Diagnostic questions

  • How many hours a week do you spend on decisions someone else could make?
  • Do your managers know, in writing, what they may approve and up to what value?
  • When an issue is beyond a manager's authority, who is the next person to decide?
  • Which customers contact the owner directly, and why?
  • What would happen to routine decisions if you were unavailable for three weeks?

Article summary

When executives become the escalation point for routine problems, the business slows to the pace of one person and its leaders lose the time needed for direction and growth. Writing down decision rights, defining escalation routes and backing managers' decisions moves routine calls to the right level and makes the business more resilient.
