When HR Becomes the Answer Desk for Information Employees Could Access

Leave balances, policy wording, payslip dates and letter requests all arrive in the HR inbox. Most of the answers already exist. Employees just cannot reach them.

Mellorca Patterns2026-11-12

Observable condition

The HR manager at a Bloemfontein logistics company with 140 employees starts the day with thirty-one unread messages. Four people ask how many leave days they have. Three ask when December pay will be processed. Two want a letter confirming employment for a bank. One asks whether the company pays for study leave. A driver telephones the front desk to ask where the leave form is kept, and the receptionist walks over to HR to find out.

Every answer exists. The leave balance sits in the payroll system. The pay calendar was agreed in October. The study-leave rule is in the policy. Nothing in the questions is difficult. The difficulty is that the only route to the answers goes through the HR manager.

This is an illustrative example. The numbers will be different elsewhere, but the shape of the day will be recognisable to most small HR teams.

Realisation

The realisation is that the HR desk has become a lookup service. People ask because asking is the only method they have, not because the information is secret. Each query is small, so the total is easy to overlook, but together they occupy the time of the person who is supposed to be hiring, resolving disputes and developing managers.

It also changes what "good service" means. Replying quickly to every question feels responsive. It also guarantees the next set of questions will arrive the next day.

Realisation prompt: for one week, tally every incoming HR query by type. Mark the ones where the answer already existed in a policy, a contract or the payroll record.

Identification

This is a pattern of mediated access. The visible symptom is an HR inbox full of routine questions. The common assumption is that employees are lazy or that HR must simply be more efficient. The structural cause is that the information is held in places only HR can open, or in documents that employees do not know exist.

The related Pattern why teams keep asking the same internal questions describes the general phenomenon. This version has a special feature: some of what employees ask for is information about themselves, which they are entitled to see.

Diagnosis

  • Personal information is locked behind HR. Leave balances, salary details and employment records are held in a system that employees cannot see. South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act gives a person the right to request access to personal information held about them, so a route for employees to see their own record is not a favour but a normal expectation.
  • Policies are hard to find. The leave policy exists but is a forty-page document in a folder few can locate. Employees ask instead of searching. The Pattern why a full document archive still leaves staff asking each other explains why having a document is not the same as being able to use it.
  • Statutory information is delivered once and forgotten. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act requires employers to give employees certain information, such as written particulars of employment and information about their pay and leave. If that information is handed over at appointment and never again made easy to check, employees return to HR to confirm it.
  • Standard letters are made by hand. A proof-of-employment letter takes ten minutes to write, with the same words, for the fortieth time this year.
  • No single "where do I find" place exists. Answers are spread across the payroll system, shared drives, notice boards and email attachments, so even HR must search.
  • Managers redirect rather than answer. Team leaders, unsure of the rule, send every question to HR instead of being given the information themselves.

Commercial impact

The first cost is HR capacity. If half the day is spent on lookups, there is little left for the work only HR can do: recruitment, handling grievances, training, and keeping the company within labour law. These tasks have a higher value but no deadline, so they are the ones that slip.

The second cost is the employee's time. Every question involves an employee waiting for an answer, often during working hours. A driver who walks to the front desk to find a form is a driver who is not driving. The Impact note employees searching for files sets out the arithmetic.

The third cost is inconsistency. When different people answer the same question from memory, answers differ. One employee is told study leave is five days, another is told ten. Inconsistent answers cause disputes that take far longer to resolve than the original question.

The fourth cost is dependence. If one HR administrator holds the knowledge, her leave becomes a risk to the business. The Insight how to move operational knowledge out of people's heads describes how to reduce it.

Common misidentification

The usual response is to hire an HR assistant to answer the questions. This increases capacity but leaves the volume unchanged. Another is to send a firm email telling employees to read the policy, which tends to produce more questions about where the policy is. A third is to buy a full HR platform before working out which questions occur, and then to discover that the policies are still unwritten.

Possibility

In a better state, an employee can see their own leave balance, next pay date and basic record without asking. Policies are short, written in plain language and kept in one place with a search function. Common letters are produced from a template with the employee's details filled in automatically, or by the employee themselves where the company approves it. Managers have a one-page guide to the rules they most often get asked about.

Some of this can be automated. Microsoft describes cloud flows as automated workflows that connect apps and services and can be started by an event such as a request arriving. A request for a letter could trigger a document built from the employee's record and sent for a single approval. The payroll package the business already uses may offer a self-service view that costs nothing extra.

HR then handles the questions that need a person: sensitive, unusual or contested ones.

Intervention

Start with the ten most frequent questions. Do not try to cover everything at once.

Practical next actions

  1. Tally the queries. Record every HR question for two weeks and rank them by frequency. The top ten usually account for most of the volume.
  2. Answer each once, in writing. For each frequent question, write a short, clear answer and the source it comes from, such as the policy clause or the pay calendar.
  3. Put the answers in one findable place. Use a single page or shared folder that employees can reach from their phones, and tell everyone where it is.
  4. Give employees their own information. Check whether your payroll or HR system offers a view of leave balances and payslips, and switch it on where privacy controls allow.
  5. Template the common letters. Create standard versions of the employment confirmation and similar documents, with fields that fill from the employee record.
  6. Brief the managers. Give team leaders a one-page summary of the leave, overtime and conduct rules so they can answer first-line questions.

Diagnostic questions

  • What proportion of HR queries have an answer that already exists somewhere?
  • Can an employee see their own leave balance without asking?
  • Where would a new driver look for the leave form?
  • How long does a standard employment letter take to produce?
  • If your HR administrator were away for two weeks, who would answer the questions?

Bottom line

When HR becomes the answer desk, the cost is not the questions themselves but the work that no longer gets done. Most routine questions have answers that already exist, and employees are often entitled to see them. Moving the top ten answers into a findable place, and giving employees direct sight of their own information, returns HR time to work that needs a person.
