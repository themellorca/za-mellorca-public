Meetings that decide nothing: The payroll cost of meeting overload

A one-hour meeting with eight people costs eight hours of salary. When it ends without a decision, that cost is repeated next week. Measuring meeting cost and designing for decisions recovers paid time.

Mellorca Impact2026-10-04

The tolerated pain

Monday's management meeting runs for ninety minutes. Each department gives an update. A difficult question about a late client project comes up, is discussed for twenty minutes and is "taken offline". Nobody writes down who will do what. On Wednesday there is a project meeting with most of the same people, where the same question comes up again. By Friday the decision still has not been made, and the client is asking.

Meetings are where many businesses try to coordinate, share information and decide. When they do the first two but not the third, they consume a large share of the most expensive time in the business, the time of managers and senior staff, without moving anything forward.

Why businesses tolerate it

  • Meetings feel like work. Being in a meeting looks productive, and an empty calendar can look like idleness.
  • Nobody sees the bill. A meeting invitation shows a time and a room, not a cost.
  • Meetings substitute for systems. When status is not visible anywhere else, a meeting is the only way to find out what is happening.
  • Inviting is safer than excluding. People are added "to be in the loop", which makes meetings larger and slower.
  • Recurring meetings never end. A weekly meeting set up for a project continues long after its purpose has passed.

What the research says

The trend is long-standing. A Harvard Business Review article on meeting overload reports that executives spend about 23 hours a week in meetings, up from fewer than 10 in the 1960s, and quotes executives who describe feeling overwhelmed by them.

The problem is not only volume but outcome. Microsoft's 2023 Work Trend Index, based on a survey of 31,000 workers in 31 countries, reports that people named inefficient meetings as their number one productivity disruptor, and that 55% said next steps at the end of a meeting are unclear. A meeting with unclear next steps is, in practice, a meeting that did not decide.

Reducing meetings can help. Research summarised in MIT Sloan Management Review on meeting-free days, covering 76 companies, found that introducing one no-meeting day a week improved autonomy, communication, engagement and satisfaction, with reduced micromanagement and stress and higher productivity. Those were large multinational companies, so the effect in a smaller South African business may differ, but the direction is consistent.

Operational symptoms

  • managers with fewer than two uninterrupted hours a day for their own work;
  • the same issue appearing on several meeting agendas over weeks;
  • meetings that end without a written list of decisions and owners;
  • status updates read aloud that could have been read in a shared document;
  • people attending in case something relevant to them comes up;
  • real work, such as reports, quotes or reviews, done after hours because the day was spent in meetings.

Meetings also fragment the day. The cost of constant switching between messages and tasks is covered in employees interrupted by messages and notifications; a calendar broken into thirty-minute gaps between meetings has a similar effect on focused work.

How the pain becomes money

  • Labour cost: every attendee's time is paid for, and the most frequent attendees are usually the most expensive people in the business.
  • Decision delay: issues that are discussed but not decided keep costing money while they wait, such as a client problem, a pricing question or a hire.
  • Opportunity cost: managers in meetings are not selling, coaching, improving processes or serving key clients.
  • Overtime and burnout: work pushed into evenings raises the risk of losing capable people.

An illustrative cost model

This example is illustrative. Every figure is an assumption for a hypothetical business. Picture a Johannesburg professional services firm with 50 staff and a management layer of ten.

AssumptionValue
Managers and senior staff10
Meeting hours per person per week12
Share of meeting time judged not to produce a decision or necessary information35%
Average loaded hourly costR650
Working weeks per year46
  • Total meeting cost: 10 × 12 × R650 × 46 = R3,588,000 a year
  • Unproductive share: 35% × R3,588,000 = R1,255,800 a year

That is before counting delayed decisions or the meetings attended by other staff. Even recovering a third of the unproductive share would return more than R400,000 of senior time a year. The 35% is an assumption; to test it, ask attendees at the end of each meeting for two weeks whether a decision was made and recorded.

Secondary consequences

Meeting overload also shapes culture. When decisions are only made in meetings, people learn to wait for the next one instead of acting, and urgent issues sit idle between Mondays. Junior staff see that being invited signals importance, so meetings grow. Remote and branch staff, who join by video and often cannot follow side conversations, receive a weaker version of the information. Over time, the business becomes slower to respond, even though everyone is busy.

When it becomes commercially urgent

  • managers are consistently working evenings to complete work their roles require;
  • important decisions repeatedly take weeks;
  • clients or suppliers are kept waiting while issues circulate through meetings;
  • the business is about to hire more managers to "free up capacity".

What better looks like

  1. Every meeting has a stated purpose. Decide, solve or plan. Information sharing happens in writing.
  2. Decisions and owners are recorded. Each meeting ends with a short list: what was decided, who does what, by when.
  3. Status is visible without a meeting. Work progress lives in a shared record, so meetings deal only with exceptions and decisions. See why chasing people for updates is a systems failure.
  4. Attendance is limited to decision-makers and contributors. Others read the notes.
  5. Recurring meetings are reviewed. Each one is re-justified, shortened or cancelled every quarter.
  6. Focus time is protected. Blocks of meeting-free time, or a meeting-free day, become normal.

Many long meetings exist because nobody is clear who can decide. If a question keeps returning, the gap may be decision rights rather than meeting technique; the Patterns piece when staff need to ask who approves what explores that.

Practical next actions

  1. Export one month of calendar data for your management team and total the hours spent in meetings.
  2. Multiply by loaded hourly cost to get a monthly meeting bill. Share it.
  3. List every recurring meeting, its purpose and its attendees; cancel or shorten at least one in three.
  4. Introduce a standard ending: decisions, owners and dates, written and circulated within the hour.
  5. Move status updates into a shared board or document reviewed before the meeting.
  6. Trial a half-day or full day without internal meetings each week and measure what changes.

The method in how to calculate the real cost of a repetitive task applies directly to a recurring meeting.

Bottom line

A meeting is one of the most expensive activities in a business, because it consumes several people's paid time at once. When meetings share information that could be read and end without decisions, the business pays a large, invisible payroll bill and still waits for answers. Making the cost visible, moving status out of meetings and insisting that meetings end in recorded decisions turns that time back into progress.
