Catching Up Before Starting: The Cost of Poor Handovers and Scattered Updates

When updates are scattered across inboxes, chat groups and conversations, staff spend the start of each day, and the first days after leave, piecing together what happened. Structured handovers and one record of status cut that time.

Mellorca Impact2026-10-05

The tolerated pain

Monday morning, or the first day back from leave. Before any real work starts, there is catching up to do. Two hundred emails, several busy chat groups, a few voice notes, and a sense that something important happened on Thursday that nobody wrote down. A customer was promised something. A supplier changed a delivery date. A job was put on hold. The only way to find out is to read everything and ask around.

The same happens on a smaller scale every day in shift-based and multi-site businesses. The morning team arrives and spends the first half-hour working out what the evening team did. A salesperson returns from a client visit and scrolls through messages to see what changed while they were out. A manager covering for a colleague phones them on leave because there is no handover note.

This is not the same as information being hard to find in general. The problem here is time-bound: work moved on while someone was away, and the business has no reliable way of telling them what changed.

Why businesses tolerate it

  • Catching up feels normal. Everyone does it, so it is seen as part of the job rather than a cost.
  • Communication is spread across channels. Email, several chat groups, phone calls and corridor conversations each hold part of the story.
  • Handovers are informal. A quick chat at the end of a shift, or a "call me if anything comes up" before leave, replaces a written note.
  • Nobody owns the status. Each person knows their part, but there is no single place showing where work stands.
  • The cost is personal. The person catching up bears the time, so it never appears as a business expense.

What the evidence shows

Absence is a predictable, regular part of working life. The Department of Employment and Labour's basic guide to annual leave sets the minimum entitlement at 21 consecutive days a year, or one day for every 17 days worked. Every employee will be away for weeks each year, plus shorter absences for illness, training and site visits. A business that has no reliable handover process will repeat the catch-up cost many times a year.

Communication overload makes catching up harder. Microsoft's 2023 Work Trend Index, based on a survey of 31,000 people in 31 countries and on Microsoft 365 usage data, reports that the average employee spends 57% of their time communicating in meetings, email and chat, and that 62% of respondents struggle with too much time spent searching for information. The more activity there is, the longer it takes to reconstruct what happened.

Healthcare offers the clearest evidence that structured handovers work. A single-centre study published in the Journal of General Internal Medicine introduced a standardised handoff for hospital residents covering six elements: a summary of the situation, recent events, baseline status, severity, an action list and a contingency plan. After the change, residents reported higher satisfaction with written and verbal handoffs and more confidence in patient care, and the study recorded a significant reduction in preventable adverse events in three of those areas. A business handover is lower stakes, but the same structure, situation, what changed, what is urgent and what to do if things go wrong, transfers directly.

Operational symptoms

  • the first hour of each day, or the first day back from leave, spent reading messages;
  • people on leave being phoned or messaged to explain the status of their work;
  • customers told different things by different people because one of them missed an update;
  • work done twice because the second person did not know the first had already done it;
  • decisions made in a chat group that half the team never saw;
  • shift changes where problems are discovered late because they were not passed on.

The way decisions disappear into message history is described in when business knowledge lives in inbox history.

How the pain becomes money

  • Catch-up labour: paid time spent reconstructing what happened instead of doing new work.
  • Error and rework cost: people act on out-of-date information and have to correct it.
  • Customer cost: missed promises and inconsistent answers damage trust.
  • Leave disruption: interrupted leave reduces rest and morale, with knock-on effects on retention.
  • Delay cost: urgent items wait until someone notices them.

The daily figure dominates, which is typical: small daily losses add up faster than occasional large ones. The model does not include errors from missed updates. Test the assumptions by asking a sample of staff to time their catch-up for one week.

When it becomes commercially urgent

  • customer complaints are traced to updates that did not reach the right person;
  • the business adds shifts, depots or remote staff;
  • staff complain that they cannot switch off on leave, a pressure captured in why holidays still need a laptop in the suitcase;
  • a key person is going on extended leave, such as parental or medical leave;
  • a costly mistake has already happened because a handover was missed.

What better looks like

The fix combines a habit and a record.

A structured handover

Use the same short format every time: what happened, what changed, what is outstanding and urgent, who to contact and what to do if something goes wrong. Keep it brief enough that it actually gets written. For leave, prepare it a day before going, not an hour before.

One place for status

Work status should live in a shared record, such as a job board, a task list or the relevant business system, not in people's inboxes. When someone returns, they read the record, not two hundred messages. Decisions made in chat should be copied to that record.

Fewer channels

Agree which channel is used for what: one for urgent operational updates, one for discussion, email for external communication. Fewer channels mean fewer places to check.

The wider principle, that handoffs fail because of how systems are designed rather than because people are careless, is set out in broken handoffs are systems problems.

Practical first steps

  1. Time the catch-up. Ask a sample of staff to record how long they spend catching up each morning and after leave.
  2. Agree a handover template. Five headings on one page, used for shift changes and for leave.
  3. Pick one status record per team. Choose the board, list or system where current status lives, and stop tracking it in chat.
  4. Map your channels. Write down which channel is for what, and close or archive groups that duplicate others.
  5. Review after a month. Measure catch-up time again and ask whether anyone was phoned on leave.

The bottom line

Every hour spent piecing together what happened while someone was away is an hour of paid time that produces nothing new. Because absence is certain and communication keeps growing, the cost repeats every day. A consistent handover and one shared record of status turn catching up from an investigation into a short read.
