The Outage Nobody Rehearsed: The Cost of Improvising When Systems Go Down

Many businesses have never walked through how they would trade if email, the accounting system or the internet went down. The first rehearsal ends up being the real outage.

Mellorca Impact2026-11-13

The first hour of an unplanned outage

At 08:15 on a Monday, the staff of a 30-person logistics business in Johannesburg cannot open the job management system. The phones ring with customers asking for collection times. The dispatcher wants to know whether to print the day's routes from yesterday's email. The finance manager wonders whether anyone has the IT provider's after-hours number. The managing director asks who decides whether to send drivers out without confirmed bookings. Everybody is busy, and almost nothing useful is happening.

The technical fault is fixed by lunchtime. The confusion lasts longer: duplicate bookings, a driver sent to the wrong address, and two days of catching up. Most of that cost did not come from the outage. It came from the fact that nobody had ever practised what to do during one.

A plan is not the same as a practised plan

Some businesses have no continuity plan at all, a problem covered in having no written recovery plan. Many others have a document that was written once, filed and never tested. The difference matters. A document says what should happen. A rehearsal shows what actually happens: who does not know their role, which phone numbers are out of date, which manual workaround nobody remembers how to run, and which decision has no owner.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology describes this in its guide to test, training and exercise programmes for IT plans. The guide explains that such events train personnel, exercise IT contingency plans and test IT systems, so an organisation can better anticipate, respond to and recover from disruptions. The principle applies just as well to a business of 30 people as to a government agency.

Where the cost of improvising comes from

Longer interruptions

Without a rehearsed sequence, the first part of every outage is spent working out who should be doing what. Contact details are searched for, decisions are escalated to whoever is senior and available, and work-arounds are invented on the spot. Each of those delays adds directly to the time the business cannot trade normally.

Paid staff waiting

During an unrehearsed outage, many staff simply wait. If 20 people are unable to do productive work for three hours, and their average loaded cost is R250 an hour, that is R15,000 of wages for one morning, before any lost sales. The related question of putting a figure on downtime is covered in not knowing the hourly cost of downtime.

Mistakes made under pressure

Improvised work-arounds create errors: orders taken on paper and later entered twice, payments released without the usual checks, customers given promises that cannot be kept. Some of these mistakes cost more than the outage itself, especially when they involve money leaving the business.

Customer and reputation damage

Customers forgive a disruption more easily than a confused response. A business that can say clearly what has happened, what it is doing and when the customer will hear back keeps far more goodwill than one that answers each caller differently.

A slower recovery afterwards

When an outage ends, the backlog has to be cleared and the work done manually has to be entered into the systems. Without an agreed process, this catch-up is often slower and more error-prone than the outage. The article on not knowing your recovery time examines the restoration side.

Why rehearsals rarely happen

Exercises are easy to postpone. They take people away from work that pays, they feel theatrical, and nobody wants to find out that their part of the plan does not work. Smaller businesses also assume rehearsals are for large organisations with dedicated continuity staff. In practice, a useful exercise for a small business is a conversation around a table for 90 minutes, not a simulated disaster.

South African businesses have had plenty of real practice with power cuts and connectivity problems, and many have become good at improvising. That experience is valuable, but it is not the same as a plan. The people who know how to handle the generator or the backup line may be on leave when the next outage happens.

What a simple tabletop exercise looks like

A tabletop exercise is a guided discussion. A facilitator describes a scenario, such as "the accounting system is unavailable from 9am and the provider cannot say when it will be back", and the team talks through what they would do, step by step. Ready-made material exists. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency publishes tabletop exercise packages covering scenarios such as ransomware, with template objectives, scenarios, discussion questions and after-action report templates. They are written for a US audience, but the structure is easy to adapt.

The useful output is not a score. It is a short list of gaps: a missing phone number, a decision with no owner, a manual process that only one person knows, a supplier contract that does not say how quickly they must respond.

The commercial trigger

The best time to run an exercise is shortly after a real disruption, while the lessons are fresh, or just before a predictable risk period such as the busiest trading month. Another good trigger is any major system change: a move to a new accounting or job management platform changes the outage plan, whether anyone updates it or not.

Practical next actions

  1. Choose the three systems whose loss would stop trading. For most businesses these are email and phones, the main operating system (job, order or practice management) and the accounting or payment system.
  2. Write one page per system. Record who decides, who contacts the supplier, how customers are told, and what manual process replaces the system.
  3. Run a 90-minute tabletop exercise. Pick one scenario, invite the people who would actually respond, and walk through the first four hours.
  4. List every gap the exercise exposes. Assign each gap an owner and a date, and fix the quick ones within a week.
  5. Keep contact details reachable offline. Print the key supplier, staff and customer contact list and keep a copy away from the office systems.
  6. Repeat twice a year with a different scenario. Rotate the facilitator so the knowledge does not sit with one person.

An outage costs what it costs. Confusion during the outage is an extra cost that a business chooses to carry, and a single morning around a table is usually enough to remove much of it.
