The free tool that became expensive: Choosing software on price alone

Free and low-cost plans are a fair start, but their limits show up in staff time, rework and missing records. Learn how to tell when the cheap choice has stopped being cheap.

Mellorca Impact2026-10-10

The free plan was a sensible start. A two-person business needed a place to chat, share files and track jobs, and paying nothing for it was exactly right. Five years and thirty staff later, the same free plan is still in use, held together by workarounds: a spreadsheet to export what the tool will not report, a folder of screenshots because older messages vanish, a colleague who copies data across by hand every Friday. Nobody is paying a subscription, and everybody is paying for the limitations.

The pain: price used as the main selection test

Free and low-cost tools are a genuine gift to small businesses. The trouble starts when a tool chosen for its price is kept after the business has outgrown it, or when price becomes the only criterion for the next choice. The software is not the problem. The unexamined limits are.

Mellorca has covered the opposite mistake, buying premium plans for basic needs. This article looks at the other side: staying on a cheap or free plan after the limits have started to cost more than an upgrade would.

What the limits look like

Limits on free plans are not secrets. They are documented, and they are designed to nudge growing users to pay. Looking at published examples shows what kinds of restrictions to expect.

  • History and retention. Slack's help centre states that free workspaces are limited to the most recent 90 days of message and file history, and that data more than a year old is deleted. For a business that uses chat as a working record, that is a real operational constraint.
  • Integrations. The same page says free workspaces can have 10 third-party or custom app installations, and that adding a new one means removing an existing one. A business that wants its chat connected to accounting, forms and project tools hits the ceiling quickly.
  • Administration. Google's comparison of Essentials editions describes Essentials Starter as free for teams of up to 100 users, but lists fewer admin controls than the paid editions, including no creating or deleting user accounts, no custom admin roles and no shared drives.
  • Storage and meetings. The same comparison lists 15 GB of storage per user on Starter against 100 GB on the first paid edition, and no recording to Drive, dial-in or breakout rooms in Meet.

None of these is a defect. They are the price of free. The cost arises when the business needs the missing capability and works around it instead of paying for it.

Why the choice goes unchallenged

  • "It is free" ends the conversation. There is no invoice to question, so there is no moment when someone asks whether it still fits.
  • The cost of workarounds is invisible. Staff time spent exporting, re-entering and searching is spread across many people and never appears as a line item.
  • Switching feels like a project. Moving to a paid tier of the same product is often simple, but moving to another product is not. People assume the second and avoid the first.
  • The limits are discovered late. Nobody reads the plan comparison until the day something goes missing.

How the hidden cost builds up

  1. Workaround labour. Time spent copying, exporting, screenshotting and chasing information the tool does not hold or share.
  2. Error and rework. Manual bridges between tools introduce mistakes, and mistakes cost time to find and fix.
  3. Lost records. If history is deleted after a set period, a dispute with a customer or supplier may have no evidence to settle it.
  4. Capacity ceiling. A tool that cannot grow with the business starts to hold back volume, new staff or new services.
  5. Support and control gaps. Without admin controls, offboarding, access changes and audit trails depend on individuals remembering to do them.
  6. Switching cost deferred, not avoided. The longer data accumulates in a limited tool, the more expensive it is to move. See systems that do not talk to each other for how this effect builds.

An illustrative cost comparison

Take a hypothetical 20-person South African design and print business using a free project and chat tool. The figures are assumptions for the example only.

Rework from copy errors1 job a month, 3 hours of fixing at R300 an hourR10 800

On those assumptions, the free tool costs about R45 700 a year more than the paid one, before counting the risk of lost records. Change the numbers to match your business and the result may reverse: some free tools are perfectly adequate and upgrading would be waste. The calculation is the point, not the answer.

Data, privacy and your legal duties

Free and low-cost tools often hold customer and employee information. South Africa's Information Regulator states that any organisation that processes personal information is a responsible party with duties that include applying reasonable security safeguards under section 19 of the Protection of Personal Information Act and reporting security compromises. A tool with weak admin controls, no access roles or no audit trail makes those duties harder to meet. That does not mean free tools are unlawful, only that the business remains responsible for what happens to the data inside them.

Exports matter too. Google documents that a data export does not delete the data from its servers, that archives expire after about a week, and that exports cannot be filtered by date range. Knowing what an export can and cannot do before you need it is part of choosing a tool.

The commercial trigger point

Review a free or cheap tool when:

  • Staff regularly say "it can't do that" and then describe a workaround.
  • You catch yourself exporting data to a spreadsheet to answer a routine question.
  • You reach an integration, user or storage limit.
  • Someone leaves and you cannot be sure what they could still access.
  • A customer or auditor asks for records that the tool no longer holds.
  • The business is about to grow, hire or add a service line.

What better looks like

A well-managed business does not avoid free tools. It chooses them knowingly. For each one it records what the limits are, what the business would do on the day those limits are reached, and what the next tier or alternative would cost. Price becomes one of several criteria, alongside fit, data control, support and exit options. The practical guidance in our article on questions to ask before you buy another tool applies equally to tools that cost nothing.

Practical steps

  1. List every free or low-cost tool in use, including those individual staff adopted themselves. Our piece on software bought without approval explains why the list is often longer than expected.
  2. Read the current plan limits on the vendor's own comparison page. Note retention periods, user and integration caps, storage, and admin features.
  3. Ask staff where they work around the tool. Each workaround is a cost; put a rough time on it.
  4. Price the next tier and compare it with the workaround cost, as in the table above.
  5. Test the export. Take a full export now, check that it is readable and complete, and record how long it takes.
  6. Check who has access and whether you can remove people and review activity.
  7. Decide: stay and accept the limits on purpose, upgrade the plan, or plan a move. Put a review date in the diary.

The bottom line

Free software is not free if the limits are paid for in staff hours, rework and missing records. It is also not a trap, since many free plans serve small businesses well for years. The expensive mistake is not choosing a cheap tool; it is failing to notice when the business has outgrown it. A short, honest comparison of workaround cost against upgrade cost turns that question into a number, and lets the business pay for capability when it is cheaper than going without.
