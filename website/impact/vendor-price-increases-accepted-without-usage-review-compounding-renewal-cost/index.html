Accepting Every Price Rise: How Unreviewed Renewals Compound Year After Year

A modest increase at each renewal looks harmless, yet it builds on itself. Learn what to check before paying and how to keep leverage with software suppliers.

Mellorca Impact2026-10-10

The renewal email arrives with a polite tone and a slightly higher number. Eight per cent, perhaps, or a new per-user rate "reflecting added value". The person who receives it is busy, the system is in daily use, and switching would be a project nobody has time for. The invoice is paid. Twelve months later it happens again. By the third year the business is paying noticeably more for essentially the same tool, and nobody ever made a decision to do so.

Price rises are normal; unreviewed ones are the problem

Vendors raise prices for many reasons, some of them fair. Costs change, features are added, and subscriptions that were underpriced at launch are corrected. The issue is not that prices rise. The issue is that most small businesses have no routine for responding when they do. Mellorca has written about automatic renewals without a value review and the way renewal dates can force better decisions. This article is about the compounding effect of accepting increases year after year without checking usage or alternatives.

Recent examples show how these changes arrive

Major vendors announce increases openly, and the pattern is familiar. In August 2021, TechCentral reported that Microsoft would raise prices for Microsoft 365 commercial customers by as much as 20 per cent, describing it as the first increase since the service launched a decade earlier and quoting a Microsoft executive saying the new pricing reflected increased value delivered. In December 2025, RCP Magazine reported that Microsoft would raise prices on many commercial Microsoft 365 and Office 365 subscriptions from July 2026, with the company pointing to new AI, security and management features. The coverage notes that the changes apply globally with local market adjustments, and that it does not explain how existing contracts or billing cycles will be handled.

That last point is the practical one. Announcements describe the headline. What actually happens to your own plan, billing period and currency exposure depends on your agreement and your reseller. Few owners read that far.

Why businesses tolerate it

  • Switching feels riskier than paying. The system holds business data and staff know it. A rise of a few hundred rand a month seems cheaper than a migration.
  • The notice is easy to miss. Price notices go to a billing contact who may have left, or to an inbox nobody monitors.
  • Auto-renewal removes the decision. If the subscription renews itself, inaction is a purchase.
  • No usage data is to hand. Without it, the business cannot say whether it needs all the seats it pays for.
  • Vendors rarely volunteer cheaper options. Lower tiers and annual-versus-monthly differences are not highlighted.

What it looks like in practice

  • Seat counts that only ever go up, including accounts for people who left.
  • Invoices that grow faster than headcount, but nobody has compared the two.
  • A tier chosen at the start that nobody has revisited, although the business now uses a fraction of it.
  • Prices quoted in foreign currency, so the rand cost moves with the exchange rate on top of any list-price change.
  • Multi-year terms or minimum commitments that were agreed once and never diarised.

How a small rise becomes a large cost

The financial effect works in three ways.

  1. Compounding. Each rise applies to a base that already includes the last one. An annual 10 per cent increase doubles the price in about seven years.
  2. Seat creep. If prices rise and seat counts quietly grow too, the bill rises on two axes.
  3. Lost leverage. A vendor that knows you never push back has no reason to offer a discount, extra months or a better tier.

An illustrative example

The numbers below are assumptions for a hypothetical 40-person South African firm and its core business platform. They illustrate the arithmetic and are not benchmarks.

Over four years the annual bill rises by about 53 per cent, although headcount has grown by 15 per cent and the tool is unchanged. Suppose a review in year 4 finds that 9 of the 46 seats are never used. At R332 a month, that is roughly R35 800 a year of avoidable spend. Suppose a lower tier would meet the needs of half the remaining users at 30 per cent less. That is a further saving of about R22 000 a year. Neither figure is a prediction; both come from the stated assumptions. The point is that a short review can recover a meaningful amount, and that skipping it for several years makes the recoverable amount larger every time.

Secondary consequences

  • Cash-flow surprises. Annual prepayments land in one month and are harder to absorb when they are larger than budgeted.
  • Dependency deepens. The longer a system is accepted without question, the more data and process depend on it, which raises switching costs. See our piece on systems that do not talk to each other for how that lock-in builds.
  • Budget distortion. Software becomes a growing fixed cost that crowds out other investment.

The commercial trigger point

You should act when any of these occur: a price-increase notice arrives; the invoice has risen more than headcount or revenue over two years; a renewal date is less than 90 days away; or the business is about to sign a multi-year term. The renewal window is the moment of greatest leverage, because the vendor still has something to lose.

What better looks like

A business that handles increases well has no special expertise, only a routine. It knows what each platform costs, when it renews and who is responsible for the decision. It checks usage before it pays. It treats a price-increase notice as a prompt to compare, not an administrative chore. And it is willing to move, or credibly say it might, when the numbers do not hold up.

Practical steps

  1. Build a renewal calendar. List every platform, its renewal date, notice period, term and contact. Set reminders 120 and 60 days ahead. Include the cancellation notice window, which is often shorter than people assume.
  2. Name an owner for each platform. This should be someone who can say yes or no, not only whoever pays the invoice.
  3. Pull usage before negotiating. Microsoft documents usage reports in its 365 admin centre that show who uses a service heavily and who barely uses it. Compare against seats held, and remove unused ones before the renewal, not after.
  4. Check the plan again. Look at what the lower tiers include. Our article on premium plans bought for basic needs explains how to tell.
  5. Get one alternative quote. Even if you do not intend to switch, a real comparison gives you something to put on the table.
  6. Ask for what you want. Annual billing in exchange for a lower rate, a price cap on the next renewal, or a phased seat reduction. Ask in writing and keep the answer.
  7. Check currency terms. If the price is in dollars, record the exchange rate used for budgeting and whether the vendor or reseller can adjust the rand price mid-term.
  8. Record the outcome so that next year's review starts from facts.

When to stay put

Sometimes the right answer to a price rise is to accept it. If the tool is central to operations, well used and well supported, and the alternatives are weaker or the migration risk is high, a modest increase may be the cheapest outcome. The aim is not to switch for the sake of it. It is to ensure that when you stay, you are staying on purpose. Our guide to deciding which software to keep offers a structured way to make that call.

The bottom line

Unreviewed price increases are expensive because they repeat. Each acceptance sets a higher base for the next one, quietly weakens the business's bargaining position and hides the seats and features no one needs. A renewal calendar, a named owner and a short check of usage and alternatives turns a recurring annoyance into a manageable decision. The saving may be modest in any single year. Over a few years, it is often the difference between a software budget the business controls and one that simply grows.
