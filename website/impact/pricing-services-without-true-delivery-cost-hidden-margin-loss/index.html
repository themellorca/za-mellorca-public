Busy Teams, Thin Margins: Pricing Services Without Knowing What They Cost to Deliver

Service firms often set fees from competitor rates or habit, not from the hours, travel, rework and admin each job really consumes. Revenue looks healthy while some work quietly loses money.

Mellorca Impact2026-11-16

Full diaries, flat profit

An engineering consultancy in Cape Town has had its best year for revenue. The team is fully booked, overtime is common and clients keep coming back. Yet the owner is puzzled: profit has barely changed. When the finance manager finally compares a sample of completed projects with the hours actually logged, the picture is uncomfortable. Small fixed-fee jobs for a handful of long-standing clients routinely take twice the hours that were priced. Site visits are not charged. Revisions requested after sign-off are absorbed. Senior engineers spend hours on work that was priced at junior rates. The business has been busy, but some of that busyness has been subsidising clients.

The example is illustrative, but the problem is widespread among firms that sell time, expertise or field work. Fees are set from what competitors charge, what clients accepted last year, or a rate card written years ago. The actual cost of delivering each type of work is rarely measured, so the margin hidden inside revenue goes unexamined.

Why service delivery cost is hard to see

A manufacturer can count materials. A service business's main cost is people's time, and time leaks in ways that do not show up on invoices:

  • Non-billable effort. Travel, internal meetings, quoting, preparing reports and chasing information all consume paid hours that may not be charged to any client.
  • Scope creep. Small additional requests are handled to keep the client happy and never recorded as extra work.
  • Rework. Corrections after errors or misunderstandings take time that the original fee did not cover.
  • Seniority mismatch. Work priced for a junior person is done by a senior one because they are available or the client asks for them.
  • Full employment cost. A person's cost is more than salary. It includes leave, benefits, equipment, software, office space and statutory employer contributions. SARS's page on pay-as-you-earn sets out that employers declare and pay PAYE, UIF and SDL monthly, and the employer contributions among these are part of the true cost of every hour a person works.

Where the margin goes

Unprofitable clients and job types

Averages hide the problem. Overall margin may look acceptable while particular clients, service lines or contract types lose money on every job. Without job-level cost data, the business cannot tell which work to grow and which to reprice or decline.

Underpriced fixed fees

Fixed-fee pricing is attractive to clients and simple to sell, but it transfers the risk of overruns to the service provider. If the fee was estimated without reference to actual past effort, overruns are absorbed every time.

Capacity consumed by low-margin work

When the team is full of underpriced work, it cannot take on better-paying work. The cost is not only the loss on each job, but also the profitable work turned away or delayed because people are busy.

Pressure on people

Underpriced work creates a cycle of overtime, fatigue and errors. Staff turnover rises, and replacing experienced people is expensive.

A transparent way to estimate the gap

The following figures are hypothetical. Suppose an engineer costs the business R720,000 a year once salary, benefits, employer contributions, equipment and a share of overheads are included. If the engineer has about 1,600 available working hours after leave and public holidays, but only 1,100 of those are billable, the true cost per billable hour is roughly R655, not the R450 you get by spreading the cost across all available hours. If the firm's fee for that engineer's time is R700 an hour, the margin is much thinner than it appears, and any job that overruns by more than a few percent loses money.

The point is the method, not the numbers. Each business should calculate its own fully loaded cost per billable hour by role, then compare it with what each type of job actually earns per hour spent.

Why it persists

Service firms tolerate the problem for understandable reasons. Time recording feels like administration and is often incomplete. Owners worry that raising prices will lose clients. Market rates feel fixed. And the profit-and-loss statement shows the business as a whole, not each job. The article on growth adding administration faster than output describes a related margin problem at the level of the whole business.

Costing without a heavy system

Detailed activity-based costing has a reputation for being complex. Robert Kaplan and Steven Anderson's Harvard Business Review article on time-driven activity-based costing proposed a simpler approach that relies on informed managerial estimates rather than on lengthy employee surveys. For a small service business, the practical version is straightforward: know what an hour of each role really costs, record time against jobs consistently, and compare actual hours with priced hours after each job closes. The guide on calculating the real cost of a repetitive task uses the same logic for internal work.

Pricing calculations are often held in spreadsheets, which brings its own risk. The pattern of one wrong formula changing a price is worth checking before relying on any rate model.

Practical next actions

  1. Calculate a fully loaded hourly cost for each role. Include salary, benefits, employer contributions, equipment, software and a fair share of overheads, divided by realistic billable hours, not total hours.
  2. Review the last twenty completed jobs. Compare hours priced with hours actually spent, including travel, revisions and admin, and calculate the real margin on each.
  3. Group results by client and job type. Identify which clients and services consistently overrun, and which are genuinely profitable.
  4. Make time recording simple and routine. Agree a small set of job codes and a daily habit, rather than detailed weekly reconstructions.
  5. Price change requests explicitly. Record extra requests as variations, even when you decide not to charge for them, so the cost becomes visible.
  6. Reprice or redesign loss-making work. Raise fees, change the scope, move work to the right level of staff, or decline work that cannot be made profitable.

Revenue tells a service business how busy it is. Only delivery cost tells it whether that work is worth doing. A firm that measures the hours behind each job can price with confidence instead of discovering, a year later, that growth was paid for out of margin.
