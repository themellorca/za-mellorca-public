When Only One Person Can Update the Website: The Revenue Cost of Slow Publishing

Prices, services, staff and specials change, but the website waits in one developer's queue. Visitors meet stale information, enquiries drop, and marketing loses the moment it was planned for.

Mellorca Impact2026-11-16

A website that runs months behind the business

A private healthcare practice group in Bloemfontein opens a new rooms location, adds two specialists and changes its consultation hours. The marketing coordinator has the new copy ready the same week. The website, however, can only be changed by an external developer who built it five years ago, and changes go into his queue alongside work for other clients. The new location appears six weeks later. In the meantime, patients phone the old number, the new specialists' profiles are missing, and a radio campaign sends listeners to a page that still shows the old hours. Front-desk staff spend the month explaining that the website is wrong.

The example is illustrative, but the situation is common. Many small and mid-sized businesses have a website that only one technical person can safely change. That person may be an employee, a freelancer or an agency. Either way, every update, however small, becomes a request in someone else's queue.

How the bottleneck forms

Single-person dependency usually comes from how the site was built rather than from anyone's intent:

  • Content is hard-coded into page templates, so changing a sentence means editing code.
  • The content management system was never set up for editors, or business staff were never given accounts and training.
  • Login details sit with one person, sometimes an agency that has since moved on.
  • Nobody owns the content, so marketing, sales and operations each assume someone else will flag changes.
  • Fear of breaking the site makes staff reluctant to touch it, even where they technically could.

Most mainstream platforms are designed to avoid this. WordPress, for example, documents a set of user roles and capabilities in which an Editor can publish and manage posts, including other people's, and an Author can publish and manage their own, without administrator access to themes, plugins or settings. The capability exists; the business has simply not organised itself to use it.

Where the revenue goes

Lost enquiries from stale information

Visitors judge a business by its website before they ever call. Outdated prices, discontinued services, old contact details and missing staff make the business look careless or closed. Some visitors leave without enquiring, and the business never sees the lost demand.

Campaigns that miss their window

Promotions, seasonal offers and event pages have a short useful life. When publishing takes weeks, campaigns launch late or point to pages that do not yet exist. Marketing spend that drives traffic to the wrong page is wasted.

Staff time spent correcting the website

When the website is wrong, the phone and email channels absorb the difference. Staff explain correct prices, hours and locations to customers who read something else online. This is invisible labour that grows with every outdated page.

Consumer and trust risk

Prices and offers on a website are part of how the business presents itself to consumers. The Consumer Protection Act aims to promote a fair, accessible and sustainable marketplace and to improve the standard of consumer information. A website showing prices or terms the business no longer honours creates disputes and complaints that front-line staff must manage, and it erodes trust.

Weaker search visibility

Search engines aim to show helpful, reliable information. Google's guidance on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content emphasises accuracy and trust, and it warns against changing page dates to make content seem fresh when nothing substantial has changed. A site that cannot be kept accurate is at a disadvantage against competitors who maintain theirs.

A transparent way to estimate the cost

The following figures are hypothetical. If a website brings in 60 enquiries a month and converts a third of them into work worth R8,000 on average, it generates about R160,000 a month. If outdated or missing content reduces enquiries by even 10 percent for a quarter of the year, the business loses around 18 enquiries, or roughly R48,000 in revenue, before counting campaign spend wasted on late pages and staff time spent correcting errors. Replace the assumptions with your own traffic, conversion and job values.

Why businesses tolerate it

Website changes feel minor compared with operational work, and each delay seems small. The technical person is often helpful and trusted, and asking for changes to the arrangement can feel awkward. Owners may also not realise how much of the site's content could be safely edited by non-technical staff. The insight on what happens when the only person who understands a system leaves describes the continuity risk that sits behind the publishing delay.

What a healthier arrangement looks like

In a healthy setup, business staff can update routine content such as prices, hours, staff profiles, service descriptions, news and campaign pages themselves, within templates that protect layout and branding. Technical people focus on structure, performance, security and new features. A named content owner checks key pages on a schedule. The business holds its own administrator credentials and domain records, even when an agency manages the site. Website forms connect to the systems that handle enquiries; the article on website enquiries landing in an email inbox looks at that next step, and the pattern of a website promising what the back office cannot see shows what happens when the two drift apart.

Practical next actions

  1. Audit the ten most visited pages. Check prices, hours, contact details, staff and services against current reality, and list every error.
  2. Measure the publishing delay. Look at the last five content changes and record how long each took from request to live.
  3. Secure ownership of access. Make sure the business holds administrator credentials for the site, hosting and domain, not only the developer or agency.
  4. Give trained staff editor access. Set up editor or author roles for the people who own content, with templates that limit layout changes.
  5. Name a content owner. Assign one person to review key pages monthly and before every campaign.
  6. Move hard-coded content into editable sections. Ask your developer to convert frequently changed content, such as prices and hours, into fields staff can update safely.

A website is often the first place customers meet the business. When updating it depends on one person's availability, the business pays in lost enquiries, wasted campaigns and staff time. Spreading safe editing access, while keeping technical control where it belongs, keeps the site as current as the business itself.
