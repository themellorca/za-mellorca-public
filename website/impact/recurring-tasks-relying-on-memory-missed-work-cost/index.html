Recurring tasks that live in someone's memory: The cost of missed work

When monthly returns, renewals and routine checks depend on someone remembering them, the business pays in penalties, rework and follow-up. A simple recurring schedule with named owners removes most of that risk.

Mellorca Impact2026-10-04

The tolerated pain

Every business runs on a calendar of small, repeating obligations. The payroll tax submission each month. The VAT return. The quarterly fire-equipment service. The annual renewal of a domain name, a professional registration or a vehicle licence. The weekly check that the backup ran, the monthly stock count, the reminder to send statements to slow-paying customers.

In many small and mid-sized businesses, none of this is written down as a schedule. It lives in people's heads. The bookkeeper "just knows" when the returns are due. The office manager remembers that the alarm contract renews in March. The operations lead has a mental list of what must happen on the first working day of each month.

Most of the time it works, which is exactly why it is tolerated. The cost shows up only on the day memory fails: a missed deadline, a lapsed licence, a forgotten follow-up, and then the scramble, the penalty and the explanation to the customer.

Why businesses tolerate it

  • It has always worked. The person who remembers has done it for years, so writing it down feels unnecessary.
  • Each task is small. No single item looks important enough to justify a system.
  • Reminders feel personal. People keep their own calendar alerts, sticky notes or phone reminders, which nobody else can see.
  • Failure is rare and blamed on the person. When something is missed, it is treated as a one-off lapse rather than a design flaw.
  • Nobody owns the full list. Recurring tasks are spread across finance, operations, facilities and IT, so no one sees how many there are.

Where the stakes are real

Some recurring tasks carry consequences set by law. The South African Revenue Service explains on its Pay-As-You-Earn page that employers must pay the tax they deducted within seven days after the end of the month, and that a late employer reconciliation attracts an administrative penalty of 1% of the annual PAYE liability, rising by 1% for every month it remains outstanding, up to 10%. SARS's page on the obligations of a VAT vendor states that returns and payment are due on or before the 25th or the last business day of the month, depending on the payment method, and that late payments attract penalty and interest.

These are only the obvious ones. Contract notice periods, insurance renewals, equipment calibration, certificate expiries and customer service reviews follow the same pattern: a fixed rhythm, a hard date and a consequence if it slips. Supplier contracts are a good example, covered in the cost of supplier contracts renewing without review.

Operational symptoms

  • deadlines met at the last minute, with work rushed and checked poorly;
  • tasks missed when the usual person is on leave, ill or busy with a crisis;
  • "who normally does this?" asked at month-end or year-end;
  • follow-ups with customers or suppliers that happen only when someone remembers;
  • penalties, interest or reinstatement fees that appear in the accounts without explanation;
  • a new employee taking months to learn what has to happen and when.

The follow-up version of this problem is particularly expensive in cash collection, as described in when cash collection depends on someone remembering to follow up.

How the pain becomes money

  • Penalty and interest cost: statutory and contractual deadlines carry direct financial consequences when missed.
  • Rework cost: rushed work done at the last minute is more likely to contain errors that must be corrected later.
  • Recovery cost: reinstating a lapsed registration, licence or service often takes more time than the original task would have.
  • Revenue and cash-flow cost: forgotten follow-ups on quotes, renewals and overdue accounts delay or lose income.
  • Management cost: senior people spend time checking whether things were done, because there is no record that shows it.

An illustrative cost model

This example is illustrative. Every figure is an assumption for a hypothetical business, not a benchmark. Picture a Durban engineering supplier with 45 staff and about 40 recurring obligations a year, spread across finance, operations and facilities.

  • Recovery time: 6 × 10 hours × R420 = R25,200
  • Management checking: 6 hours × 12 months × R650 = R46,800
  • Illustrative total: about R120,000 a year

The model leaves out the hardest costs to price: a customer lost because a service visit was forgotten, or an insurance claim complicated because a required inspection lapsed. One such event can outweigh the whole table. Replace the assumptions with your own by reviewing the last two years of penalties, fees and late renewals.

When it becomes commercially urgent

  • the person who carries the schedule in their head is leaving, retiring or going on extended leave;
  • penalties or interest appear more than once in a year;
  • the business is adding sites, entities or product lines, multiplying the obligations;
  • an audit, insurer or major customer asks for evidence that routine checks are done;
  • a lapsed item has already caused a service failure or a compliance query.

The link to staff absence is direct. If only one person knows the schedule, the business has a single point of failure, a risk explored in when one employee knows how everything works.

What better looks like

The fix is not complicated software. It is moving recurring work from memory into a shared schedule that anyone with the right role can see. Ordinary tools already do this. Microsoft's guide to recurring tasks in Planner explains that a task can repeat daily, on weekdays, weekly, monthly or yearly, or on a custom pattern, and that when one occurrence is completed the next one appears automatically. Each occurrence can be tracked separately, so the business gets a record of what was done and when.

The tool matters less than the principle. Healthcare learned this long ago. The World Health Organization's Surgical Safety Checklist resources describe a 19-item checklist that builds deliberate pauses into surgery so the team confirms each item together rather than relying on individual memory, and report that it has been associated with reductions in complications and deaths. A business calendar of recurring obligations applies the same idea to much lower-stakes work: the routine is written down, someone owns it, and completion is visible.

Good practice usually includes:

  • one register of every recurring obligation, with its frequency, due date and consequence if missed;
  • a named owner and a named backup for each item;
  • reminders set well before the hard deadline, not on the day;
  • a short checklist for tasks with several steps;
  • a record of completion that a manager can review without asking;
  • automation for anything that can run without judgement, such as scheduled reports or reminder emails.

Designing handovers that do not depend on memory is covered in more depth in how to design a handoff that cannot depend on memory.

Practical first steps

  1. Ask each team for its recurring list. Give them one week to write down everything that happens on a daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly or annual rhythm.
  2. Add the consequence. For each item, note what happens if it is missed: a penalty, a lapsed service, an unhappy customer or nothing much.
  3. Check the accounts. Look for penalties, interest, reinstatement fees and lost early-payment discounts over the last two years.
  4. Load the high-consequence items first. Put statutory and contractual deadlines into a shared, recurring schedule with owners and backups.
  5. Review monthly. A ten-minute check of overdue items each month is cheaper than one missed return.

The bottom line

Memory is a poor system for work that repeats. It fails quietly, usually when the business is busiest or the usual person is away, and the cost arrives as penalties, rework and lost goodwill. A shared schedule with named owners costs little to set up and turns a hidden risk into visible, checkable work.
