Meeting Decisions Never Recorded: The Cost of Debating Them Again

When decisions made in meetings are not written down, teams re-argue them, act on different understandings and lose the reasons behind the choice.

Mellorca Impact2026-11-14

The decision everyone remembers differently

A Durban property management company holds a Monday meeting with six managers. In March, they agree to stop using a particular cleaning contractor after a run of complaints from tenants. Nobody writes it down. In June, a new facilities coordinator hears that the contractor is cheaper than the alternative and signs a renewal. Two managers object, one says they never agreed to anything, and the meeting spends forty minutes re-opening a question that was closed three months earlier.

That is the pain of important decisions being made in meetings but never recorded. The conversation is not wasted the first time. The waste is in the second, third and fourth times the same discussion has to happen because no one can show what was decided, who was responsible, or why.

Discussion is not the same as a decision

Most meetings produce a mixture of information sharing, debate, opinions and, occasionally, decisions. The decisions are the part with lasting value, and they are often the part least likely to be captured, because everyone in the room believes they will remember them.

They do not. People leave with different understandings, particularly where a decision was reached by a nod rather than an explicit statement. A manager who was silent may have disagreed. A manager who said "let's look at it" may have meant yes. Without a written record, each person acts on the version that suits their own workload.

Where the money goes

Repeated debate

If a decision is revisited once, a six-person meeting spends perhaps 40 minutes on it. At an illustrative loaded cost of R450 per person-hour, that is R1,800 for the meeting time alone. A business that holds ten such re-run discussions a year loses R18,000 in meeting time before counting preparation. The general cost of meetings that end without clear outcomes is described in meetings without decisions; this article is about the narrower problem where a decision did occur but left no trace.

Work done on the wrong assumption

The more expensive case is when two people proceed on different understandings. One orders stock; the other cancels the budget. A quote goes out at the old price. A project team builds to the superseded specification. The cost is the work itself, plus the time to unwind it, and plus the awkwardness of discovering it late.

Lost context

A decision is only useful later if people know why it was made. If a rejected option comes back six months later, a record of the reasons would let the team answer in two minutes: "we considered that, and here is what we found". Without the record, the same research is repeated. This is a close relative of the problem in same internal questions, undocumented knowledge.

Accountability gaps

A decision with no named owner and no date is at risk of never happening. People leave the meeting with a vague sense that "someone" will handle it. The pattern is explored in employees unsure who owns the next action.

Governance exposure

For some decisions, a record is a legal expectation. The Companies Act, the legislation CIPC administers, contains requirements for companies to keep records, including minutes of meetings of shareholders and directors, and of certain resolutions. Directors who cannot show what was decided, and on what information, are in a weaker position if the decision is later challenged. Not every management meeting is a board meeting, but for decisions on spending, contracts, borrowing or risk, writing down the outcome is good practice even when no statute requires it. This is general information and not legal advice; take specific questions about your company's obligations to a company secretary or attorney.

Why decisions slip through

  • Nobody is the scribe. If minutes belong to everyone, they belong to no one. The most senior person is usually the worst choice, because they are busy contributing.
  • Full minutes feel like a chore. Two pages of narrative that nobody reads create a reason to skip minutes altogether.
  • The meeting ends in a rush. Participants leave for their next call, and the last five minutes that should have confirmed outcomes are lost.
  • The record is stored where nobody looks. Notes on a personal laptop, a photograph of a whiteboard in someone's camera roll, or a chat message buried in a group are all technically records, and none of them is findable.
  • Tools produce transcripts, not decisions. Automatic recording and summarising are useful, but a transcript is not a decision log. Microsoft's overview of AI features in Teams shows the kind of assistance on offer, and the same discipline applies: a person still needs to confirm what was actually decided and who owns it. Microsoft's training module on making meetings more productive with AI is a reasonable starting point for seeing what the tools can and cannot do.

A lightweight decision log

You do not need a minutes template that runs to three pages. A decision log is a single table, kept in one agreed place, with one row per decision. A workable row has these columns:

DateDecisionReasonOwnerDueReview date
12 MarchStop using the current cleaning contractor from 1 MayEleven tenant complaints in six weeksFacilities managerNotice by 31 MarchCheck complaints in July

One sentence for the decision, one for the reason, a named owner, and a date. If the decision cannot be written in one sentence, it probably has not yet been made.

The log should be reachable by everyone affected, searchable and editable only by the person responsible for it. A shared document, a channel in your collaboration tool, or a list in your project system will all do. What matters is that there is one location, and that new joiners are told where it is.

How to run the last five minutes

The habit that does the most good costs the least. Reserve the last five minutes of every recurring meeting to read out the decisions as they will be recorded. Ask "is that right?" and wait for a response. This quickly exposes disagreements that would otherwise have surfaced in June, and it takes less time than the first re-opened debate.

Circulate the log entries within 24 hours. Anyone who disagrees can say so while the meeting is still fresh. After a week, treat the entry as the official record.

Practical next actions

  1. Choose one place for decisions. Create a shared page or table, name an owner, and tell every meeting attendee where it is.
  2. Appoint a rotating scribe for each recurring meeting. Rotate monthly, so the task is shared and nobody is stuck with it permanently.
  3. Use the one-sentence rule. Record the decision, the reason, the owner and the due date, and keep narrative notes out of the log.
  4. Read the decisions back before the meeting closes. Spend five minutes confirming exactly what was agreed and who is responsible.
  5. Send the log entries the same day. Ask for corrections within 24 hours and then treat the record as final.
  6. Review last quarter's repeated debates. List the topics that came back more than once and log the current decision for each so they stop recurring.
  7. Check any meeting tools you already pay for. If your platform can produce summaries or action lists, test them on one meeting and compare them with what people actually agreed.

The value of a decision log builds quietly. In the first month it feels like extra admin. By the third, people begin to say "check the log" in place of "I thought we agreed". That change is where the saved hours come from.
