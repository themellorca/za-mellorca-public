Solving the Same Problem Twice: The Cost of Uncoordinated Projects

When projects are not centrally coordinated, separate teams build the same thing twice, and the business pays for wasted hours, competing outputs and delayed delivery.

Mellorca Impact2026-11-14

Two teams, one problem, two solutions

A regional retailer in the Western Cape wants to stop losing time at month end. The finance team starts building a spreadsheet that pulls branch sales into a single report. Six weeks later, the operations team, which never heard about it, commissions a freelancer to build a similar report from the point-of-sale exports. A third version appears in the head office, built by an analyst who needed the numbers for a board pack. Three people have spent weeks solving the same problem. Each solution is slightly different, and when the board asks which sales figure is right, nobody can answer quickly.

This article is about that pattern: teams duplicating work because projects are not centrally coordinated. It is not about laziness or poor teamwork. It happens in sensible, busy businesses because nobody has a single view of what is already under way, and the cost lands in wasted effort, competing outputs and delayed delivery.

How duplication starts

Duplicated projects rarely begin as duplicates. They begin as reasonable local decisions.

  • Each team solves its own pain. A manager sees a problem, finds a budget and starts. There is no step where anyone asks, "Is someone else already doing this?"
  • Projects live in private places. Plans sit in personal spreadsheets, email threads and chat groups. We describe that in projects managed through email and spreadsheets.
  • Names differ. One team calls it a dashboard, another a report, a third a tracker. They are the same need described in three vocabularies.
  • Nobody owns the list. Without a named person who maintains a list of active projects, the list does not exist.
  • Staff move on. A half-finished initiative is forgotten, and a year later a new manager starts it again.

The same thing happens with software. The article on departments buying separate tools for the same need shows the equivalent in subscriptions, where the duplication is easier to see because the invoices arrive each month.

The real cost of duplicated effort

Wasted hours at salary rates

Take the retailer example. If three people each spend 60 hours building a version of the same report, and their loaded cost averages R300 an hour, the total is R54,000. A single version would have cost R18,000, so R36,000 bought nothing. Add the hours spent in meetings arguing about which figure is right, and the true figure is higher. The numbers are illustrative; the shape is typical. A closely related picture is in departments duplicating the same work, which looks at the payroll angle.

Competing outputs and lost trust

Two versions of the same report do not produce twice the value. They produce disagreement. Leaders begin to question every figure, and the next request for a number is met with a request to "check it manually", which adds another round of effort.

Delays and opportunity cost

People who duplicate work are not doing something else. Projects that would have moved the business forward wait while the same ground is covered a second time. This opportunity cost is the hardest to see and often the largest.

Integration and support burden

Each additional tool, report or automation must be supported, updated and eventually retired. If three versions exist, three sets of knowledge exist, held by three people. When one leaves, nobody knows which version to keep. See unowned small automations and the cost of their failure for how that support gap appears.

Central coordination is not central control

Many managers resist coordination because they picture a committee that slows every idea down. That is the wrong model. Coordination means visibility, not permission. It is a simple, shared answer to three questions: what is under way, who is responsible, and what does it depend on.

Project portfolio practice treats this as a management responsibility that sits above any single project. Atlassian's overview of project portfolio management describes consolidating active and proposed projects into a single view so that leaders can see the collective demand on resources and spot conflicts between competing initiatives. You do not need portfolio software to use the idea. A shared view exists to help a business choose which work to do and to notice overlaps early. The aim is to avoid the retailer's problem: three teams, three solutions, and no knowledge of each other.

A lightweight way to coordinate

A business with 50 to 300 people can achieve most of the benefit with a minimum of structure.

  1. One project register. A shared list with a row for each active or planned initiative: name, owner, purpose in one sentence, systems touched, start date, expected finish and status.
  2. A rule that new work is added before it starts. Anyone beginning a project adds it to the register first. This is a five-minute step, not an approval gate.
  3. A short monthly review. Thirty minutes with the owners to scan the list for overlaps and dependencies. Overlaps are resolved by agreeing who leads and who joins.
  4. A named register keeper. One person maintains the list and chases stale rows.

Dependencies belong on the register as well, because hidden dependencies cause delays of their own. The page on project deadlines slipping because of hidden dependencies shows how a missing link between two projects becomes a missed deadline.

Tools that help, and their limits

Software can make a register easier to maintain and share. Microsoft documents Dynamics 365 Project Operations, which connects sales, resourcing, project management and finance in one application, with project management tools for planning work and tracking cost and progress, and many other planning tools offer portfolio views. For a smaller business, a shared spreadsheet or task board maintained by one person can serve for a long time. The tool matters less than the habit of entering projects before work starts and reading the list at a fixed time each month. A powerful system that nobody updates is no better than the private spreadsheets it replaced.

How to tell whether you have the problem

Ask five department heads to write down their three most important current projects, with no discussion. Compare the lists. If two lists contain something that sounds similar, or a project appears that someone else did not know about, you have duplication or a visibility gap. Repeat the exercise after three months of using a register. The number of surprises is your measure of progress.

Practical next actions

  1. Run the five-list test. Ask each department head for their three current projects and compare them side by side for overlap.
  2. Create one project register. Use a shared spreadsheet or task board with the owner, purpose, systems affected, dates and status for each initiative.
  3. Name a register keeper. Make one person responsible for keeping it current and for asking owners about stale entries.
  4. Add the entry-before-start rule. No project begins, and no freelancer is briefed, until it appears on the register.
  5. Hold a 30-minute monthly review. Look only for overlaps, shared dependencies and projects that have stopped moving.
  6. Merge or stop one overlap now. Pick the clearest duplication on the register, agree a single lead, and record the hours saved.

Coordination is a small amount of administration that returns the hours currently spent solving the same problem twice.
