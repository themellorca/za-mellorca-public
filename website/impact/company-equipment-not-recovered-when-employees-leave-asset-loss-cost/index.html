Equipment Not Returned When Staff Leave: The Cost of Lost Company Assets

Laptops, phones and monitors that walk out with departing staff cost replacement money, delay new starters and leave data and contracts exposed.

Mellorca Impact2026-11-14

The laptop that left with the employee

A Johannesburg marketing agency has 35 staff. When a designer resigns on the last day of the month, her manager is busy with a pitch, HR is finalising her payout, and IT is out at a client. She leaves at 17:00 with her goodbye cake and her handbag. Her laptop, a docking station, a second monitor she took home during lockdown and a company phone are still with her.

Three weeks later someone asks where the laptop is. Nobody knows who last recorded it. The phone is still on the company plan. The monitor was never on an asset list at all. Eventually the agency writes off the laptop, replaces it, and keeps paying for the airtime.

This is the pain of equipment not being recovered when employees leave. It rarely feels like a crisis, because each loss is small. Added together, it is a steady leak of capital, and it is nearly always preventable with a short checklist and a clear owner.

What gets lost, and what it really costs

The visible loss is the purchase price of the device. The full cost is wider.

  • Replacement cost. A business laptop, monitor, headset and phone can easily total R25,000 to R40,000 at current retail prices. These figures are illustrative; check your own invoices.
  • Delay to the new starter. If a replacement has to be bought, configured and delivered, the next person waits, as explained in new employees without equipment.
  • Continued service charges. Mobile contracts, data SIMs and software licences tied to a device carry on billing.
  • Data exposure. A laptop that is still with a former employee may hold client files, saved passwords and email. The risk behind that is explored in former employees still active in systems.
  • Management time. Chasing a former colleague by WhatsApp, drafting a letter and deciding whether to pursue the matter all cost hours that nobody budgeted for.

Why equipment goes missing

People rarely keep company property out of dishonesty. Most losses come from gaps in the process.

No single record of who holds what

Equipment is bought by different people, delivered to different places and handed over informally. If the asset list is a spreadsheet last updated eight months ago, nobody can say with confidence what a leaver should return.

Offboarding that starts too late

The resignation letter reaches HR, but the checklist begins on the last day. By then the employee is working from home, on leave balance, or already in a new role. Collecting equipment from someone who has gone is far harder than collecting it while they are still on site.

Equipment held outside the office

Remote and hybrid work spread devices across suburbs and cities. Chargers, monitors, tablets and test phones sit at home with no one tracking them. The situation is similar to the way orphaned software subscriptions survive after a person leaves: nothing triggers a review.

Unclear ownership of the process

HR assumes IT will collect the laptop. IT assumes the manager will. The manager assumes HR has a form. Responsibility is shared by everybody, so it is owned by nobody.

What the law says about recovering the cost

An employer who has not recovered equipment sometimes considers deducting the value from the leaver's final pay. South African employment law restricts this. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act places conditions on deductions from remuneration, including requirements for a written agreement and limits on what may be deducted for damage or loss. A deduction that does not meet those conditions can create a dispute that costs more than the equipment. This article is not legal advice. Take the specific facts to an employment lawyer or your bargaining council before deducting anything.

The practical lesson is to prevent the problem. A clear written equipment policy, signed when the equipment is issued, gives the business a firmer footing than an argument after the person has left.

Building a handover that works

Keep an asset register that is actually used

At minimum, record the item, serial number, date issued, person assigned and their manager. Take a photograph of the serial label at issue. Update the register on the day equipment moves, not at a quarterly review. A shared sheet is acceptable if one person owns it and nobody buys equipment outside the process.

Issue equipment against a signed acknowledgement

A one-page form listing the items, their condition and the employee's responsibility to return them is simple and inexpensive. Include accessories, because chargers and docks are exactly the items that vanish.

Start offboarding on the day notice is given

A standard checklist that begins when a resignation or dismissal is confirmed gives the business weeks, not hours, to arrange collection. For couriers or remote staff, send a prepaid collection booking and a box. Agree a date for the final return before the last working day.

Reduce the damage if a device is not returned

Managed devices help here. Tools such as Microsoft Intune let an administrator remove company data when a device is leaving organisational control. Intune's documentation distinguishes between the retire action, which removes company data from a device while leaving personal content intact, and the wipe action, which restores a device to factory settings. Choosing the right one for each device matters: a personally owned phone should normally be retired, while a company laptop that is lost may justify a wipe. Other management products offer similar controls. The point is that a business with enrolled devices has options when equipment does not come back; a business without them has none.

Standardise where you can

If every laptop is a different model, it is harder to reissue returned equipment and harder to know what is missing. The savings from fewer models are covered in mixed laptop models and accessories.

Practical next actions

  1. Do a one-off equipment count. Ask each employee to confirm, with a photograph, every company item they hold, including monitors, headsets, tablets, phones and SIM cards.
  2. Build or clean up the asset register. Capture item, serial number, issue date, current holder and manager, and name one person responsible for keeping it correct.
  3. Introduce a signed equipment acknowledgement. Use it for every new issue and for existing staff at their next review, with legal advice on the wording.
  4. Write an offboarding checklist that starts at notice. Include return of equipment, account closure, cancellation of mobile contracts and licence reassignment, with an owner and a date for each step.
  5. Arrange collection for remote leavers before their last day. Book a courier, supply packaging and ask for a tracking number.
  6. Enrol company laptops and phones in device management. Test the retire and wipe options on a spare device so you know what each does before you need it.
  7. Review monthly airtime and licence bills against current staff. Any line without a current holder is a signal that equipment or access has been left behind.

None of this requires distrust of your people. It simply replaces memory and goodwill with a record, so that when someone leaves, everyone knows what is expected and the business keeps what it paid for.
