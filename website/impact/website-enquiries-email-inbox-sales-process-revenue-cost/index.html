An inbox is not a sales process: The revenue cost of emailed website enquiries

When website enquiries land in an email inbox, nobody can see which were answered, quoted, won or lost. Capturing them as records in a managed sales process makes revenue leakage measurable and fixable.

Mellorca Impact2026-10-07

The tolerated pain

The website contact form works. When someone fills it in, an email arrives in info@ or in the owner's inbox. Somebody reads it, replies, maybe forwards it to a salesperson. Sometimes a quote follows. Sometimes the conversation continues on WhatsApp or by phone. Sometimes nothing happens because the email was read on a phone between meetings and then sank below thirty newer messages.

Ask the business three simple questions and the problem appears. How many website enquiries arrived last month? How many received a quote? How many became paying customers? In most inbox-based setups nobody can answer with confidence. An inbox records that a message arrived. It does not record what happened next.

Three related problems are covered elsewhere on Mellorca: the revenue cost of slow lead response, enquiries that nobody owns and website forms that fail silently. This article is about the structural gap underneath all three: an inbox is not a sales process.

Why businesses tolerate it

  • It was the default. Website builders and developers set forms to send email because it requires no further setup.
  • Volumes started small. Five enquiries a week can be managed from memory. Fifty cannot, but the change happened gradually.
  • The CRM feels like extra admin. Salespeople see logging an enquiry as typing for the manager's benefit, especially when the CRM is not connected to the form.
  • Email feels personal. Replying directly to a prospect feels faster and warmer than working from a system.
  • Marketing and sales are measured separately. Marketing counts form fills; sales counts deals. Nobody counts what happens in between.

Operational symptoms

  • enquiries forwarded between people with "can you handle this?" and no confirmation that anyone did;
  • two salespeople replying to the same prospect, or none;
  • quotes sent from personal inboxes that leave with the employee;
  • no record of why an enquiry was not pursued, so poor-fit enquiries and missed ones look identical;
  • marketing spend judged on clicks and form fills because nobody can link spend to won deals;
  • a monthly scramble to assemble a sales report from sent items and memory.

What a managed sales process records that an inbox cannot

The difference is not the software brand. It is whether each enquiry becomes a record with an owner, a stage and an outcome. Microsoft's documentation on qualifying and converting leads illustrates the idea. A lead is either qualified, which creates an opportunity record to track the deal, or disqualified while keeping an audit trail. If the prospect returns, the record can be reactivated with its notes and attachments, without re-entering everything. An email thread offers none of that structure.

Getting enquiries into such a record does not always require rebuilding the website. HubSpot's documentation on collecting submissions from non-HubSpot forms describes capturing submissions from an existing website form as contact records, provided the form meets certain conditions such as being a static HTML form with an email field. Most CRMs offer a similar route, whether through native forms, a connector or an integration platform.

The personal-information angle

Website enquiries contain names, phone numbers, email addresses and often details of a person's needs or circumstances. The Information Regulator's page on the Protection of Personal Information Act explains that a responsible party must secure the integrity and confidentiality of personal information through reasonable technical and organisational measures, and that data subjects may ask for their information to be corrected or deleted. When enquiries are scattered across several personal inboxes and forwarded copies, it is much harder to control access, answer such a request or know where a person's details are held.

How the pain becomes money

  • Revenue loss: enquiries that are never answered, or never followed up after the first reply, are sales the business paid to attract and then let go.
  • Wasted marketing spend: without enquiry outcomes, budget keeps flowing to channels that generate form fills but not customers.
  • Labour cost: time spent forwarding, searching sent items and rebuilding sales reports by hand.
  • Continuity risk: when a salesperson leaves, their open enquiries and quotes leave with their inbox.
  • Compliance exposure: scattered personal information is harder to secure and to account for.

An illustrative cost model

This example is illustrative. Every figure is an assumption for a hypothetical business. Picture a Gqeberha solar installer receiving website enquiries through a contact form that emails a shared inbox.

AssumptionValue
Website enquiries per month80
Share never worked beyond a first reply15% (12 enquiries)
Share of worked enquiries that become sales10%
Average gross profit per saleR22,000
Admin time rebuilding monthly sales reports6 hours at R350
  • Lost sales: 12 enquiries × 10% = 1.2 sales a month; 1.2 × R22,000 = R26,400 a month in gross profit
  • Annual lost gross profit: R26,400 × 12 = R316,800
  • Reporting time: 6 × R350 × 12 = R25,200 a year
  • Illustrative total: about R342,000 a year, before any effect on marketing budget decisions

The assumption that drives everything is the share of enquiries never worked. The point of moving to a managed process is that you would no longer need to assume it. You could count it.

When it becomes commercially urgent

  • paid advertising drives a meaningful share of enquiries, so each lost enquiry has a known acquisition cost;
  • more than one person replies to enquiries;
  • a salesperson leaves and nobody can see what they had open;
  • leadership asks which marketing channel produces customers, and nobody can answer;
  • a customer asks what personal information the business holds about them.

What better looks like

  1. Every enquiry becomes a record. The form writes directly to the CRM or sales system; an email notification is optional, not the system of record.
  2. Each record has an owner and a stage. New, contacted, quoted, won, lost or disqualified, with a reason.
  3. The source is captured. Which page, campaign or channel produced the enquiry is stored with it.
  4. Follow-up is prompted. Open enquiries without activity appear in a list someone reviews.
  5. Personal information sits in one controlled place. Access is role-based, not dependent on who was copied on an email.

The Insights article how a CRM turns into an expensive address book is a useful warning: a CRM only helps if enquiries flow into it automatically and the stages reflect how the team actually sells.

Practical next actions

  1. Count last month's website enquiries from the inbox and try to trace each one to an outcome. The share you cannot trace is your starting measure.
  2. Check where your website form sends submissions today, and who receives them.
  3. Define five or six enquiry stages and the reasons an enquiry can be closed as lost or disqualified.
  4. Connect the form to your CRM so each submission creates a record with its source.
  5. Review open enquiries without activity at least weekly.
  6. Remove personal inboxes as the place where enquiries live.

Bottom line

A website that generates enquiries has done its job. An inbox that receives them has not done the next one. Without owners, stages and outcomes, a business cannot see how many paid-for enquiries it loses, which channels actually produce customers or where personal information is held. Routing enquiries straight into a managed sales process turns guesswork about lost revenue into a number the business can manage.
