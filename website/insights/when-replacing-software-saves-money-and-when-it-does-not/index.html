When Replacing Software Saves Money—and When It Does Not

A cheaper licence does not guarantee a cheaper operation. Counting migration, retraining, lost productivity and process change shows when a replacement genuinely pays for itself.

Mellorca Insights2026-11-16

The financial manager of an East London distribution business noticed that the company was paying about R38,000 a month for its warehouse management system. A competing product offered similar features for R21,000. On paper that was a saving of over R200,000 a year, and the board approved the switch. Eighteen months later the business had spent more on the migration, extra staff during the transition and a rebuilt integration with its courier partners than it had saved, and pick accuracy had dropped for two quarters while staff learned the new system.

Replacing software sometimes saves real money. Often it does not, at least not on the timescale people expect. The difference usually lies not in the licence prices but in what the comparison leaves out.

The cost of staying is not just the licence

Start by counting what the current system really costs. The UK Government Digital Service's guidance on managing legacy technology lists reasons it may be time to migrate, including that the cost of maintaining old technology has become greater than replacing it, that inefficiency is preventing necessary changes, and that supplier support is ending. Each of these is a cost of staying, and few of them appear on the software invoice.

For a business system, the full cost of staying usually includes:

  • Licences, hosting and support fees.
  • Specialist time to maintain customisations and integrations.
  • Staff time on workarounds, re-keying and manual checks caused by the system's limits.
  • Errors and their consequences, such as credit notes, rework and customer complaints.
  • Risk: an unsupported version, a single person who understands it, or security weaknesses.
  • Opportunity cost: changes the business cannot make because the system will not allow them.

Our article on comparing the cost of a tool with the cost of the process around it shows how to estimate the workaround and error costs, which are often larger than the licence.

The cost of switching is mostly one-off and mostly hidden

The cost of replacing a system includes far more than the new subscription:

  • Selection and contracting: internal time on requirements, demos and negotiation.
  • Configuration: setting up the new system to match how the business works.
  • Data migration: cleaning, mapping, loading and checking records.
  • Integrations: rebuilding every connection to accounting, CRM, payroll, couriers and banks.
  • Parallel running: paying for both systems, and often doing work twice, for weeks or months.
  • Training and lost productivity: people working more slowly while they learn.
  • Process change: rewriting procedures, reports and controls.
  • Exit costs: notice periods, data extraction fees or early termination charges from the old supplier.

The GDS guidance on defining a purchasing strategy adds a warning that applies to any business: even small modifications to off-the-shelf software can remove most of the benefits of using it, and customisations and workarounds can increase costs, make maintenance harder and restrict future upgrades. If you plan to customise the new system heavily to make it behave like the old one, you are likely buying the old system's costs again.

Expect a dip before the gain

Research on technology adoption suggests that a temporary drop in productivity after a major change is normal rather than a sign of failure. In their paper on the productivity J-curve, Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson argue that new general-purpose technologies require significant complementary investment in new processes, business models and skills, much of it intangible and poorly measured, so that early productivity is understated and later gains overstated. Their work is about whole economies, but the shape is familiar in a single business: costs and disruption come first, benefits later.

For a replacement decision, that means the payback calculation should include a realistic dip, typically weeks to months of slower work, and the business case should still hold after it.

When replacing usually does save money

  • The current system needs expensive specialist support that is becoming harder to find.
  • Large amounts of staff time go into workarounds that the replacement would remove without customisation.
  • You are paying for two or more overlapping systems and can consolidate to one.
  • The current supplier is ending support or forcing an expensive upgrade anyway.
  • The new system fits your process largely as it comes, with configuration rather than custom development.

When it usually does not

  • The saving is mainly on licence price, and the current system works acceptably.
  • The real problems are in the process, the data or the way people use the system, which will follow you to the new one. Our article on why switching software is not always the answer covers this trap.
  • The current system is deeply integrated with others, and every connection must be rebuilt.
  • The business is at a busy or unstable moment, such as peak season or a restructure, when the dip will hurt most.
  • The replacement will need heavy customisation to fit.

Compare over three to five years

Put staying and switching side by side over three to five years, not one. Include one-off switching costs in year one, a productivity dip in the first months, and the ongoing differences in licence, support, workaround and error costs. Then test the result against pessimistic assumptions: migration taking twice as long, adoption slower than hoped. If switching only wins in the optimistic case, the honest answer is usually to improve what you have. Our guide on deciding whether to replace or reconfigure a system sets out the alternatives to a full replacement.

Use the renewal date, but do not let it rush you

Contract renewals are a natural moment to ask these questions, as our article on how software renewal dates can force better technology decisions explains. Start the analysis six to nine months before the date. A decision made in the last month, under pressure to avoid another annual commitment, tends to underestimate switching costs.

Practical next actions

  1. Calculate the full annual cost of the current system, including workarounds, errors, specialist support and risk, not only licences.
  2. List every one-off cost of switching, from data migration and integrations to parallel running and exit fees.
  3. Build a three-to-five-year comparison that includes a realistic productivity dip after the switch.
  4. Test the comparison with pessimistic assumptions and check whether switching still wins.
  5. Before deciding to replace, identify which problems are caused by process or data rather than the software, and fix those first.
