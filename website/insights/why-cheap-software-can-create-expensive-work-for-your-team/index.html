Why Cheap Software Can Create Expensive Work

A low subscription price hides the re-keying, workarounds and exit costs that land on your staff; here is how to count them before you buy.

Mellorca Insights2026-11-14

A bookkeeper in Pretoria finds a tool that tracks customer follow-ups for a few hundred rand a month. It is half the price of the alternative the owner had been considering. It is adopted in an afternoon. Six months later, one staff member spends two hours every Friday exporting its data, cleaning it and re-typing it into the accounting package, because the two do not connect. The subscription is still cheap. The Fridays are not.

Cheap software is not a problem in itself. The problem is that the price on the invoice measures only one of the costs, and often the smallest. This article looks at the work that cheap software can create, why it is easy to miss, and how to check for it before you buy.

The price is not the cost

The UK government's Technology Code of Practice is the standard used in its spend-control process, and its guidance repeatedly asks buyers to look beyond the price tag. In the section on defining a purchasing strategy, it tells teams to aim to understand as much of the full cost of building or buying a product as possible, and to understand the product's lifecycle, including upgrading, continuous improvement and retirement. You can read the full text in the purchasing strategy guidance. Its data guidance goes further and calls for a purchasing strategy that considers the total cost of a tool or service through its full lifecycle, as the guidance on making better use of data sets out.

The wording is government-specific, but the idea is simple. Software has a cost of acquiring, a cost of running and a cost of leaving. Cheap tools tend to be cheap on the first and expensive on the other two, because the work that the vendor has not built is left to your staff.

Where the expensive work hides

Our earlier piece on why cheap SaaS can become expensive operations looks at pricing structures. Here the focus is on the human work that accumulates around a low-cost tool. It tends to appear in six places.

  • Re-keying and exporting. If the tool does not connect to your other systems, someone moves the data by hand. This is the commonest hidden cost, and it grows with volume. See how duplicate software creates duplicate work.
  • Workarounds for missing features. A missing approval step becomes an email. A missing report becomes a spreadsheet. A missing permission setting becomes a rule everyone is trusted to remember.
  • Checking and correcting. Copies drift apart. Someone has to compare, find the differences and decide which is right.
  • Training and re-training. A tool with weak onboarding or an awkward interface costs time on every new hire, and on every returning user after a long gap.
  • Support by colleagues. When the vendor offers little help, the person who happens to know the tool becomes its unofficial support desk, and their own work waits.
  • Switching and exit. If exports are limited, leaving costs weeks of manual extraction. The cost arrives later and is paid once, but it is large.

A quick way to estimate the real cost

You do not need a financial model. A rough calculation is enough to show which side of the line a tool sits on.

  1. Write down the monthly subscription for all users.
  2. List every recurring manual task the tool creates, and estimate the hours per month for each.
  3. Multiply the hours by a realistic internal cost per hour, which is the loaded cost of salary and overheads and not only the wage.
  4. Add one-off costs spread over three years: set-up, training, integration work and the expected cost of migrating off.
  5. Compare the total with the same calculation for a more expensive alternative that includes the connections or features you would otherwise do by hand.

The cheaper tool sometimes still wins. It should win because the numbers say so, not because the subscription line is smaller. For a longer discussion of how to judge value after purchase, see how to measure whether software is paying for itself.

Test it on your hardest problem before you commit

A demonstration shows the tool working on its best day. A trial on your own problem shows how it behaves on yours. The purchasing guidance recommends asking for a demonstration or trial on a smaller scale, and during the trial it suggests trying to solve one small but hard problem, testing integration with your current products and testing the user experience. That advice transfers directly to a small business.

Pick the most awkward real case you have, such as a customer with three delivery addresses, a credit note against an invoice from last quarter or a staff member on unpaid leave. Run it through the trial. If the tool handles the hard case, the easy ones will follow. If it needs three workarounds, you have found your hidden cost before paying for it.

Questions to ask before you buy

Add these to whatever checklist you already use. Our article on questions to ask before you buy another tool has a wider list.

  • What does it connect to out of the box, and what needs custom work or a paid add-on?
  • Which features sit in higher tiers, and which of them will we need within a year?
  • What are the limits on records, users, storage or automation runs, and what happens when we reach them?
  • Can we export everything, in a usable format, without a fee?
  • Who inside the business will own the tool, configure it and support other users?

The Technology Code of Practice also asks buyers to consider whether a product will integrate and adapt with existing technology, as its overview of all thirteen points shows. For a small business, "integrate" is the question most likely to turn a cheap tool into an expensive one.

When cheap is the right answer

None of this means you should buy the most expensive option. A cheap tool is fine when the task is self-contained, the data does not need to move, few people use it and leaving it would be easy. A simple tool for a single team's task list, with no integrations and easy export, can be excellent value. The risk lies in tools that sit in the middle of a process and touch data that other systems need.

Practical next actions

  1. Pick your three cheapest paid tools and list the manual tasks each one creates every week, however small.
  2. Estimate the monthly hours and loaded cost of those tasks and compare the total with the subscription.
  3. For any tool where hidden work exceeds the subscription, ask the vendor about integration options and higher tiers, and cost them properly.
  4. Add a trial step to your buying process: one hard real case, one integration test and one exit test before you sign.
  5. Record the real cost of each tool next to its subscription in your software register, and review the figures at renewal.

The cheapest software is the one that costs you the least once everything is counted. That is seldom the one with the lowest invoice.
