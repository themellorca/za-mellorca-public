Why Continuous Improvement Needs a Backlog

Improvement ideas that live in meetings, chats and memories get lost or argued over. A single ordered backlog turns them into a steady flow of small, tested changes.

Mellorca Insights2026-11-16

In March, the team at a Polokwane agricultural equipment dealer held an improvement workshop. It produced 46 sticky notes: duplicate customer records, slow warranty claims, a stock report nobody trusted, a quoting template with the wrong VAT wording, and much more. Three of the ideas were fixed that month. By August the flipchart had been thrown away, two of the people who raised the best ideas had stopped suggesting things, and the same complaints had resurfaced in a new workshop.

This pattern is common. Businesses are rarely short of improvement ideas. They are short of a system for holding, choosing and finishing them. That system has a plain name: a backlog. Without one, continuous improvement becomes occasional improvement, driven by whoever is loudest or most frustrated this week.

What a backlog is

The term comes from software teams, but the idea is general. The Scrum Guide defines a product backlog as an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product, and the single source of work for the team. Three words in that definition matter for any improvement effort.

  • Single: one list, not ideas scattered across meeting notes, emails, chat groups and people's heads.
  • Ordered: ranked, so everyone can see what comes next and what does not.
  • Emergent: it changes as the business learns, with new items added and old ones dropped.

An improvement backlog applies this to the way the business operates: processes, systems, data, documents and handoffs.

Why improvement fails without one

The Lean Enterprise Institute describes kaizen as small, incremental improvements that add up to significant long-term benefits, through a process that involves everyone and encourages people to identify problems and propose practical solutions. That depends on three things a backlog provides.

First, ideas need somewhere to go. When a staff member suggests a fix and hears nothing, they stop suggesting. A backlog gives every idea a visible home and a status, even if that status is "not now".

Second, small fixes need protection from big projects. Without a list, attention flows to the largest or most recent problem, and the dozens of small irritations that cost a few minutes each, many times a day, never get done.

Third, choices need to be explicit. A business can only work on a few improvements at once. Without an ordered list, those choices are made implicitly, by default or by volume. With one, they are made deliberately and can be explained.

What to put in each item

Keep items short. Each should answer:

  • Problem: what goes wrong, for whom and how often, in one or two sentences.
  • Evidence: a count, example or measure, even a rough one, such as "about 15 warranty claims a month wait more than a week for parts approval".
  • Proposed change: the idea, if there is one. It is fine for an item to be a problem without a solution yet.
  • Size: small, medium or large.
  • Owner: the process owner who will decide whether and how to act.
  • Status: new, ready, in progress, testing, done or declined.

The list can live in a shared spreadsheet, a simple board in your project tool, or a list in the system your team already uses. The tool matters less than having only one.

How to order it

Ordering is where most of the value comes from. A practical approach for a smaller business is to score each item on two dimensions, the value of fixing it and the effort required, and work mostly from the high-value, low-effort corner. Our article on prioritising digital improvements when budget is limited describes the scoring in more detail, and ranking manual tasks before automating shows how to estimate the value of removing manual work.

Add two simple rules. Keep a share of capacity, say a third, for small items so they are not permanently starved by larger ones. And review the order monthly with process owners, since what was urgent in March may not be in August.

Refine before you start

The Scrum Guide describes backlog refinement as breaking items down into smaller, more precise pieces, adding detail such as description, order and size. For improvement work this matters because most ideas arrive as complaints: "warranty claims are slow". Before work starts, refine the item into something testable: "Allow branch managers to approve parts for warranty claims under R5,000 without waiting for head office."

Test changes, do not just implement them

A backlog organises the work, but each item still needs a way to check that the change helped. The Institute for Healthcare Improvement's Model for Improvement pairs three questions, what are we trying to accomplish, how will we know a change is an improvement, and what change can we make, with Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles that test a change on a small scale before spreading it. The same method suits business processes: try the new approval rule in one branch for a month, compare claim times, then decide.

That is why the backlog needs a "testing" status. Items should not move to "done" until someone has checked the result. Our article on what to measure at each stage of a business workflow suggests measures to compare before and after.

Close the loop with the people who raised items

The cheapest way to sustain improvement is to tell people what happened to their ideas. When an item is done, tell whoever raised it and the team affected. When an item is declined, say why. Declined items with a clear reason are far better for morale than items that vanish.

Watch for backlog bloat

A backlog can become a graveyard of hundreds of ideas nobody will ever do. Prune it quarterly. Remove items that are no longer relevant, merge duplicates, and decline anything that has sat untouched at the bottom for six months. A list of 30 live items that people trust is more useful than 300 that nobody reads. For the process-design thinking that feeds the best items, see why repeated workarounds are a warning sign.

Practical next actions

  1. Gather every outstanding improvement idea from meeting notes, emails and chats into one list in a tool the team already uses.
  2. Give each item a problem statement, evidence, size, owner and status.
  3. Score items for value and effort, order the list, and reserve about a third of improvement capacity for small items.
  4. Refine the top five items into specific, testable changes and trial each one on a small scale before rolling it out.
  5. Review the order monthly, prune the list quarterly, and tell people what happened to the ideas they raised.
