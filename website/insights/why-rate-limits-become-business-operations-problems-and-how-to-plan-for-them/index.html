Why Rate Limits Become Business Operations Problems

Software platforms cap how many requests your integrations and automations can make. When volume grows, those caps quietly turn into late orders, stale stock figures and missed messages. Here is how to plan for them.

Mellorca Insights2026-11-13

An online retailer in Gqeberha runs a Black Friday promotion. Orders arrive faster than ever, and the integration that copies each order from the online store to the stock system starts falling behind. By mid-morning, stock levels on the website are an hour out of date. Products that have sold out are still on sale. By the afternoon, the customer service team is phoning people to cancel orders it cannot fulfil.

Nothing crashed. No error appeared on anyone's screen. The integration was being slowed down by the platforms it talks to, because it was making more requests than those platforms allow. That limit, usually called a rate limit or throttling, is a technical detail on the vendor's side. On the business side, it becomes oversold stock, refunds and a bad day for customers.

What a rate limit is, in business terms

Almost every cloud platform caps how many requests a single customer, application or user can make in a period of time. The cap protects the platform and its other customers. When you exceed it, the platform slows you down or temporarily refuses your requests until the period resets.

Microsoft's documentation on Microsoft Graph throttling is a clear example. When a threshold is exceeded, the service limits further requests from that application for a time, returns an HTTP 429 "Too Many Requests" response and suggests how long to wait before trying again. It also notes that limits vary by scenario, so writes might be throttled while reads still work. Automation platforms have their own limits too. Microsoft's page on limits for automated, scheduled and instant flows explains that when a flow exceeds its limits its activity slows, and that a flow consistently above the limits for 14 days is turned off.

Read that last point again from a business perspective: an automation that the business depends on can be switched off by the platform because it has outgrown its allowance.

Why rate limits surprise businesses

Rate limits rarely matter during design and testing, because test volumes are small. They start to matter later, for reasons that are good news in themselves:

  • Growth. More customers, orders or transactions mean more requests.
  • Peaks. Month-end, payroll runs, promotions and the start of a school term can multiply volume for a few hours or days.
  • New automations sharing an allowance. Limits often apply per account, user or tenant. A second automation added by another team uses the same allowance as the first.
  • Bulk jobs. A one-off import, clean-up or migration run during working hours can starve the everyday integrations.
  • Inefficient design. An integration that checks every record every few minutes, rather than reacting only to changes, burns through its allowance doing nothing useful.

Because throttling slows things down rather than stopping them outright, the first symptom is usually stale data, not an error. That makes rate limits a close relative of the problem we describe in how silent automation failures damage operations.

The operational consequences

When integrations are throttled, the business sees effects such as stock figures that lag reality, orders that reach the warehouse late, invoices created hours after delivery, customer notifications that arrive out of order and reports that disagree because different systems updated at different times. The cost lands on operations and customer service, not on the technical team, which is why rate limits are a business operations problem.

How to design integrations that cope

You cannot remove a vendor's limits, but you can design around them. The main techniques are well established.

Retry properly. Microsoft's guidance on the retry pattern recommends waiting before retrying temporary failures, increasing the delay between attempts and avoiding aggressive retries that make a busy service worse. The Graph guidance adds that honouring the suggested wait time is the fastest way to recover. Both warn that retries can cause an operation to run twice if it is not safe to repeat. Our article on designing retries without creating duplicate work covers that risk in detail.

React to changes instead of polling. Where a platform offers change notifications or webhooks, use them. Checking for changes every few minutes is one of the most common causes of hitting limits for no benefit.

Batch where possible. Sending fifty updates in one request usually costs less allowance than fifty separate requests, depending on the platform.

Queue the work. When volume spikes, put work in a queue and process it at a steady rate, so nothing is lost and the order of events is preserved. See handling failed integrations without losing the work in flight.

Prioritise. Not all requests matter equally. Stock updates and new orders should not wait behind a nightly report refresh. Separate critical and non-critical work so the critical path keeps its allowance.

Plan capacity like any other resource

Treat platform allowances the way you would treat warehouse space or staff hours. For each critical integration, find out which limits apply, whether they are per user, per application or per account, and what your current usage is. Estimate usage at your peak, not your average. If a promotion will triple orders, will the integration still keep up?

Check how limits interact with licensing. On some platforms, a higher licence tier or a dedicated capacity add-on raises the allowance. That cost belongs in the business case for growth, not as a surprise after the fact.

Schedule bulk work deliberately. Imports, data clean-ups and historical syncs should run outside peak hours and, where possible, with their own allowance so they do not compete with daily operations.

Make throttling visible

Because throttling degrades quietly, you need explicit signals. Useful measures include the delay between an event in one system and its arrival in the next, the number of throttled or retried requests per day, the size of any queue waiting to be processed and warning messages from the platform about limits. Set thresholds that matter to the business, such as "stock on the website more than ten minutes behind", and alert the person who can act. The right targets depend on how critical each integration is, a question covered in what service levels make sense for business-critical automations.

Practical next actions

  1. List your business-critical integrations and automations and, for each, find the platform limits that apply and whether they are shared with other automations.
  2. Estimate request volume at your busiest hour of the year and compare it with those limits.
  3. Replace frequent polling with change notifications where the platform supports them, and batch requests where it does not.
  4. Check that retries wait, increase their delay, honour suggested wait times and cannot create duplicate records.
  5. Move bulk imports and clean-ups outside peak hours and separate critical from non-critical work.
  6. Add a business-level alert on data delay for each critical integration, routed to someone who can act.
