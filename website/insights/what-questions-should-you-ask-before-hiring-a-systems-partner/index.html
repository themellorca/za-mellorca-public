What Questions Should You Ask Before Hiring a Systems Partner?

The right systems partner leaves your business more capable and less dependent. These questions test diagnosis, delivery, data handling, documentation and the exit before you sign.

Mellorca Insights2026-11-16

A Stellenbosch wine exporter hired a small consultancy to connect its online shop, warehouse and accounting system. The work was delivered on time and the integrations ran well for a year. Then the consultancy's lead developer left, the remaining staff could not explain how the connections worked, and the exporter discovered that the integration platform account, with all its configurations, was registered in the consultancy's name. Moving it took three months and a dispute about who owned what.

None of this was malicious, and all of it was avoidable with better questions at the start. Choosing a systems partner is not only about technical skill. It is about how they diagnose, how they deliver, how they treat your data, what they leave behind, and how easily you can carry on without them.

Why the choice matters more than it looks

A systems partner gains access to the core of your operations: customer data, financial systems, integrations and often administrator accounts. The UK National Cyber Security Centre's supply chain security guidance notes that most organisations rely on suppliers to deliver products, systems and services, that vulnerabilities can be introduced at any point in the supply chain, and that many companies lose sight of their suppliers. It proposes principles grouped under understanding the risks, establishing control, checking your arrangements and continuous improvement. A small business does not need a formal programme, but it does need to ask the right questions before granting access.

Questions about diagnosis

  • How will you understand our current operation before proposing a solution? A good partner wants to see how work actually flows, not just collect a feature list. Be wary of a proposal that arrives before anyone has looked at your processes.
  • What would you advise us not to do? Partners who only ever recommend more software, more customisation or bigger projects are selling, not advising.
  • How do you decide between configuring what we have and buying something new? Listen for an answer that starts from the business problem. Our article on avoiding vendor-led architecture explains why this matters.

Questions about delivery

  • Who exactly will do the work, and what happens if they leave? Ask to meet the people, not only the sales lead. Ask how knowledge is shared within their team.
  • How will you deliver in stages, and what will we have at the end of each one? Prefer partners who deliver something usable early over those who promise everything at the end.
  • How do you handle changes in scope? Get a clear answer about how changes are requested, priced and approved. Our guide on scoping a systems implementation before you sign covers what the contract should say.
  • Can you show us a similar piece of work, and can we speak to that client? A reference from a business of similar size and complexity is worth more than a list of large logos.

Questions about data and security

Under South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act, a partner who processes personal information on your behalf is usually an operator, and you remain the responsible party. The Information Regulator's POPIA guidance explains that the responsible party remains responsible for notifying security compromises, and that an operator must notify the responsible party if a compromise affects the personal information it processes for them. That makes these questions essential:

  • Which of our systems and data will you access, and with which accounts? Insist on named, individual accounts with the least access needed, not shared administrator logins.
  • Where will our data be stored or processed, including in any tools you use? This includes integration platforms, test environments and AI tools.
  • How and how quickly will you tell us about a security incident? Get this in writing, in the contract.
  • What happens to our data and your access when the engagement ends?

Questions about ownership and documentation

  • In whose name will accounts, subscriptions and integration platforms be registered? The answer should almost always be yours, with the partner given access.
  • What documentation will we receive, and in what form? Expect a description of what was built, how it connects, how to operate it and what to do when it fails. Our article on why documentation is part of the business system explains what good handover looks like.
  • Who owns any custom code, scripts or configurations?

Questions about after go-live

The UK Government Digital Service's guidance on defining a purchasing strategy makes a point that applies to any business buying outside expertise: when you procure a team to build something, consider how you will manage the technology once the specialist team has built it. It also warns that customisations and workarounds to off-the-shelf products can increase cost, make maintenance harder and restrict upgrades. So ask:

  • What support do you offer after launch, at what cost and with what response times?
  • Could another competent firm, or our own staff, maintain what you build?
  • How much of the solution relies on custom development rather than standard configuration?
  • How do you monitor what you have built, and who gets told when it fails?

Questions about the exit

Every engagement ends eventually. Ask how a handover to another provider would work, how long the notice period is, and what a clean exit costs. A partner who is comfortable discussing their own replacement is usually one who builds things you can keep.

How to judge the answers

Look for specific, unhurried answers backed by examples. Vague answers to the data, ownership and exit questions are warning signs, regardless of how impressive the technical pitch was. If you are unsure what your current systems need, an independent assessment first, as described in what a digital systems audit should tell you, gives you a baseline to judge proposals against.

Practical next actions

  1. Write the questions above into a short evaluation sheet and ask every shortlisted partner the same ones.
  2. Meet the people who will actually do the work and ask for a reference from a client of similar size.
  3. Confirm in the contract that accounts and subscriptions are registered to your business, and that incident notification is defined.
  4. Agree the documentation and handover you will receive, and what support looks like after go-live.
  5. Ask each partner to describe how you would move to another provider, and judge the answer as seriously as the technical proposal.
