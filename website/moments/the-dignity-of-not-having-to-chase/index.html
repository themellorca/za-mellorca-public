The Dignity of Not Having to Chase

Chasing customers for documents, signatures and payments casts staff as nags. How follow-up that runs by itself protects relationships and the self-respect of the people who used to do the chasing.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-13

Some jobs quietly turn into chasing. The administrator at a small practice sends the same email three times to get a signed engagement letter. She phones about a missing tax certificate, then phones again. She asks, as politely as she can, whether the invoice from two months ago has been paid. Every message is reasonable. Together they turn a skilled professional into the person customers sigh at when they see her name.

This piece is about the cost of being the one who chases, and the relief when routine follow-up stops depending on a person's persistence. It is reasoned from general research on job demands and support, not from a study of South African service businesses.

How chasing becomes a role

Most service businesses depend on things customers have to send: documents, signatures, approvals, information, payment. Customers are busy, and many of these requests are not their priority. So someone in the business has to remind them, and then remind them again. Because the reminders are irregular and personal, they fall to whoever cares most about the work being finished.

Consider a hypothetical accounting practice with eight staff and three hundred clients. At filing season, the practice needs bank statements, invoices and signed forms from most of them. One administrator keeps a list and works through it each day, sending emails and making calls. She knows which clients answer quickly and which need five reminders. That knowledge lives in her head and her inbox. When she is away, the chasing stops.

The relational cost

Chasing changes how a person is seen. A customer who receives a third reminder from the same person begins to associate that person with pressure, not service. The staff member knows it. They soften their wording, apologise for following up, add "just checking in" to messages that are not really check-ins. The work becomes a constant effort to stay polite while being persistent.

That effort has a cost to dignity. Professionals want to be valued for what they know and do, not for how often they remind. Being cast as the nag, even unfairly, is a small and repeated injury. It is also unequal: the person chasing carries the awkwardness, while the person who is late often feels little.

What the research says

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standard on demands calls for demands that are achievable within agreed hours and for people's skills to be matched to the job. A role that has drifted into constant chasing often fails both tests: the volume rises unpredictably at busy times, and the work uses persistence more than skill. The standard on support says employees should know how to obtain the resources they need to do their job, which includes tools that carry routine work.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work names excessive workloads and the under-use of skills among psychosocial risks. Chasing on its own is not harmful, but a job dominated by it can carry both.

Follow-up that runs by itself

Much routine chasing is predictable. The business knows in advance which documents it needs, from whom and by when. That means the reminders can be scheduled: a first request, a polite reminder after a set number of days, a second reminder, and only then a personal call. Many practice-management, accounting and customer relationship tools can send these automatically, with a link that lets the customer upload or sign directly.

The effect is not only time saved. The reminders come from the business, not from a person. Customers receive a consistent, neutral message that does not carry anyone's frustration. When the staff member does step in, it is for a real conversation, perhaps a client who is struggling or confused, not for the fifth identical nudge. The Moment on the slow leak of time through manual reminders looks at the time; this piece is about the person's standing. The pattern on cash collection that depends on someone remembering applies the same thinking to payments, and the impact analysis on inconsistent payment follow-up counts the cash cost.

Practical next actions

  1. List what you chase. Write down every type of item your staff chase customers for, such as documents, signatures, approvals and payments, with how often each is late.
  2. Write a standard sequence. For each type, agree a schedule: first request, reminder after a set number of days, second reminder, then a personal call. Write the messages once, in a friendly and neutral tone.
  3. Use the tools you have. Check whether your accounting, practice-management or customer system can send scheduled reminders and accept uploads or e-signatures through a link. Set it up for the most common item first.
  4. Reserve people for conversations. Agree that staff only step in after the automated sequence, and that their call is a conversation about what is getting in the way, not another reminder.
  5. Look at the persistent cases. Review monthly which customers ignore every reminder. Some need a different arrangement or a frank discussion led by the owner, not more chasing.

The business outcome

Documents and payments arrive more consistently, because reminders never depend on someone having time. Busy seasons are less chaotic. When the administrator is away, follow-up continues. Customer relationships improve, because contact from staff means help, not pressure.

The human dividend

The dividend is time and predictability, and with them a return of dignity. The administrator knows what each week of follow-up will look like, and spends their effort on the cases that need judgement. They are no longer the face of every reminder, and can be seen again as the knowledgeable professional they are.

The trade-offs

  • Automated messages can feel cold. Poorly worded reminders can irritate customers. Write them carefully and keep the tone human.
  • Some customers respond only to people. For certain clients, a personal call will always work better, and the sequence should allow for that.
  • Setup takes effort. Writing sequences and configuring tools takes time that may be hard to find in a busy season.
  • Reminders cannot fix everything. A customer who cannot or will not pay needs a business decision, not a better reminder.

Why it matters

People do not take jobs to become nags. When a business makes routine follow-up its own responsibility, carried by a schedule and not by a person's persistence, it protects something easily overlooked: the self-respect of the staff who deal with customers every day, and the goodwill between them.
