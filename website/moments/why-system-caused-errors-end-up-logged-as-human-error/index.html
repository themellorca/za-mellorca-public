Why Errors Caused by Systems Get Logged as Human Error

When a form, screen or handover invites a mistake, the log still names the person who made it. Why that happens, what it costs, and how to record causes honestly.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

An order ships to the wrong branch. A payment is captured twice. A customer receives a quote with last month's price. Somebody investigates, a form is opened, and a line near the bottom asks for the cause. The dropdown offers four choices, and one of them is "human error". It is quick, it is usually accepted without question, and it ends the conversation.

This piece looks at that small moment of logging, and at how often a cause that sits in the system gets recorded as a cause that sits in a person. It is a hypothesis drawn from general guidance on human failure, not a finding about any particular South African employer, and the example below is illustrative.

The cause box that closes the case

Error logs are written under pressure. The exception has already cost someone an hour, a customer is waiting, and the person filling in the form wants the matter closed. A category such as "human error" is easy to defend because it is almost always technically true: a person did press the button, or type the figure, or skip the step.

But "a person did it" and "a person caused it" are different statements. HSE's overview of managing human failures begins from the point that everyone can make errors, however well trained and motivated, and that human failure is not random. The same page describes factors that make errors more or less likely, among them poor design, distraction, time pressure, workload, competence, morale and communication systems. Those are conditions that a business creates, and a log that records only the final action leaves all of them out.

The result is a record that looks tidy and teaches nothing. Next month the same form invites the same slip, and the log shows another "human error", possibly with a different name beside it.

How a system invites the mistake

It helps to be specific about what "the system" means here. It is rarely a dramatic failure. More often it is ordinary design that makes the wrong action easy:

  • Look-alike choices. Two customer accounts with almost the same name, or two product codes that differ by one character, sit side by side in a drop-down list.
  • Silent defaults. A field opens pre-filled with yesterday's value, and nothing marks it as unconfirmed.
  • Missing checks. A figure can be entered in the wrong unit, or a date in the past, and the screen accepts it.
  • Rushed handovers. The note left by the previous shift omits the one detail the next person needed.
  • Workload. The task is done at the end of a long queue, with a target that rewards speed.

HSE's guidance prefers to control such risks by redesigning the task or equipment, not by reminding people to be careful. That preference only works if the log records the design problem in the first place.

What it does to the person

Being named as the cause has a cost beyond the single incident. Staff learn quickly which entries count against them. Some begin to hide near misses, because a near miss logged is a mark earned for nothing. Others become slow and over-cautious, double-checking everything by hand, which is its own form of lost time. The most capable are often the ones given the awkward tasks, so they collect the most entries and look the least reliable on paper.

HSE's stress guidance on demands asks employers to make sure that workloads are matched to people's skills and abilities, and that employees can cope with the demands of their jobs. Its guidance on role asks that the requirements placed on people are clear and compatible. When a person is blamed for an error that the job design made likely, both standards are quietly failing, and the log is where the failure is hidden. Our piece on why being blamed for system errors wears people down describes the feeling from the other side of the desk.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Gqeberha distributor that supplies hardware to about forty small shops. Orders are captured by two clerks from phone and WhatsApp messages. Over a quarter, eleven deliveries go to the wrong shop. Every entry in the incident log reads "captured wrong delivery address: human error", and the clerk who handles the busiest hours has seven of them.

The owner is about to have a conversation about care and attention. Before doing so, she asks someone to read the original orders. Three things emerge. Several shops trade under similar names in the same town, and the customer list shows only the name. The delivery address field carries over from the previous order when a clerk starts a new one. And the busiest hours coincide with the morning cut-off for same-day dispatch, when each clerk has a queue of messages and no time to confirm anything.

None of those findings excuses a careless entry, and a few may still be care problems. But the log, as written, would never have shown them. After the owner changes the list to show the town and the account number, clears the carried-over address, and moves the cut-off by thirty minutes, the wrong-branch deliveries become rare. The clerk with seven entries was not the problem.

What honest logging looks like

The aim is not to remove accountability. Some errors are lapses of care, and some are deliberate breaches of a rule; HSE separates errors from violations, and the two call for different responses. The aim is to find out which kind of event you are looking at before the log says. The UK National Cyber Security Centre's guidance on incident management makes a related point for security incidents: a structured process includes a review after the event, so that the organisation learns from it. The same habit applies to everyday operational errors.

A workable log asks three things after the action: what was the person trying to do, what about the task, screen, tool or timing made this outcome likely, and what would have caught it earlier. "Human error" can stay as a category, but it should be a starting point for those questions, not an answer to them.

The trade-offs

There are costs to this. Writing contributing conditions takes minutes that busy teams feel they do not have. A manager has to be willing to hear that their own process, target or staffing level is part of the cause, and that is harder than it sounds. In a very small business, with a handful of staff and one owner, there may be too few events to see a pattern. And some people will still make careless choices, which the log should be able to say plainly when the evidence supports it.

Practical next actions

  1. Pull the last twenty entries marked "human error" or similar and read the original circumstances for five of them. Note how many involved a confusing screen, a default, a handover gap or a rush.
  2. Add two questions to your error form: "What made this easy to get wrong?" and "What would have caught it earlier?" Keep the answers to a sentence each.
  3. Count entries by process or screen as well as by person. If one form produces most of the entries, fix the form before you speak to anyone.
  4. Tell your team in plain words that a logged near miss will not count against them, and then keep to that when the next one is reported.
  5. Choose one recurring error and change the task: show the account number beside the name, clear carried-over fields, or add a check for impossible values. Review the log in a month to see whether it stopped.

Where the cause is a missing step between teams, the same thinking applies to the one missing item that stops a larger process, and clear ownership of each fix helps; see why clear ownership gives people room to decide. Requests that vanish between people also produce errors that look personal, as in the request that disappears into a queue nobody can see.

The test of a good log is simple. A year from now, someone reading it should be able to see what changed in the system because of what went wrong, and should not find the same person's name beside the same kind of mistake.
