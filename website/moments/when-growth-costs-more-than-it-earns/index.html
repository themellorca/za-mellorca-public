When Growth Costs More Than It Earns

More customers can leave an owner with less cash and more hours. How to tell whether growth is paying for itself, and what to check before saying yes to the next big order.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-14

Revenue is up. The order book is full, the phone rings, the team is busy, and the owner is working harder than at any point since the first year. Yet the bank balance does not reflect it. Suppliers are being paid later, the overdraft is used more often, and the owner has quietly stopped taking a full salary. Growth has arrived, and so has a strange feeling that the business is getting poorer.

This piece looks at that pattern, in which growth consumes more than it earns, and at how an owner can notice it early. It is a hypothesis built from general financial principles and public tax guidance, not a finding about any particular South African business.

Why more revenue can mean less cash

Revenue is not cash, and cash is not profit. A business that grows has to pay for the growth before it is paid for it. Stock is bought before it is sold. Extra staff are hired before the extra work is invoiced. Customers who take sixty days to pay are funded, in effect, by the business that serves them. The faster the growth, the larger the gap between money going out and money coming in.

Several things tend to combine:

  • Costs arrive first. Wages, rent, fuel and supplier accounts are due on fixed dates, while customer payments arrive on theirs.
  • Prices were set for the old business. A price that covered costs at small volume may not cover the extra overtime, courier charges or discounts that volume brings.
  • The owner is the unpriced input. The extra hours the owner works to hold the growth together rarely appear in any cost line.
  • Obligations scale with size. More staff, more invoices and more turnover bring more compliance work and more money to set aside for the state.

Cash that passes through is not the business's own

The South African Revenue Service's pages on value-added tax explain that registered vendors charge VAT on their sales and pay it over to SARS. The money sits in the business account for a while, and it can look like a healthy balance, but it is owed. A growing business that spends VAT money on stock or wages in a busy month finds the bill waiting when the return is due.

The same applies to employee tax. SARS's guidance on pay-as-you-earn explains that employers deduct tax from employees' pay and pay it over. Each new hire therefore adds a monthly payment that is separate from the salary itself, and a cash forecast that counts only the net pay will fall short. Neither obligation is exotic. Both are routine, and both are easy to overlook in the excitement of a larger order book.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance on work-related stress identifies demands, control and support as core factors in how work affects people. An owner in a growth squeeze often faces high demands, little control over customers' payment timing and thin support, because there is no money to hire help. The financial strain and the personal strain feed each other.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Gqeberha company that makes and installs custom shopfittings. It wins a contract to fit out eight branches for a regional retailer. The contract is worth more than the company's whole previous year, and the owner says yes without hesitation.

To deliver, the company hires four installers, rents a second workshop and buys materials in bulk. The retailer pays sixty days after each branch is signed off. By branch five, the company has paid out three months of wages and rent and received only the first two payments. The owner covers the shortfall from a personal account, works Saturdays on site and misses the quote requests that normally keep the pipeline alive. At the end, the contract is complete and profitable on paper, but the company has no new work lined up, a tired owner and a smaller cash cushion than before it won.

The human tax: financial resilience

The cost falls on financial resilience: the ability to absorb a bad month without panic. A business operating on thin cash lives close to the edge, and every late payment, breakdown or illness becomes a crisis. For the owner this often extends into the household, because personal savings and security are tied to the business.

It is also a cost in choice. An owner who must take every order to keep the cash moving has lost the ability to decline. The business is no longer growing on the owner's terms. It is being pulled along by its own obligations. The note on the owner choosing growth on their own terms describes what the alternative looks like.

What could realistically change

The remedy is not to refuse growth. It is to price it, fund it and staff it with eyes open. An owner can work out the real margin on a job after all direct costs, including a fair rate for the owner's own time. They can forecast cash week by week for the length of a contract, not only the profit at the end. They can negotiate deposits or staged payments so that the customer shares the funding load. And they can set a rule that no single customer or contract may exceed a stated share of cash exposure.

The piece on why knowing your margins changes what you can choose explains how costing gives the owner the right to say no, and the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake describes the weekly picture that makes surprises rarer.

Practical next actions

  1. Cost the next big yes. Before accepting a large order, list every extra cost it will bring, including staff, stock, transport, financing and tax, and add a monthly figure for your own time.
  2. Forecast cash by week. Build a simple week-by-week view of money in and out for the length of the contract, with VAT and employee tax shown as separate lines that are not available to spend.
  3. Ask for a deposit or staged payments. Put the request into the quote, and agree payment dates in writing before work begins.
  4. Set an exposure limit. Decide the largest share of your cash or working capital that any one customer may tie up, and hold to it unless you have reviewed it deliberately.
  5. Review at the halfway point. Compare actual costs and cash against the forecast, and decide whether to adjust price, pace or scope while there is still room to act.

The business outcome

A business that checks whether growth pays for itself tends to take fewer bad contracts and to fund good ones more calmly. Its owner has a clearer view of which customers and products add cash and which merely add activity. Over time that clarity supports better prices, better terms and a team that is not stretched to cover a shortfall.

The human dividend

The dividend is financial breathing room. It means a cash buffer that survives a late payer, a household that is not carrying the business's risks, and the freedom to turn down work that does not add up. What the owner does with that room, whether it is steady expansion, a slower pace, a first proper holiday or a larger buffer, is for them to decide.

The trade-offs

  • Declining volume can cost a foothold. A large customer who is refused today may not come back, and some low-margin work builds a reputation worth having.
  • Costing takes time. A busy owner may feel they cannot spare the evening it takes, which is why the work often goes undone.
  • Buffers slow expansion. Holding cash back means growing more slowly than a competitor who borrows more, and that is a legitimate choice with real consequences.
  • Deposits can lose deals. Some customers will not agree to staged payments, and the owner must decide whether that customer is worth the exposure.

Why it matters

Growth is celebrated, and an owner who questions it can feel ungrateful. But a business exists to give its owner and its people a more secure life, not to be larger for its own sake. When growth costs more than it earns, the right response is to count the costs honestly, and then to choose the volume that the business can carry without borrowing against the people who run it.
