Relief from Friction

The friction of signing up for something at work

Enrolling, registering and requesting access at work is rarely one step. Where the friction comes from, who pays for it, and how to make sign-ups short.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-08

Signing up for something at work sounds like a small task. Enrolling in the company's retirement fund, registering as a supplier on a customer's portal, requesting access to a new tool, joining the medical aid scheme, submitting a vehicle allowance claim for the first time. Each is a single action in principle. In practice each can take an afternoon, three emails, two forms with overlapping questions and a call to someone who is on leave.

This piece is about that friction: where it comes from, who pays for it, and what a small business can do to make signing up for something the easy part of the process. It is a hypothesis about a common experience, grounded in general workplace research, not a measurement of any particular business.

What makes a sign-up feel heavy

Friction is rarely one big obstacle. It is the accumulation of small ones.

  • The same details asked again. ID number, address, bank details and employee number are typed in for the fifth time because the forms do not talk to each other.
  • Unclear next steps. The form is submitted, and then nothing. There is no confirmation, no indication of who has it, and no sense of how long it should take.
  • Supporting documents in the wrong format. The portal wants a PDF under a certain size, the document is a phone photo, and the person who can fix it is the person already behind on work.
  • Several people need to approve, in sequence. Each hand-off waits in someone's inbox.
  • Nobody owns the process. When it goes wrong, the person signing up has no one to ask, and the people who might know each assume it belongs to someone else.

Mellorca has described the wider version of this in collecting employee information every pay period, and in paper forms captured twice. The sign-up is where those costs meet the individual person.

What the wider evidence shows, and what it does not

No research measures sign-up friction in South African workplaces specifically, and the figures below should be read as context, not proof.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index reports that, in its Microsoft 365 data, people spent 57% of their time communicating and 43% creating, and that in its survey 64% said they struggled to have the time and energy to do their job. A sign-up that needs three emails and a chaser draws on exactly the time and energy that survey says is already short.

Government sources show that small businesses face real administrative change too. SARS's page for small businesses notes that from 1 April 2026 the compulsory VAT registration threshold rose from R1 million to R2.3 million, and that Turnover Tax is available to eligible businesses with a turnover of up to R2.3 million. Each of these is a registration decision with forms, eligibility checks and a login at the end of it. For a small owner, signing up for the right thing is itself a skill.

It is also worth remembering what a sign-up should ask for. The Protection of Personal Information Act, summarised on the South African Government site, rests on the idea that personal information processed for a purpose should be adequate, relevant and not excessive. A form that asks for everything "just in case" is not only irritating; it collects more than the purpose needs.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Pietermaritzburg firm of quantity surveyors with eleven staff. A new hire, a graduate surveyor, starts on the first of the month.

In her first week she is asked to complete a personal details form for payroll, a separate form for the retirement fund, a third for the medical aid scheme, a leave and attendance profile, and an IT request for access to the project software. Her name, ID number and bank details appear on all of them. The retirement fund form must be signed and scanned. The medical aid form has a cut-off date that nobody mentioned. The IT request goes to a manager who is on site and does not reply for four days.

The office administrator, who is trying to help, spends most of her week chasing the same person for the same missing information through three channels. By Friday the graduate has completed everything and has no idea which parts are finished, which are pending and which have quietly failed. None of this is anybody's fault. It is what happens when each sign-up was designed separately.

The human tax: time and energy

The obvious tax is time. A sign-up that should take fifteen minutes expands into a recurring interruption, spread across days.

The less obvious tax is energy. Sign-ups tend to happen at the start of something: a new job, a new client, a new tool. That is when people are trying to make a good impression and learn a great deal. Spending that attention on paperwork that could have been prefilled is a poor use of it. For new starters, it can also set a tone about how the organisation treats people's time.

The tax falls on the administrator as well, who becomes a human relay between forms and people. The article on the evening spent chasing signatures shows what that looks like at its worst, and the time tax of re-explaining the same thing shows its everyday version.

What could realistically change

The principle is to make each sign-up short for the person doing it, even if that means more up-front work for the person designing it.

  1. Ask once, reuse everywhere. Capture the core personal details a single time, in one place, and use them to prefill the rest.
  2. Remove questions that are not needed. For every field, ask what decision depends on it. If nothing does, delete it.
  3. Show the whole journey up front. A short checklist of what needs to be done, by whom and by when, turns a vague sense of obligation into a finite list.
  4. Confirm receipt and status. A simple acknowledgement, and a place to see what is pending, removes a surprising amount of chasing.
  5. Make forms usable. The W3C's accessibility tips for designers recommend, for example, marking required fields with a symbol as well as colour and making sure interactive elements can be reached by keyboard. Those same habits help every user, including someone filling in a form on a phone in a car park.
  6. Name an owner. One person responsible for the sign-up process, who can answer questions and fix what is broken.
  7. Offer an alternative route. For people without reliable data or a printer, a short conversation can be more efficient than a portal.

The business outcome

New starters, suppliers and customers reach productive work sooner. Administrators spend less time chasing. Fewer sign-ups lapse unnoticed, which reduces missed deadlines for benefits and registrations.

The human dividend

The dividend is time: minutes and attention returned to the person who would otherwise be on the phone about a form. It is small in any single case, but sign-ups recur, and a process that is easy the tenth time is one nobody has to think about.

The trade-offs

  • Prefilled forms can carry errors forward. One wrong detail now appears in several places, so the source needs to be checked.
  • Collecting once concentrates data. A single store of personal details is convenient, and also a larger responsibility under POPIA, so access needs to be controlled.
  • Some third parties will not change. A medical aid scheme or a customer portal may insist on its own form, however inconvenient.
  • Streamlining takes setup time. The first few sign-ups through a redesigned process may be slower than the old way.

Why it matters

Sign-ups are small, frequent and easy to dismiss, which is why friction in them goes unaddressed for years. Yet they are often the first experience of a process that a person has, and they set expectations about how the business works. A form that can be completed once, correctly, with a clear sense of what happens next is a modest thing, and a respectful one.
