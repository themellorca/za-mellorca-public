Why Purpose Gets Lost in Administrative Noise

People start businesses and choose careers for a reason that is rarely paperwork. How avoidable admin slowly crowds that reason out, and how to tell necessary administration from noise.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-07

Ask people why they started a business, or why they chose a particular kind of work, and the answers are rarely about administration. They talk about building things, teaching, caring for people, cooking, solving a technical problem, serving a community they know. The reason is usually specific, and often it is the thing they are proudest of.

Ask the same people how they spent last Tuesday, and a different picture can emerge. Chasing a signature. Re-sending a quote. Finding the latest version of a spreadsheet. Answering the same question from three people in three channels. Logging in to a portal to download a document to upload to another portal.

None of those tasks is unreasonable on its own. The problem is cumulative. For some people, there comes a point when the work they came to do is no longer the work that fills their days. This piece looks at that drift: how it happens, what it takes, and what can realistically be changed.

Necessary administration and administrative noise

It is worth separating two things that are often lumped together.

Necessary administration is work that the business, its customers or the law genuinely require. A South African small business with staff must register for PAYE, UIF and the Skills Development Levy, as SARS's guidance for small businesses sets out, and once turnover passes the compulsory threshold it must register for VAT. Records must be kept, staff paid correctly, contracts honoured. This is part of running a responsible business, and it would be wrong to treat it as an enemy of purpose.

Administrative noise is the extra work created by how that administration is organised: the re-keying, chasing, searching, reconciling and repeating that happens because information is in the wrong place or a process has no owner. The VAT return is necessary. Spending two evenings assembling the figures for it from four sources is noise.

What the wider evidence shows, and what it does not

Large-scale workplace research does not measure purpose directly, and it would be a stretch to claim it proves that administration destroys meaning. It does show the conditions in which the drift happens.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index found that, in its Microsoft 365 data, people spent 57% of their time communicating, through meetings, email and chat, and 43% creating. In the accompanying survey, 64% of respondents said they struggled to have the time and energy to do their job, and 68% said they did not have enough uninterrupted focus time.

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 report found that global employee engagement fell to 20% in 2025, with engagement among managers dropping more sharply. Gallup's measure covers many factors, and administrative load is not singled out as the cause.

Together these figures describe a working world in which much of the day goes on coordination rather than the core of the job, and in which many people do not feel connected to their work. The link between the two in any particular South African business is a hypothesis, not a finding. It is a plausible one, and it can be tested.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: an East London early childhood development centre run by a qualified practitioner who founded it eight years ago, with nine staff and around seventy children.

The founder started the centre to teach. Today, parent fee tracking lives in a spreadsheet that is reconciled by hand against bank statements. Staff leave is recorded in a notebook. Funding and compliance documents are kept in a mixture of email attachments, a desktop folder and printouts. Parents ask the same questions about fees, closures and events through WhatsApp, phone and the front gate.

In this scenario, the founder spends most mornings on the fees and messages and most afternoons on documents. Teaching happens when a staff member is absent. The founder still believes in the work. They simply rarely do it.

The human tax: attention and dignity

The first tax is attention. Noise is not just time-consuming; it is fragmenting. A day broken into twenty small administrative interruptions leaves little room for the kind of sustained thought that skilled work needs.

The second tax is closer to dignity, and it needs careful wording. For some people, the gap between the work they are capable of and the work they actually do can feel diminishing, a sense of being used below their purpose. Others will not experience it that way at all, and some genuinely enjoy administration. The concern is not admin as such; it is admin that has crowded out the thing a person came to do, without anyone deciding that it should.

What could realistically change

The interventions start with seeing the noise clearly, and only then choosing tools.

  • Keep a two-week log. Record what the person whose purpose is being crowded out actually spends time on. Mark each item as necessary, noise or core work. The proportions are often surprising.
  • Remove before you automate. Some noise disappears once a duplicate step, an unnecessary approval or a second copy of a record is removed.
  • Give recurring admin a home and an owner. Fee tracking, leave and compliance documents in one agreed place each, with a named person responsible, rather than spread across inboxes and notebooks.
  • Answer common questions once. A short published answer for the questions parents, customers or staff ask every week.
  • Automate the repetitive remainder. IBM describes workflow automation as replacing manual steps with software that executes all or part of a process, and stresses that success depends on good process design rather than technology alone. Bank feeds, reminders and simple forms can take over a great deal of predictable work.
  • Protect the core work deliberately. Put it in the diary. Recovered time that is not protected tends to be absorbed by the next layer of noise.

The first consequence, and the business outcome

The immediate change is that the founder, or the specialist, gets back blocks of time and attention that are not interrupted by avoidable admin. The business outcome is that its most distinctive capability, whatever people actually come to it for, receives more of its most capable people's time.

The human dividend

The dividend is time and attention returned to the work a person regards as theirs. For some that will be teaching, building or serving clients. For others it may be strategy, mentoring or simply leaving at a reasonable hour. The point is not to prescribe what the time is for. It is to make the choice possible again.

The trade-offs

  • Delegated admin is still someone's job. Moving noise from a founder to a junior colleague does not remove it; it may simply move the tax.
  • New systems create their own noise at first. Learning a tool, migrating records and fixing teething problems can make things worse for a while.
  • Some control is lost. A founder who handled everything personally may feel less connected to details they used to see.
  • Purpose cannot be engineered. Reducing noise creates room; it does not guarantee that the room will feel meaningful.

Why it matters

Most businesses exist because someone cared enough about a particular kind of work to build around it. When that work is slowly displaced by avoidable administration, the business can keep running while losing touch with its own reason for being.

Mellorca's interpretation is modest. Necessary administration is part of the job and deserves respect. Administrative noise is a design problem, and design problems can be worked on. Reducing the noise will not supply anyone's purpose, but it can stop burying it.
