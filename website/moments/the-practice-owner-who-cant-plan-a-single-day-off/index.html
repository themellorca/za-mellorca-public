The practice owner who can't plan a day off

For many practice owners, a day off is a risk assessment. Why absence feels impossible, what it costs, and how rules, checklists and shared access can restore the choice.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-10

A practice owner sits down in October to plan the year-end break. The diary shows patients booked into the second week of December. The practice manager is competent but has never run a month-end alone. The only person who knows how the medical aid claims are batched is the owner. The locum is booked, but nobody has explained the stock ordering. After forty minutes the owner closes the calendar and decides to look again next week. This happens most years.

This piece looks at that pattern: why a professional practice owner can find it nearly impossible to plan a single day off, what that costs, and what a business can do so that time away is a decision rather than a negotiation. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on control at work and key-person risk, not a finding about any one practice.

Why the owner of a practice is hard to replace

A practice, whether dental, veterinary, legal, accounting or medical, has a particular shape. The owner is usually the main producer of revenue, the clinical or professional lead, the employer and the person who understands how everything fits. Their name is on the door, and often on the registration or licence. That combination makes absence expensive in a way that is not true of a manager in a larger company.

It also makes absence hard to plan. The owner's knowledge is held in habit rather than in a document: which supplier gives credit, which patient needs a different tone, which bank account to move money from when payroll is tight. Each item is small. Together they make a day off feel like a risk.

The management literature calls this key-person or "bus factor" dependency: the number of people who would have to be unavailable before the work stops. In many small practices the number is one.

The human tax: autonomy and time

The first cost is time, and it is easy to understand. The owner works through days that would normally be rest, takes calls on leave and carries the practice in their head on the beach. The article on the price of never fully switching off on leave describes how that feels from the inside.

The second cost is autonomy, which is less obvious. Autonomy is usually thought of as the freedom to decide what to do. It also includes the freedom to decide not to. The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards for work-related stress treat control, meaning how much say people have over how they do their work, as one of the core conditions for healthy work. An owner who cannot choose when to be absent has lost a piece of that control, even though the business is theirs.

The World Health Organization's guidance on mental health at work lists excessive workloads, inflexible working hours and low control among risks to mental health. Owners are not exempt from those risks because they hold the title, and in a one-owner practice they are often more exposed.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a four-person veterinary practice in Pietermaritzburg. The owner-vet has not taken more than three consecutive days off in six years. The reasons, when she lists them, are specific. The booking system requires her login to override double-bookings. The pharmacy order is placed by phone because the wholesaler has never set up online ordering for the practice. The receptionist cannot approve a payment plan for a client in difficulty, and the practice has a rule that only the owner can authorise write-offs.

None of these is unreasonable by itself. Together they mean that any absence of more than a day generates a stream of messages. In the end she goes on a family trip, answers eleven calls from the car park of a game reserve, and comes home less rested than when she left. The next year she does not book the trip.

What actually stops the day off

When owners describe why they cannot leave, they often say "there is too much to hand over". It is more useful to break that into categories, because each needs a different fix:

  • Decisions only the owner can make. Approvals, exceptions, write-offs, prices.
  • Tasks only the owner knows how to do. Claims batching, month-end, ordering, payroll.
  • Access only the owner has. Logins, bank approvals, supplier accounts.
  • Relationships only the owner holds. The referring doctor, the key corporate client, the landlord.
  • Reassurance only the owner can give. The staff member who wants to hear that things are fine.

Most owners find that the first three can be fixed with design, and that the last two need time and deliberate introduction. The practical guide on how to know whether your business depends too much on one person is a good way to find out which categories apply.

What could realistically change

A day off does not require a perfect business. It requires a few things to be true for the specific days the owner wants to be away.

  1. Write down the decisions, with limits. Give the practice manager a clear rule, such as "approve write-offs up to R1,500 without asking". Rules are cheaper than being reachable. The piece on what real delegation requires from systems explains why delegation fails without visible limits.
  2. Rehearse a short absence. Pick one ordinary Friday and stay away. Note every message that arrives and what it was about. That list is the real handover document.
  3. Share access safely. Use a password manager or role-based permissions so that no single login is a bottleneck, and so that a trusted deputy can act in an emergency.
  4. Move recurring tasks off memory. Put month-end, ordering and claims steps in a checklist, and where possible automate the reminder. The guide on how to design a handoff that cannot depend on memory explains the principle.
  5. Agree what counts as an emergency. A clear definition, such as a safety incident, a bank problem or a legal notice, reduces the volume of calls and lets the owner trust the silence.

Where the worry is more serious, such as illness rather than leave, the article on the owner facing illness with no one who knows the systems covers the more urgent version of the same problem.

The business outcome

A practice that can run for a few days without the owner is better placed in several ways. Clients are served consistently, because the process does not depend on one person's availability. Staff grow, because they are trusted with real decisions within limits. The owner's own judgement improves, because rest does. The practice is also easier to value and sell, because a buyer is paying for a business that works, not for a person who holds it together.

For an owner thinking about the longer term, a practice that cannot spare its owner for a week is unlikely to be spared for a retirement.

The human dividend

The dividend is optionality and autonomy together: the ability to look at a calendar and choose a few days away, and to treat that choice as ordinary. It does not promise that the owner never works on leave, or that every holiday will be silent. It offers the more modest freedom of deciding for themselves, rather than having the practice decide for them.

The trade-offs

  • Delegation involves risk. Someone may make a decision the owner would not have made, and some of those will cost money.
  • Documentation takes time up front. The weeks spent writing down the practice are weeks the owner cannot spend seeing clients.
  • Professional rules may limit delegation. Clinical and legal responsibilities often cannot be handed to a non-professional, so some absences need a locum or a colleague.
  • Some owners like being needed. The practice may be part of their identity, and stepping away can be uncomfortable even when it is possible.
  • Costs of cover. A locum or an extra senior hire is a real expense.

Why it matters

The practice owner who cannot plan a day off is rarely failing at planning. They are running a business whose knowledge, access and decision rights sit with one person. Moving them into rules, checklists and shared access does not remove the owner from the practice. It gives the owner a choice that most employees take for granted.
