Why retirement plans depend on a business that runs without you

For many owners the business is the retirement plan. What it takes for a business to keep running when its owner steps back, and why that is the real retirement question.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-08

For many owners of small South African businesses, the business is the retirement plan. There may be a retirement annuity, a property or some savings alongside it, but the expectation is that the business will either keep paying an income or be sold for a sum that makes stopping possible. Both outcomes rest on the same assumption: that the business is worth something, or can keep producing something, when the owner is no longer in it every day.

This piece looks at why that assumption is fragile, what it means for the people involved, and what a business that "runs without you" actually consists of. It is a general discussion, not financial advice, and anyone planning their own retirement should speak to a qualified adviser.

The plan that hides inside the business

A retirement plan built on a business is different from one built on a pension fund. A fund pays out regardless of what the member does next. A business pays out only if it can be sold or can continue without the person who built it.

The difference shows up in a plain question: if the owner stepped away for six months, what would happen? For a business that depends on its founder's relationships, knowledge and daily decisions, the honest answer may be that revenue would fall and key staff or customers might leave. A buyer would see the same thing and price it in, if they were willing to buy at all. Wikipedia's description of succession planning frames it as a process for identifying and developing people who can take over leadership roles when they become vacant, and notes that business exit planning is a distinct but related discipline.

The dependency is often invisible to the owner, because it grew gradually. The owner does the quoting because they always did. They know which customer needs a call, which supplier will extend terms, and which of the three bank accounts has the VAT money. Each piece of knowledge is small. Together they form a business that quietly requires one particular person.

Why this is a human matter, not only a financial one

It is tempting to treat owner dependency as a valuation problem. For the people involved it is more personal.

  • The owner may feel unable to stop. A retirement date that depends on finding a buyer, or on the business running itself, can recede year after year.
  • The owner's family may share the financial exposure without having a say in how it is managed, or may be expected to step in if the owner falls ill.
  • Long-serving staff have their own futures tied to the business, and may feel uncertain about who will lead it and whether their roles will survive a sale.

The tax that this places on a person is a loss of optionality: the range of ways in which they can choose to spend the later part of their working life narrows to those that the business allows. A planned retirement is one of the clearest examples of choice, and it is the one that most depends on whether the business has been made independent of its owner. The related experience of unplanned absence is described in Mellorca's article on the owner facing illness with no one who knows the systems.

What "runs without you" actually requires

The phrase is easy to say and hard to define. It does not mean a business that is unattended or an owner who has no role. It means that the essential work continues, decisions are made at the right level, and the knowledge needed to operate is held by more than one person.

The idea has a well-known informal name in technology teams: the "bus factor", described in Wikipedia's entry on the bus factor as the number of team members who would have to be lost before a project stalls. A bus factor of one is a useful way to describe a business that depends on a single person, and it applies well beyond software.

In operational terms, a business that can run without its owner generally has most of the following:

  • Documented processes. The routine work, from invoicing to ordering, is written down well enough for someone else to follow it.
  • Shared access and records. Bank, accounting, supplier and customer information is held in systems that more than one trusted person can reach, with sensible controls, not in one person's head, phone or inbox.
  • Delegated decisions with limits. Managers can approve routine matters up to an agreed value without waiting. Mellorca's piece on the manager who can't act without the founder shows the opposite, and what real delegation requires from systems sets out what is needed.
  • Relationships held by more than one person. Key customers and suppliers know at least one other contact, and that contact is able to make commitments.
  • Reliable numbers. A buyer, a successor or a lender will want to see clear figures. A business that cannot produce them without the owner's involvement is harder to hand over.
  • A named successor or plan. Whether a family member, a manager, a buyer or a staff buy-out, someone is being prepared, not merely hoped for.

Mellorca's analysis of key-person operational dependency covers the operational cost of the problem in more detail.

The formal protections, and what they cannot do

There are financial tools designed to soften the risk. Key person insurance, described in Wikipedia's entry on key person insurance, is a policy a business takes out on someone whose loss would significantly harm it, with the business as beneficiary. A buy-and-sell agreement, set out in Wikipedia's overview of the buy-sell agreement, is a contract that governs what happens to an owner's share if they die, leave or become incapacitated.

These instruments can help, particularly with the money, and the details differ by country and circumstance, so professional advice from an attorney or financial adviser is essential. What they cannot do is make the business capable of operating without the person. Insurance can replace some lost income; it cannot replace the knowledge or the relationships. That part has to be built.

An illustrative scenario

Consider a hypothetical example, not a real business: a Port Elizabeth company that supplies and repairs industrial pumps, run by its founder, who is sixty-one and hopes to retire at sixty-five. There are twelve staff. The founder writes every quote, knows the three largest customers personally, and is the only person who understands how the pricing was built.

A friend who is also an owner asks a plain question at a braai: what would you do if you had to take six months off? The founder realises that the honest answer is that the business would stagnate, and that any buyer would see the same.

Over the next two years the founder makes a series of unglamorous changes. The pricing method is written down with the sales manager, who starts producing quotes for smaller jobs. A second person is introduced to each of the three large customers. Supplier and customer records move out of the founder's inbox into a shared system. A monthly report is produced by the bookkeeper rather than assembled by the founder. But at sixty-three, the founder takes a month away and the business carries on. That month changes the conversation about retirement from a hope into a plan with a date.

The human dividend

The dividend is optionality. A business that does not need its owner every day gives the owner a real choice: to retire at a chosen time, to step back gradually, to sell on better terms, to pass the business on, or simply to stay because they want to rather than because they must. It also gives staff and customers some assurance that the business has a future beyond one person.

The trade-offs

  • Letting go is hard. Many owners are tied to the business by identity as well as money. Mellorca's discussion of the founder whose identity is fused with every task explores this.
  • It takes years. Documentation, delegation and relationship-sharing are slow, and the time needed is easy to underestimate.
  • Delegation carries risk. Others will make decisions the owner would not have made, and some will be mistakes.
  • Sharing knowledge shares power. Staff who gain access to more of the business may use the position well or leave with it.
  • It may not raise the sale price. Valuation depends on many things, and independence from the owner is only one of them.

Why it matters

If the business is the retirement plan, then the question that matters is not only how much it is worth today, but whether it can carry on when its owner steps back. Making that true is mostly ordinary operational work: writing things down, sharing access, delegating properly and introducing people to each other. None of it is guaranteed to produce a comfortable retirement, but it moves the option from the realm of hope into the realm of choice.
