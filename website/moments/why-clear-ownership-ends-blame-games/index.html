Why Clear Ownership Ends Blame Games

When an approval stalls or a mistake slips through, the first question is often who is to blame. Why unclear ownership breeds blame, and how naming owners restores calm and dignity.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-16

A customer order goes out at the wrong price. The quote was approved, but by whom? Sales says finance signed it off. Finance says it only checks credit, not prices. The production manager says he just builds what the order says. By the end of the week, three people have spent hours defending themselves, the customer is still waiting for a corrected invoice, and the same gap is still open for next month.

This piece looks at how unclear ownership turns ordinary mistakes into blame games, and how naming clear owners for approvals and recurring steps restores calm. It is a hypothesis drawn from human-factors and workplace-stress guidance and from approval-tool documentation, not a study of any particular organisation.

How blame fills the gap

When a step has a clear owner, a problem with that step has an obvious first stop. The owner looks at it, fixes it, and if needed changes the process. When nobody owns the step, the problem has nowhere to go. People start asking who should have caught it, and that question quickly becomes who is to blame.

Approvals are especially prone to this. An approval chain looks like ownership, because several people have signed. In practice, each signer may assume the others checked the important part. The result is a chain of signatures with no one actually responsible for the thing that went wrong.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance on human failure treats errors as normal and predictable, and argues that investigations should ask why a failure happened rather than stopping at operator error. It recommends designing systems and tasks that tolerate error. Unclear ownership is precisely the kind of system condition that makes failure likely and then disguises its cause.

An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a small furniture manufacturer in Gqeberha with thirty staff. Price changes on custom orders are approved by email. Sometimes the sales lead approves, sometimes the owner, sometimes the finance clerk if the others are out. Nobody wrote down who should approve what.

When an order is under-priced, the conversation turns to who replied to which email. The finance clerk, who is the most junior, ends up feeling responsible because her name is on the last message, though she was never told prices were her job. She becomes cautious, starts refusing to reply to approval emails, and the approvals slow down further.

The human tax: confidence and dignity

The tax is confidence. People who expect to be blamed for gaps they did not own become defensive. They keep personal records of who said what, copy more people into emails and avoid taking initiative. The story why being blamed for system errors wears people down explores what this does to people over time.

The tax is also dignity. Being held responsible for something you were never given to own is a quiet humiliation, especially when it falls on the least senior person in the room. The HSE standard on role expects organisations to make requirements clear and to provide ways for employees to raise uncertainty or conflict in their roles. Ownership is how clarity becomes real.

What clear ownership looks like

Clear ownership is not a grand responsibility matrix. In a small business it is usually a simple list: each recurring decision or step, who owns it, who covers when that person is away, and what the owner is allowed to decide alone. The related piece why clear ownership gives people room to decide looks at the freedom this creates for the owner of a task.

Approval tools can help make ownership visible. Microsoft's documentation on Power Automate approvals describes sending requests to named approvers, choosing whether everyone must approve, whether the first response is enough, or whether approvals happen in sequence, and keeping a history of responses. The value is less the automation than the forced clarity: you cannot configure an approval without deciding who approves and what their approval means.

Ownership must come with authority and time. Naming someone as owner of a step they cannot control just relocates the blame. The story why being accountable without authority is exhausting makes this point directly.

Practical next actions

  1. List recurring decisions. Write down the approvals and checks that happen at least monthly, such as price changes, refunds, purchases and payments.
  2. Name one owner per item. For each, name a single owner and a cover person, and state what the owner's approval actually confirms.
  3. Match authority to ownership. Make sure each owner has the access, limits and time to do what they own.
  4. Route approvals to the owner. Use a simple approval tool or shared rule so requests reach the right person, with a record.
  5. Change the first question. When something goes wrong, ask what in the process allowed it, before asking who did it.
  6. Review after incidents. After a mistake, check whether the ownership list needs updating, and update it.

The business outcome

Clear ownership shortens the time between a problem appearing and being fixed, because there is no debate about who should act. Approvals move faster when approvers know exactly what they are confirming. Fewer hours are spent on defensive emails and reconstruction of events. And mistakes become a source of learning, because people are willing to talk about them.

The human dividend

The dividend is predictability. People know what is theirs, and what is not. A junior clerk who is not named as the owner of pricing no longer worries that every under-priced order will land on her. An owner who is named knows their decisions will be respected. That certainty makes work calmer, and it makes honesty about mistakes safer.

The trade-offs

  • Pressure can concentrate. A named owner may feel exposed; ownership must be paired with support.
  • Ownership without authority is unfair. Naming someone without giving them the means to act makes things worse.
  • Rigid lines can discourage help. People may stop helping with tasks that are not theirs, so cooperation should still be encouraged.
  • Lists need upkeep. When people leave or roles change, the ownership list must be updated or it becomes another source of confusion.

Why it matters

Blame is what organisations do when they have not decided who owns what. It wastes time and wears people down, often the least senior first. Naming owners, and giving them what they need, turns arguments about the past into work on the problem. It is a small act of organisation with a large effect on how fair work feels.
