The Friction of Working Across Countries and Time Zones

Working with clients or colleagues abroad stretches the day at both ends: early calls, late replies, holidays that do not line up. How to keep cross-border work from eating every evening.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-15

A Cape Town software consultancy wins a contract with a client in Toronto. It is good news. Within a month, the project lead's day has a new shape: client calls at four in the afternoon that run into the evening, messages arriving overnight that need answers before the client wakes, and a colleague in Nairobi whose working week does not quite match either. The contract pays well. The lead's evenings have quietly become part of it.

This piece looks at the everyday friction of working across countries and time zones, a growing reality for South African businesses that export services or work with partners abroad. It is a hypothesis built from labour law, public holiday rules and time-keeping standards, not a study of any specific firm.

Where the friction comes from

Time differences are the obvious source, but not the only one. Cross-border work involves several mismatches at once:

  • Overlapping hours are few. With a six- or seven-hour difference, the shared working window may be two hours, and every meeting competes for them.
  • Clocks move. South Africa does not use daylight saving, but many trading partners do, so the gap changes twice a year, often on different dates in different countries.
  • Holidays do not line up. South Africa's public holidays, set under the Public Holidays Act, include days such as Freedom Day and Heritage Day that clients abroad may not know about, and a public holiday falling on a Sunday moves to the Monday.
  • Weekends can differ. Some partners work different days of the week, so the overlap shrinks further.
  • Travel compresses everything. Visits abroad mean long journeys, jet lag and catching up on missed local work on return.

Time rules are less fixed than people assume. The Time Zone Database maintained under IANA, which most software relies on to calculate local time, is updated regularly because governments change offsets and daylight-saving rules. Calendar software usually handles this correctly, but people's mental arithmetic does not, which is how a meeting ends up an hour off.

The human tax: time and energy

The time cost is that the working day stretches at one or both ends. An early call with a partner in Asia and a late call with a client in North America can bracket the same day. The energy cost comes from never quite being off: the phone at dinner shows messages from people who are in the middle of their working day.

Presence suffers too. The record names presence as a dividend, and its absence is felt at home. A parent on a call at bath time, or a partner who checks messages during the evening because the client is awake, is physically present but not fully there. The story when the working day has no natural end describes that drift in general terms.

South Africa's Basic Conditions of Employment Act sets minimum standards on working time and overtime for covered employees. Cross-border work does not suspend those standards, and a business that relies on staff routinely working into the evening should look at whether its arrangements still respect them.

What could realistically change

Much of the friction can be reduced by design rather than endurance. Agreeing a fixed overlap window with each client, and protecting it for meetings, keeps calls from spreading across the day. Rotating inconvenient meeting times, so the burden does not always fall on the South African side, is fairer. Shifting some working hours, such as starting later on days with an evening call, prevents a longer day rather than simply adding to it.

Written updates reduce the need for meetings at all. A short daily or twice-weekly status note, shared in a place the client can read in their own morning, replaces many calls. The story the meeting that could have been a shared record explores this. Sharing a calendar of South African public holidays with clients at the start of the year avoids surprises.

Travel needs similar care. Building recovery time after long-haul trips, rather than returning straight to a full diary, prevents the pattern described in when travel days become catch-up days.

Practical next actions

  1. Map the overlap. For each client or partner abroad, write down the hours that fall within both sides' normal working day, including how daylight-saving changes affect it.
  2. Agree a meeting window. Ask clients to schedule calls within the overlap, and propose rotating any meetings that must fall outside it.
  3. Adjust hours, don't add them. Where an evening call is unavoidable, let the person start later that day.
  4. Replace some calls with written updates. Introduce a short status note that clients can read in their own morning.
  5. Share your holidays. Send clients a list of South African public holidays for the year and plan deliveries around them.
  6. Set response expectations. Agree with clients which messages need a same-day reply and which can wait for the next working day.

The business outcome

Clear overlap windows and written updates make cross-border projects more predictable, which clients appreciate. Fewer meetings fall outside working hours, reducing fatigue-related errors and the risk of losing experienced staff to burnout. The business also builds a reputation for being easy to work with across time zones, which supports winning further international work.

The human dividend

The dividend is time and presence. A project lead whose evening calls are limited, rotated and offset by later starts can be at the dinner table with their attention intact on most nights. What they do with that evening is their own choice, whether family, rest, study or simply quiet. The point is that the international contract no longer claims it by default.

The trade-offs

  • Clients may resist. Some will expect the supplier to fit their hours, and pushing back carries commercial risk.
  • Later starts disrupt local work. Shifting hours can make the person less available to colleagues in South Africa.
  • Written updates take discipline. A status note only replaces a meeting if it is clear, timely and read.
  • Some work is time-critical. Launches, incidents and deadlines will still require out-of-hours effort, which should be the exception rather than the norm.

Why it matters

International work is an opportunity for South African businesses, and many people enjoy its variety. The friction it brings is not a reason to avoid it, but it is a reason to design for it. Agreeing windows, writing things down and sharing the inconvenience fairly lets a business grow abroad without asking its people to give up their evenings to do it.
