Why every new hire starts with a week of waiting

Laptops, logins and approvals that arrive late turn a new hire's first week into waiting. Why it happens, what it costs, and how to give the start of a job an owner.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-09

A new employee arrives on Monday, bright and ready. The laptop is not there yet. The email address has not been created. The person who was meant to show them around is on leave, and nobody is certain who has the authority to approve the system access they need. By Wednesday they have read the staff handbook twice and learned where the kettle is. By Friday they have done an hour or two of real work. The business has paid for a week it did not use, and the new hire has formed their first impression of how it runs.

This piece looks at that first week: why waiting is so common, what it costs the person and the business, and what would make the start of a job feel like the start of a job. It is a hypothesis about a recurring pattern in small and mid-sized businesses, not a claim about any one employer.

Why the wait happens

Nobody plans a week of waiting. It accumulates from small dependencies, each reasonable by itself.

  • Preparation starts too late. The offer is accepted, the start date is set, and the request for a laptop, a login and a desk goes to whoever remembers on the Friday before.
  • Several people hold one piece each. HR holds the contract, finance holds the payroll set-up, IT or an outside provider holds the accounts, and the line manager holds the first assignments. None of them can see what the others have done.
  • Absences break the chain. When the one person who knows how to do a step is away, the step simply does not happen. The article on handing over an undocumented job during parental leave describes the same fragility from the other side.
  • Nobody owns the whole sequence. Each task has an owner, but the experience of the new person has none.

Mellorca has described the cost side of this in its work on new employees without equipment and on repeating HR administration for every new hire. This piece concentrates on how the delay feels to the person who has just joined.

What the research says about onboarding

Gallup reports that only 12% of employees strongly agree that their organisation does a great job of onboarding new people. The same article links onboarding to retention, noting that a poor early experience can weaken the connection a new employee formed during recruitment. It also cites the Society for Human Resource Management on turnover being highest in the first months of employment. These are largely US findings and should not be read as South African measures, but the underlying logic travels well: the first days set expectations for everything after.

The first week also coincides with the period in which a person is most uncertain. Gallup's burnout research lists unclear expectations and lack of manager communication among the main drivers of exhaustion. A new employee who has been given nothing to do, and no clear sense of what is expected of them, is in exactly that position, only earlier than usual.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Pretoria logistics company of about forty staff hires a new bookkeeper. The offer is signed three weeks before the start date. HR emails the finance manager and IT provider with the start date, but the finance manager is travelling and the IT provider treats the email as informational.

On the first Monday there is a desk and no machine. The bookkeeper is lent a spare laptop belonging to a colleague on leave, which has no accounting software installed. The licence has to be purchased. The bank profile requires a signed mandate from a director who is on site on Thursday. The tax software needs an administrator to add a user. The first week produces one afternoon of real work, mostly shadowing.

No single person has failed. The sequence has no owner, no checklist and no one who can see it as a whole. The bookkeeper, meanwhile, has started to wonder whether they have joined a business that is as disorganised as it appears.

The human tax: time and confidence

The first cost is time, both the business's and the employee's. The business pays a salary for days when output is near zero, and the colleague who ends up improvising a solution loses time from their own work. The employee may be unsure whether to ask questions or stay out of the way.

The second is confidence. A new hire in a new job is already working out whether they made the right decision. When the first week suggests that the business cannot organise a laptop, it is natural to wonder what else it cannot organise, such as pay, support or career progress. Some people will shrug this off; others will quietly begin looking elsewhere. Either way, the business has spent its first and best chance to show that it is a good place to work.

Processes that are written down also help people settle in. The Moments piece on why documented processes help people grow into roles argues that clear procedures let newcomers act with some independence sooner.

What could realistically change

The fix is a sequence with an owner, not a larger welcome pack.

  1. Start the clock at acceptance. Treat the signed offer as the trigger for a short checklist: equipment ordered, accounts requested, access approvals scheduled, first-week plan drafted, a named buddy confirmed.
  2. Name one owner for the whole experience. This is usually the line manager or an HR coordinator. Their job is not to do every task, but to see that each one is done by the first morning.
  3. Make the status visible. A simple shared list showing what is done, pending and blocked lets anyone see where things stand. The principles in designing a handoff that cannot depend on memory apply directly.
  4. Prepare for absences. Every step on the checklist should have a named backup, and the approvals that need a director should be requested ahead of time.
  5. Plan real work for the first days. Even if systems are late, a small, genuine task, a conversation with a customer, or a walk through the process the person will own, gives the week purpose.
  6. Handle personal information properly. The new hire's ID, bank details and tax numbers are personal information under POPIA, so collect them once, store them securely and limit who sees them.

The broader point about documentation is covered in what business processes need documentation first; onboarding is a strong candidate because it repeats every time someone joins.

The business outcome

New staff reach productive work sooner. Colleagues are interrupted less. The business learns which of its steps are slow, because the checklist exposes them, and every later hire benefits from the same fixes. The outcome is also less tangible: a new employee's early sense that the business is competent and expects them to succeed.

The human dividend

The dividend is time and predictability. For the new person, it means arriving to a desk, a login and a plan, and knowing roughly what the first month will look like. For the colleagues who help them, it means not having to improvise. Different people want different things from a first week, and some will prefer to be left to find their own way; the aim is to remove avoidable obstacles, not to schedule every hour.

The trade-offs

  • Preparation takes effort before the person arrives. Someone has to spend time on a role whose holder is not yet there.
  • Early plans can be wrong. A hire may start with a different priority from the one planned, and the checklist should flex.
  • Small teams have limited slack. Where one person is HR, finance and operations at once, a long checklist may itself become a burden.
  • Over-structured first weeks can feel impersonal. A balance between process and human welcome is needed.

Why it matters

The first week of a job is the cheapest opportunity a business has to demonstrate that it is organised and that it values the person it chose. A checklist, an owner and a backup for each step cost little. A week of waiting costs more than it appears to, and the bill arrives in confidence and goodwill.
