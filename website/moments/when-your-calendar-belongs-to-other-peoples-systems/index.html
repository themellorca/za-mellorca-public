When your calendar belongs to other people's systems

Open slots get filled by booking links, recurring invites and scheduling tools that only see that you are free. What it means when your calendar is arranged by other people's systems, and how to take some of it back.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-04

On Monday morning the week looks reasonable. By Wednesday it is full. A customer used the booking link to take the Thursday slot that was meant for writing a proposal. A supplier's system sent a recurring monthly review that landed on top of the team check-in. Two colleagues used the scheduling assistant, saw white space on Friday afternoon, and filled it. None of them did anything wrong. Each saw a free slot and took it.

The person whose calendar it is never decided any of this. Their week was arranged, one reasonable invitation at a time, by other people's systems.

The scene is illustrative. This piece is about what happens when the calendar, which looks like a personal tool, becomes something that mainly other people and other systems write in.

Systems that only know "free" and "busy"

Modern scheduling tools are genuinely useful. Microsoft's documentation for Bookings, for example, describes booking pages that let customers, clients or colleagues see availability and book a time, removing the back and forth of arranging appointments, with staff availability and business hours configured by the organisation. Outlook lets people set their working hours and location, and the scheduling assistant shows that information to organisers planning meetings. Other calendar products work in similar ways.

These tools are built on a simple model: a slot is either free or busy. A slot with nothing in it is free. That model is efficient for the person booking. It is less kind to the person being booked, because the time they meant for thinking, preparing or finishing work usually has nothing in it, so it looks free.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index analysis of anonymised Microsoft 365 activity found that half of all meetings in its data take place between 9 and 11 in the morning and between 1 and 3 in the afternoon, and that 57% of meetings were ad hoc calls without a calendar invitation. These are global figures from one vendor's data, not a measure of South African businesses. They suggest two pressures at once: scheduled meetings cluster in the same hours, and much of the remaining time is taken by unscheduled calls.

How the calendar slips out of your hands

Consider an illustrative case: an East London accounting practice with about 22 staff. Clients book consultations through an online link that shows every open hour from eight to five. Partners and senior staff accept internal invitations by default. Software vendors and the practice's bank send their own recurring check-ins. The managing partner spends evenings on the client work that should have happened in the day, because the day was booked.

  • Booking pages expose every open hour. Unless limits are set, a booking link offers all working time, including the hours meant for focused work.
  • Working hours are left at the default. If the calendar says eight to five, schedulers will use eight to five, including the part-time staff member who leaves at two.
  • Recurring invites outlive their purpose. A weekly meeting set up for a project that ended months ago still arrives every week.
  • Accepting is easier than declining. A one-click accept is the path of least resistance, and declining can feel uncooperative.

The waiting and sequencing side of lost control is covered in when the system decides what you can do today, and what happens to attention inside crowded meetings in when screens sit between colleagues who sit together. The administrative cost of booking by hand, which these tools rightly reduce, is described in the cost of manual appointment scheduling.

The human tax: autonomy and time

The main cost is autonomy. A calendar is one of the few places where a person can see and shape their working week. When most of it is filled by others, the person stops deciding what their week is for and starts reacting to it. Work that needs a long, quiet stretch, such as a proposal, an analysis, or a difficult conversation prepared properly, gets pushed to the edges of the day.

The time cost follows. If focused work cannot happen in working hours, it tends to happen before or after them. Not everyone experiences this as a loss: in some roles, being available is the job, and a full calendar is a sign of a healthy business. Mellorca does not assume any particular reaction. The point is that the shape of the week is often set by default settings rather than by choice.

What the evidence suggests about taking time back

MIT Sloan Management Review reported research on 76 large companies, each with more than 1,000 employees, that introduced between one and five meeting-free days a week. Employees reported more autonomy, cooperation, engagement and satisfaction, higher productivity, and less stress and micromanagement. The best overall results were reported in companies with three meeting-free days. These were large multinational firms, and a small practice cannot simply copy the arrangement. The research does show that reducing meetings deliberately did not, in those firms, damage the work, and that people valued the control it gave them.

What could change

  1. Set real working hours. Enter the hours you actually work in the calendar, including part-time patterns, so schedulers see the truth.
  2. Put focus time in the calendar. Block recurring periods for focused work and mark them busy. A protected block is visible to every system that reads the calendar.
  3. Limit what booking pages offer. Offer clients defined slots, with buffers between appointments, rather than every open hour.
  4. Audit recurring meetings each quarter. Cancel or shorten any that no longer have a purpose, and ask external parties to do the same.
  5. Agree team norms. For example, one meeting-light morning a week, and a shared understanding that declining an invitation with a reason is acceptable.

In the illustrative practice, booking slots limited to three mornings and two afternoons, protected client-work blocks, and a quarterly cull of recurring meetings might move most of the managing partner's evening work back into the day.

Trade-offs

  • Less availability for others. Clients and colleagues may wait longer for a meeting. For some roles and some clients, that cost is real.
  • Rules need upkeep. Booking limits and focus blocks must be adjusted as work changes, or they become another rigid default.
  • Visibility cuts both ways. Publishing working hours and locations helps schedulers, but some people experience it as monitoring, even where the software states it is not used for tracking.
  • Some meetings are the work. For sales, service and leadership roles, a busy calendar may be appropriate. The aim is choice, not emptiness.

Why it matters

The business case for calendar discipline is better use of skilled time and fewer evenings spent catching up. The human case is about who decides how a week is spent. When working hours, booking rules and focus time are set deliberately, other people's systems fit around the person rather than the other way round.

That is the dividend: a calendar that belongs to its owner again, and a working week that can be shaped rather than merely survived.
