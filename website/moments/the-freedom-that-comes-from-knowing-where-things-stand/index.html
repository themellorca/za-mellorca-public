The freedom that comes from knowing where things stand

Permission to leave early means little if you cannot tell whether the work is on track. How uncertainty about the state of work keeps people in place, and how a single, current view of work returns real choice.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-07

It is three o'clock on a Thursday and a team leader would like to leave early for a child's school event. The work is probably fine. The jobs due tomorrow are probably on track. The supplier delivery probably arrived. But "probably" is the problem. Without a way to check, the safest choice is to stay, walk the floor, phone a few people and leave once the uncertainty has been settled. By then the event is over.

This situation is rarely described as a systems problem. It is usually described as being conscientious, or as the nature of the job. But a lot of what keeps people tethered to work is not the work itself. It is not knowing where the work stands.

This piece is about the reverse of that: the particular kind of freedom that appears when the state of things can be seen at a glance.

Visibility as a precondition for choice

Autonomy is often described as having permission to decide. But permission is not enough on its own. A person who is allowed to leave early, reorganise their week or take a day off still needs to know whether doing so will leave something undone. Without that knowledge, the permission is mostly theoretical.

Usability research has said something similar about software for decades. The first of Jakob Nielsen's widely used usability heuristics, published by Nielsen Norman Group, is visibility of system status: a design should keep users informed about what is going on, through appropriate feedback within a reasonable time. The same idea applies to the business as a whole. If people cannot see what is going on, they have to find out by asking, checking or staying.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standards for work-related stress include a standard on role, which sets as a goal that the organisation provides information to enable employees to understand their role and responsibilities, and has systems to raise uncertainties. Its standard on demands focuses on achievable demands in relation to agreed hours of work. Uncertainty about whether work is on track is one way that demands quietly spill beyond those hours.

How uncertainty keeps people in place

In many small and mid-sized businesses, the state of work lives in several places at once: a job list in one system, messages in a group chat, a whiteboard in the workshop, and the memory of whoever spoke to the customer last. Knowing where things stand means assembling a picture from all of them. Some common effects:

  • People check in person. Walking around and asking is reliable, but it requires being there.
  • People check repeatedly. Without a trustworthy view, the same questions are asked several times a day, by phone or message, even outside working hours.
  • People hold information in their heads. The team leader becomes the only one who knows the full picture, which makes it harder to hand over.
  • Reports arrive too late to help. A weekly summary tells people where things stood, not where they stand.

Mellorca has explored the related costs in managers chasing status updates and the distinction in having data versus having visibility. The human side is that uncertainty narrows choices. When you cannot see, you stay.

The human tax

The tax here is autonomy and predictability. People give up time they could have used differently, not because the work demanded it, but because they could not confirm that it did not. Some will feel this as low-level anxiety; others as a habit they no longer notice. It would be wrong to claim a single experience. But the practical effect is consistent: the boundary of the working day is set by uncertainty rather than by the work.

An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative case: a Bloemfontein facilities maintenance contractor with about twenty-five staff. Jobs are logged in a booking system, but technicians report completion by WhatsApp, parts orders are tracked in a separate spreadsheet, and the operations supervisor reconciles all three in their head.

In this scenario, the supervisor rarely leaves before the last technician has messaged in, and checks the phone through the evening. When the business changes so that technicians close jobs in the booking system from their phones, with parts status linked to each job, the supervisor gains a single list showing what is done, what is late and what is waiting for parts. On most days, a two-minute look at that list is enough to know whether leaving at four is safe.

What could realistically change

  1. Decide where the truth lives. For each kind of work, agree on one place where its status is recorded. Other channels can remain for conversation, but not for status.
  2. Record status at the point of work. The person doing the job updates it as part of finishing, not later from memory.
  3. Show exceptions, not everything. A view that highlights only what is late or blocked is quicker to read than a full list.
  4. Be honest about freshness. Reporting tools show data only as current as their last update. Microsoft's documentation for Power BI notes that models on shared capacity can be refreshed on a schedule up to eight times a day. A view that refreshes overnight is not a view of now, and people should know which they are looking at.
  5. Make the view available to the people who carry the worry. Visibility helps most when it reaches the person who would otherwise stay late to check.

What changes first, and the human dividend

The first change is a shorter question. "Is everything on track?" becomes something answered by looking rather than asking. Fewer calls and messages are needed to confirm the obvious. The business outcome is earlier warning of genuine problems, because they stand out from the work that is fine.

The human dividend is the freedom the title refers to. Knowing where things stand makes it possible to choose: to leave on time, to take an afternoon, to focus on something else without a background question running. It also helps the people doing the work, who are asked "where are we on this?" less often. Mellorca has looked at the wider experience of waiting for information; this is the other side of the same coin.

The trade-offs

  • Recording status is work. Asking people to update a system as they go takes time, and it falls on those already doing the job. If the update is fiddly, it will be skipped.
  • Visibility can feel like surveillance. A board that shows every task and its owner can make people feel watched, especially if it is used to judge rather than to coordinate.
  • A clear view can raise expectations. If the owner can see everything at any hour, some may feel entitled to ask about it at any hour.
  • A stale view is worse than none. If people come to trust a status that has not been updated, they may leave believing everything is fine when it is not.

Why it matters

Much of what keeps people at work late, or checking messages at home, is not a task. It is a question without an easy answer. Mellorca's reading is that a single, trustworthy, current view of work is one of the less glamorous ways a business can return choice to its people. The change is operational. The effect is personal: being able to walk out of the door and know, rather than hope, that things are where they should be.
