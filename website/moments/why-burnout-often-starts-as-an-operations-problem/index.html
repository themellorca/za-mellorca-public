Why burnout often starts as an operations problem

Exhaustion is often framed as personal, yet after-hours work usually traces back to how operations are organised. Where it comes from and what a business can change.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-08

Burnout is usually described as something that happens to a person. A person who did not rest enough, did not set boundaries, did not manage stress. The advice that follows is personal too: take leave, switch off notifications, find a hobby. Some of that is sensible. But for many people the road to exhaustion runs through something far less personal, and far more fixable: the way the work itself is organised.

This piece looks at the operational side of burnout, especially the part that spills into the evening. It does not diagnose anyone, and it is not medical advice. Burnout is a health matter, and anyone who recognises it in themselves should speak to a qualified professional. What follows is a hypothesis about how everyday operations can wear people down, and what a business can change.

How operations produce exhaustion

Consider the work that arrives after hours. A customer emails at seven in the evening and expects a reply. A system job fails overnight and someone must check it before the day starts. Month-end reconciliation spills into the weekend because the data arrived late. A manager approves leave requests at ten at night because there is no other time. None of these tasks is large. Each is a small claim on the hours that are supposed to belong to the person.

The pattern behind them is usually operational rather than personal:

  • Work depends on one person's availability. If only one person can approve, check or fix, then that person is on call whether or not anyone has said so.
  • Information arrives late. When inputs come in at the end of the day, the processing is pushed into the evening.
  • Checking replaces trust. When nobody can see whether a process ran, someone checks, often at night.
  • Peaks are planned for nobody. Payroll week, month-end and year-end are predictable, yet they are staffed as if they were surprises.

Mellorca has looked at several faces of this: when the working day has no natural end, the inbox that follows you home and when payroll week means late nights for one person. This piece is about what they have in common.

What the evidence says, and its limits

Gallup's research on employee burnout, based on a study of nearly 7,500 full-time employees in the United States, found that 23% reported feeling burned out at work very often or always, and a further 44% sometimes. Those are American figures and say nothing directly about South Africa. What is more useful for our purposes is the finding that the strongest correlates of burnout had less to do with hard work than with how people are managed. The five most highly correlated factors were unfair treatment, an unmanageable workload, unclear roles, a lack of communication and support from a manager, and unreasonable time pressure.

Several of those are operational. An unmanageable workload and unreasonable time pressure are, to a large extent, outcomes of how work is routed, scheduled and staffed. Gallup also reports that employees who said they often or always have enough time to do their work were about 70% less likely to experience high burnout. Correlation is not cause, and the study is not about after-hours work specifically, but the direction is consistent with the argument here.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index adds some context: in its survey, 64% of respondents said they struggled to have the time and energy to do their job, and in its Microsoft 365 data people spent 57% of their time communicating rather than creating. Neither figure is about burnout, and neither is South African.

The legal frame is worth a mention. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act sets minimum conditions of employment in South Africa, including rules on working hours, and the Occupational Health and Safety Act places duties on employers regarding health and safety at work. Both are published on the Government site. How they apply to a given arrangement is a question for qualified advice. The operational point is that a business which relies on unrecorded after-hours work is relying on something it has probably not designed on purpose.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Rustenburg company that supplies fuel and lubricants to mining contractors. It has thirty staff. Its finance lead, who is also the de facto systems administrator, is the person to whom everything falls.

Contractors place orders until late afternoon. Delivery notes are captured the next morning. Invoices for the previous day's deliveries are due to customers by eight. The overnight bank feed fails about once a fortnight and has to be restarted. Fuel price adjustments are published at the start of the month and must be loaded before the first invoice run. When load-shedding hits the office during the day, the missed processing moves to the evening.

The finance lead works until nine most nights, checks the bank feed from her phone at six in the morning, and has not taken more than three consecutive days off in two years. She would say she is dedicated. A colleague would say she is exhausted. Both are true, and neither is a performance problem. It is a design problem: the work has been arranged so that one person's evenings are the buffer.

The human tax: wellbeing and energy

The first tax is wellbeing. Time that should be recovery, such as a meal, sleep or time with other people, is repeatedly interrupted or shortened. The second is energy, which is not restored when the evening is spent on the same work as the day.

The effects are not limited to the person. Their family, their colleagues and the quality of their work all absorb some of it. As in why "I'll do it tonight" became the default plan, the arrangement becomes normal because it works, until the day it does not.

What could realistically change

The objective is not to ban evening work, which some people choose. It is to stop the business depending on it by default.

  1. Trace the after-hours work. For a month, note every task done outside agreed hours, who did it, and what triggered it. This is the most important step, because the pattern is usually concentrated in a few tasks.
  2. Separate the predictable from the unpredictable. Month-end and payroll can be planned and staffed. A true emergency cannot, and needs a rota and a named backup.
  3. Move deadlines upstream. If invoices are late because inputs are late, the fix is earlier inputs, not later evenings.
  4. Replace checking with visibility. A simple alert that fires only when a job fails, sent to a rota rather than one person, removes the need for the early-morning look.
  5. Cross-train the single points. If only one person can restart the bank feed, two more should be able to.
  6. Agree what can wait. A clear rule on which messages need an evening response and which can wait until morning removes the guesswork that keeps people half-available.
  7. Make rest a managed matter. Leave and time off in lieu should be planned around the peaks, as in making Friday evening start on Friday evening.

The business outcome

The business becomes less dependent on goodwill and more on design. It is less exposed to the sudden absence of its most stretched person, and it gets better work from people who are rested.

The human dividend

The dividend is time and presence: evenings that belong to the person, and the ability to be properly present wherever they are, whether that is with family, at rest, or on something they have chosen. What people do with the time is theirs to decide.

The trade-offs

  • Some peaks cannot be removed. Month-end, tax deadlines and customer emergencies will still happen; the aim is to share them.
  • Rotas shift the load, not remove it. A shared on-call duty can be fairer, but it is still a duty and must be compensated or recognised.
  • Not everyone wants fewer evenings. Some people prefer flexible hours, and arrangements should respect that choice.
  • Operations are not the whole story. Unfair treatment and unclear roles also matter, and no process change substitutes for a manager who listens.

Why it matters

When burnout is framed as a personal failing, the remedy is personal too, and the cause keeps operating. When it is seen partly as an operations problem, a business has something concrete to examine: who does what after hours, why, and what would let them stop. That examination will not resolve every cause of exhaustion, but it can remove some of the avoidable ones.
