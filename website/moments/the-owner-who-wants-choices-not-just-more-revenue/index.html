Optionality and Choice

The Owner Who Wants Choices, Not Just More Revenue

Growth is the stated goal, but many owners describe wanting something else: the ability to step away, hand over or change course. What it takes to widen those options.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-11

Ask a room of business owners what they want, and the first answer is nearly always the same: growth. More customers, more turnover, a bigger team. It is the answer that fits the language of business. But ask the same owners, one at a time and a little later, what they would do if the business were running well, and the replies change. One would like to take a month off without the phone ringing. Another would like to hire a manager and spend her time on the part of the work she enjoys. A third would simply like to know that if he wanted to sell in five years, or close in two, he could.

These are not answers about revenue. They are answers about choices. Revenue is one of the things that can pay for choices, but it is not the same thing, and a business can grow steadily while the owner's options stay exactly as narrow as before.

The growth that does not widen the owner's options

There is a familiar pattern in owner-led businesses. Turnover rises. The owner works the same hours, or longer, because every extra customer needs the owner's attention. The business has more to lose, and fewer people who can step in. Revenue is up, and the owner's freedom to choose is, if anything, down.

Mellorca uses the word optionality for the thing that is missing: the existence of credible alternatives about work, investment, leave, expansion or exit. An owner has optionality when several paths are open and nothing about the current arrangement forces the choice. Optionality does not mean doing less. An owner who wants to work sixty hours a week on something they love is exercising a choice. The question is whether it is a choice at all, or a default that no one else can relieve.

Whether a particular owner feels this way cannot be assumed. Some are happily absorbed in the day-to-day and would not trade it. Mellorca treats the description above as a hypothesis about some owners, based on how owner-dependent businesses tend to work, not as a claim about all of them.

What narrows the options

Several operational conditions tend to appear together:

  • Decisions routed through the owner. Pricing, approvals, exceptions and supplier choices wait for one person. Mellorca has written about the manager's side of this in the manager who can't act without the founder.
  • Knowledge held in one head. Procedures, customer histories and system passwords sit with the owner or one long-serving employee. This is the subject of key-person dependency is a systems problem.
  • Unclear margins. If the owner does not know which work makes money, then growth may simply add effort. Mellorca explores this in why knowing your margins changes what you can choose.
  • No picture of what a buyer, a manager or a successor would need. The business may be sound, but nothing about it can easily be handed over.

The United States Small Business Administration's Business Resilience Guide, released to help owners prepare for, respond to and recover from disasters, is built around a similar logic. It walks owners through understanding current operations, including essential functions and dependencies, identifying key vendors and collaborators, protecting vital resources such as data, and strengthening financial readiness including cash flow. It is written for disaster planning, not for owner freedom. But the exercise it describes, writing down what the business depends on, is also the starting point for knowing which dependencies limit the owner's choices.

An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative case, not a real one: a family-owned logistics business in the Eastern Cape with about thirty staff. Revenue has grown for three years. The owner personally quotes every large job, because only he knows the real cost of each route. He approves every payment run. He is the only person who understands how the dispatch spreadsheet works.

When he talks about his plans he does not mention growth first. He mentions a family farm that he would like to spend more time on. He also says, quietly, that he does not know whether he could ever sell the business, because he cannot see what a buyer would find if they looked.

A project aimed purely at revenue might add more customers and more routes. A project aimed at options would look different. It would record how routes are costed, so that someone else could quote. It would agree approval limits, so that routine payments do not need him. It would move the dispatch logic out of one spreadsheet. None of this would raise turnover directly. All of it would make more choices available to him.

What could realistically change

  1. Ask the owner what choices they want. This is a different conversation from asking about targets. It may produce answers such as "two weeks away each year without being called", "a manager who runs the yard" or "the option to sell". Each points to different work.
  2. Map the dependencies on the owner. Which decisions, tasks and pieces of knowledge would stop if the owner were unavailable for a month? The SBA's emphasis on documenting essential functions and dependencies is a practical model.
  3. Reduce them in order of importance. Start with what would cause most harm if unavailable, not what is easiest.
  4. Make the numbers visible to others. People cannot take over what they cannot see. Margins, cash position and commitments need to be available beyond the owner. Mellorca discusses the sequencing question in how to prioritise digital improvements when budget is limited.
  5. Test the arrangement. A planned week away, with the phone off, is more informative than any plan on paper.

What changes first, and the business outcome

The first change is that someone else can do things the owner used to do. That might be a quote, an approval or the reading of a report. The owner's attention is released for choices only they can make.

The business outcome is a firm that is less fragile and easier to hand over, whether to a manager, a family member or a buyer. It does not follow that revenue rises, and Mellorca does not claim that it will. What follows is that the business is more able to continue without a single person, which many owners and their families, lenders and customers would regard as valuable.

The human dividend

The dividend is optionality, with autonomy alongside it. It is the practical possibility of choosing among several futures instead of being held by one. The World Health Organization's guidance on mental health at work lists flexible working arrangements among the organisational measures to prevent risks, and the UK Health and Safety Executive's standard on control describes people having a say over their schedules and breaks. Those are written about employees, but owners are people at work too, and the principle carries over without any claim that owners are a special case.

It is worth saying again that Mellorca does not prescribe what the options should be. Time with family is one possibility. Taking on a harder project, learning, stepping back to a smaller role and doing nothing for a while are others. The business that widens the owner's choices has done its job whichever the owner takes.

The trade-offs

Making a business less dependent on its owner can feel like a loss. For many founders, being indispensable is part of identity. Mellorca has looked at that in when the founder's identity is fused with every task. Handing over a decision can feel like handing over a piece of oneself.

The work takes time and money in the short term. Documenting costing methods, agreeing approval limits and moving systems is effort that does not appear in this month's sales. An owner under cash pressure may reasonably decide that the effort must wait.

Delegation carries risk. A manager may price differently, or make a decision the owner would not have made. Some of the owner's judgement cannot be written down, and some customers prefer to deal with the owner.

And more options do not guarantee a better life. An owner may find that having choices is uncomfortable when they have not made one for years. Optionality is a possibility, not an outcome.

Why it matters

A business exists to serve many interests, and the owner's is one of them. When a business is measured only by revenue, the owner's own question, which is what the business lets them do, disappears from view. Mellorca's reading is that for some owners, widening the range of credible choices is a more accurate description of what they want than growth alone. For those owners, the best use of a business system is not simply to bring in more money. It is to make a few more doors openable.
