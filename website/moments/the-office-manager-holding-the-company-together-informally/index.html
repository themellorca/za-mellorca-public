The Office Manager Holding the Company Together Informally

In many owner-led firms the office manager quietly does the job of several functions with no title for it. What that costs, and how to formalise it fairly.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

In many owner-led businesses there is someone with the title of office manager who does a good deal more than the title suggests. She (or he) orders the stationery and also chases the debtors. Sorts out the landlord and also explains the leave policy to a new hire. Reminds the owner about the licence renewal that nobody else remembered. None of this is in a job description, and much of it is not visible to anyone who has not tried to do it.

This piece looks at what it costs the person, and the business, when one role quietly holds many functions together. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on role clarity and workload, not a finding about any specific South African firm, and the scenarios are illustrative.

How a role grows without anyone deciding

An office manager's job tends to expand by default. Whatever does not clearly belong to someone else lands on her desk. The firm is small, the owner is busy, and she is willing. A task is done once as a favour and is hers from then on. Over the years she becomes the nearest thing the business has to a finance clerk, HR officer, facilities manager, compliance tracker and IT contact, while still being paid as an administrator.

This is partly a symptom of owner dependency. When the owner makes the decisions but is not available to run the details, someone has to hold the middle. The pieces on the manager who cannot act without the founder and the founder who wants to step back but cannot look at the same structure from higher up. The office manager is often the person who absorbs the gap.

What the informal role costs her

  • Dignity. Her contribution is large but unnamed. When it goes well, nothing seems to have happened. When something slips, she is the one asked. The expertise she has built in several fields is not recognised in her title, pay or standing.
  • Energy. A role made of interruptions is tiring. She is switching between payroll queries, a leaking tap and a client call with no time to finish any one properly. The work is never done because there is always another small thing.
  • Accountability without authority. She is expected to make things work without the power to decide spending, enforce a process or say no to a request.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards for work-related stress put role clarity among the conditions that matter. Its page on role in the management standards says that employees should understand their role and responsibilities and that the organisation should ensure they do not have conflicting roles. An informal role with many functions and no authority is a difficult fit for that standard.

What it risks for the business

The business gains from the arrangement, usually without noticing. It receives the output of several jobs for the price of one, and it avoids the harder conversations about who owns what. But the arrangement is fragile. If she leaves, takes leave or burns out, a large number of tasks have no obvious home. The cost article on key-person operational dependency sets out how that concentration builds, and the piece on why clear ownership gives people room to decide describes the alternative.

There is also an employment-law dimension. South African employers are expected to be clear about duties and remuneration, and the government's Basic Conditions of Employment Act page is a starting point for what the written particulars of employment should cover. Where the real job has outgrown the written one, the gap can matter for pay, hours and fairness.

An illustrative case

Think, as a hypothetical example, of an East London architecture practice with eighteen staff. The office manager began as a receptionist twelve years ago. She now prepares the monthly payroll inputs, keeps the professional-registration renewals, manages the supplier accounts, onboards new hires, and is the first call when the server misbehaves. The owners are good architects and deliberately light on administration.

When she takes ten days' leave, a registration lapses unnoticed, two supplier payments are late and a junior is sent home on the first morning because nobody had set up their access. Nobody realised there were so many separate tasks until they stopped happening.

Making the informal role visible

The aim is not to dismantle what works. It is to make it visible, fair and shared. That begins with learning what the role actually contains, then deciding which tasks belong in it, which belong elsewhere and which can be simplified or removed. The guide to making ownership clear at every stage offers a useful way to think about this.

Practical next actions

  1. Ask her to keep a two-week task log. Everything she does, however small, with rough time. Present it as a way to recognise the work, not to audit it.
  2. Group the tasks into functions. Finance, people, premises, compliance, technology and general admin. Note how many hours go to each.
  3. Decide an owner for each function. Some stay with her and are formalised in her job description. Some go to a named colleague or an outside provider. Some can be dropped.
  4. Review the title and pay against the real work. If the role is half finance and half HR, a conversation about grading and remuneration is overdue.
  5. Write down the recurring dates. Licences, renewals, filing deadlines and payment runs, in a shared calendar with a second person who can see it.
  6. Give her the authority that matches the accountability. A spending limit, a defined right to decline requests, and a clear route to the owner for decisions.

The business outcome

A business that formalises what its office manager does gets continuity, clearer cost, and a better view of what administration really takes. It becomes able to cope with a leave or a resignation without panic, and it can decide on purpose which tasks to automate, outsource or keep.

The human dividend

The dividend is predictability and dignity. Predictability means a role whose boundaries are known, so that the day has a shape and a holiday does not hand the company a crisis. Dignity means the skill she has built is named, graded and paid for. What she does with the recovered time and standing is her own choice: a promotion, a narrower role, further study or a steadier working week.

The trade-offs

  • Formalising changes the arrangement. Some office managers value the freedom and trust of an informal role, and may not welcome fixed boundaries.
  • It costs money. Fair grading and extra cover increase payroll, which a small firm may find hard to carry.
  • Handing work over loses context. Tasks moved to other people may be done less well at first, because her knowledge goes with the task.
  • The owner's habits matter. Without a change in how the owner delegates, the informal role will quietly regrow.

Why it matters

The person who holds a company together informally is doing important work that the company has not yet agreed to see. Naming it, sharing it and paying for it protects the business from a single point of failure and gives the person the standing their contribution already deserves.
