When Sunday Evening Becomes Monday Morning

In some businesses the week's plan does not exist until someone builds it on Sunday night: who goes where, what is delivered, which job comes first. What changes when the plan is ready before the weekend begins.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-03

For some people, Sunday evening has a shape of its own.

Supper is finished. The week has not yet started. And somewhere between the dishes and bed, the phone comes out, a notebook opens, and Monday gets built: which team goes to which site, which delivery has to be chased, which customer is expecting a call first thing, who said on Friday afternoon that they might be off.

Nobody asked for this to happen on a Sunday. It happens because, in many businesses, the plan for the coming week does not exist anywhere until someone puts it together, and Sunday evening is the last quiet moment in which to do it.

Some people are content with this. A short look ahead helps them sleep, and that is a legitimate preference. This piece is concerned with the other case: where Sunday planning is not a choice, but the only way Monday can start on time.

The weekend is not as closed as it looks

There is public evidence that the weekend boundary is porous for many knowledge workers. Microsoft's June 2025 Work Trend Index report on the infinite workday, based on aggregated and anonymised Microsoft 365 signals, found that nearly 20% of employees who were actively working on weekends checked email before noon on Saturday and Sunday, and that more than 5% were back in email on Sunday evenings from 6pm onwards. The same report found that 57% of meetings were ad hoc calls without a calendar invitation.

That last figure says something useful about planning. A large share of coordination is happening on the fly rather than being scheduled in advance. If that is how the working week runs, it is not surprising that the next week also tends to be organised at the last possible moment.

These figures describe Microsoft's global user base. They are context, not a South African measurement, and they do not capture field-based work at all. But they suggest the Sunday-evening pattern is not imaginary.

Why the plan arrives so late

The record behind this story is hypothesised. The mechanism proposed here is testable rather than proven.

In many small and mid-sized businesses, the inputs to next week's plan are scattered. Job requests sit in messages. Material deliveries are confirmed by phone. Staff availability lives in a WhatsApp group. Progress on this week's work is known to the people who did it, but not recorded anywhere the planner can see. And Friday afternoon, when the week could be closed, is usually spent finishing the week rather than preparing the next one.

So the plan cannot be made on Friday, because the information is not yet together. It cannot be made on Monday morning, because work starts at 07:00. That leaves the weekend.

The cause is not a lack of diligence. If anything, it is diligence compensating for a missing process. The human tax falls on whoever has quietly become responsible for making Monday work.

An illustrative Sunday

Consider an illustrative example: a Polokwane building maintenance contractor with three teams and about twenty-five staff.

  • Clients log jobs by phone, email and WhatsApp to the owner and the operations supervisor.
  • Teams report progress verbally at the end of each day; nothing is updated in a shared list.
  • Material orders are placed by the supervisor and confirmed by suppliers by phone, sometimes on Friday afternoon.

In this scenario, the supervisor spends roughly an hour every Sunday evening reading back through messages, calling one or two team leaders, and drafting a WhatsApp message that tells each team where to be on Monday. On weeks when something changes over the weekend, the message is rewritten late at night.

Mellorca has described related problems in why field teams still phone the office for information and how to design a handoff that cannot depend on memory. The Sunday hour is what those problems cost the person who holds them together.

The human tax

The record names three taxes: time, energy and presence.

Time is the obvious one: an evening hour every week adds up across a year. Energy is less visible: the effort of reconstructing a picture from fragments is tiring in a way that following an existing plan is not. Presence is the subtlest. Even before the planning starts, someone who knows they will have to do it may carry part of Monday through the rest of Sunday.

We cannot say how any individual experiences this. Some will barely notice. But the mechanism is real enough to be worth designing out where it is not wanted.

What could realistically change

The interventions are about moving planning earlier and giving it a home.

  1. Hold the work in one list. Every job, request and commitment lands in a single shared record with an owner and a status, however simple the tool.
  2. Capture progress as it happens. A brief end-of-day update from each team, recorded rather than spoken, means the planner starts from the current position.
  3. Set a Friday planning slot. A fixed half hour on Friday to draft next week's allocation, while people are still available to answer questions.
  4. Publish the plan before the weekend. Teams know on Friday where they start on Monday. Changes after that go through one agreed route.
  5. Accept a draft. A plan that is eighty per cent right on Friday and adjusted on Monday is usually better than a perfect plan built on Sunday night.

The WHO and ILO guidance on healthy telework, although written for remote work, makes a related point: it recommends clear priorities, shared ways of signalling availability and sufficient rest. Those are organisational practices before they are technology choices.

What changes first, and the business outcome

The first change is timing. The plan exists on Friday afternoon. Monday morning starts from a known position, and the weekend is not needed to create one.

The business outcome is steadier operations. Teams waste less time waiting for instructions, material problems surface earlier, and the business is less dependent on one person's weekend. If the supervisor is ill or on leave, someone else can read the plan.

The human dividend

The dividends are time and predictability.

Time, because the Sunday hour is returned to whatever the person chooses to do with it.

Predictability, for the planner and for everyone else. Team members know on Friday where they will be on Monday, rather than finding out from a late-night message. The week begins when it is meant to begin.

The companion piece Friday Evening Should Start on Friday Evening asks whether work can stop at the end of the week. This one asks whether the next week can be ready before the weekend starts, so that it does not have to be assembled on the way back in.

The trade-offs

  • Friday becomes busier. Planning time has to come from somewhere, and the last afternoon of the week is already full in many businesses.
  • Recording progress is extra work for teams. If updates are not short and clearly useful, they will be resented or skipped.
  • Plans made earlier change more. Some Monday adjustments are inevitable, and teams need to accept that a Friday plan is a starting point.
  • Some people prefer the Sunday look. Removing the need should not mean removing the option.

Why it matters

Weekends are finite. For many people they are where rest, family, faith, sport, study or nothing in particular happens. When the next week has to be built inside them, it is rarely because anyone decided it should be. It is because the business has no earlier moment where the plan comes together.

Mellorca's interpretation is that Sunday-evening planning is usually a process gap carried by a conscientious person. Give the week's work a single home and a Friday deadline, and Monday can start on Monday. What returns is not a rule about weekends, but the ability to decide for oneself when the week begins.
