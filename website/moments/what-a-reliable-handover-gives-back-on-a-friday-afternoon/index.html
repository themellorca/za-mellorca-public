What a reliable handover gives back on a Friday afternoon

When someone else is covering the weekend or Monday, the last hour of Friday decides whether the person leaving can actually leave. What a dependable handover returns to both people, and what it takes to build one.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-05

Late on a Friday afternoon, in many businesses, someone is trying to hand over. A colleague will be covering the weekend, or will hold the fort on Monday while they are away. There are open orders, a customer waiting on a reply, a delivery that may or may not arrive, a supplier who promised to call back.

The handover might be a conversation at a desk, a voice note, a long WhatsApp message or a hurried email. It is usually done at the end of a tiring week, by someone who wants to go home, to someone who wants to know what they are walking into.

Whether that last hour goes well has a surprising amount to do with how the weekend goes, for both of them.

Handovers are a known risk point

Industries that run around the clock have long treated handover as a point where things go wrong. The UK Health and Safety Executive's good practice guidelines on shift work include a plain instruction to set standards and allow time for communication at shift handovers. The point is not that handover is difficult in principle, but that it tends to happen at the end of a duty period, when people are tired and eager to leave, and that it needs structure and time to work.

Most offices, workshops and shops are not safety-critical plants, and the stakes are different. But the mechanism is recognisable. Information that lives in one person's head has to move to another person's head in a short window, and anything that does not make the trip becomes a problem for whoever is holding the work next.

Mellorca has written about the general design of this in how to design a handoff that cannot depend on memory. This piece looks at what a better handover returns to the people on either side of it.

What an unreliable handover costs both people

For the person leaving, an unreliable handover rarely ends at five o'clock. Because they know the handover was incomplete, they stay reachable. The phone stays on. A message on Saturday asking where the signed delivery note is gets answered from a braai or a sports field. For some people, the weekend becomes a kind of partial shift, never formally worked but never fully off.

For the person covering, the cost is different. They are responsible for work they only partly understand. They may not know which customer has already been promised a call, or which order is waiting on a payment. Each gap means either contacting the absent colleague, which they may feel awkward about, or guessing.

It would be wrong to claim to know how any particular person experiences this. It is reasonable to observe that both roles carry a tax: the leaver pays in time and presence, and the coverer pays in uncertainty and exposure.

Why handovers end up depending on memory

The usual cause is not carelessness. It is that the work itself lives in personal places.

  • Customer and supplier conversations sit in a personal inbox that the covering colleague cannot see.
  • Commitments, such as "I'll call you back on Monday", are recorded nowhere except the person's memory or calendar.
  • Open items are tracked in a personal notebook, a private spreadsheet or the person's head.
  • There is no agreed format for a handover, so its quality depends on how much time and energy the person has left on Friday.

When work is organised this way, a handover has to be a transfer of memory. And memory transfers badly at the end of a week.

An illustrative Friday

Take an illustrative example, not a real business: a Bloemfontein agricultural supplies business with about twenty-five staff, open on Saturday mornings. Two sales administrators alternate Saturday cover.

In this scenario, each administrator manages their own customers through their own mailbox. On Friday, the one going off writes a list of open items from memory and sends it to the one covering. The list is usually good, but never complete. Most Saturdays, the covering administrator sends two or three messages asking about orders that were not on the list, and the absent colleague answers them.

Neither administrator is doing anything wrong. The way the work is set up means that the Saturday cover can only be as good as Friday afternoon's memory.

What could realistically change

The improvements are mostly about putting work where both people can see it, and giving handover a shape.

  • Use shared places for shared work. Microsoft's documentation describes shared mailboxes as suited to addresses that several people need to access, such as sales or support addresses, with permitted users able to send as the shared address. Customer conversations that a colleague may need to pick up belong in a place like that rather than a personal inbox.
  • Share the relevant calendar. Microsoft's support guidance on calendar permissions explains that a delegate can see and edit calendar items and respond to meetings on someone's behalf, within the same organisation. Agreed callbacks and appointments are then visible to the person covering.
  • Keep open items in a list both people can see. A simple shared task list or job board means the handover is a review of something that already exists, not a reconstruction.
  • Agree a handover format. A short, fixed set of headings: what is open, what is promised to whom, what might go wrong, who to contact if it does. Fifteen minutes with a format is usually better than an hour without one.
  • Agree what counts as a reason to make contact. If the covering colleague knows which situations genuinely need the absent person and which do not, both can relax about the rest.

What changes first, and for the business

The first change is that the Friday handover gets shorter and more complete, because most of what needs handing over is already visible. The covering colleague can answer most questions by looking rather than asking.

For the business, customers experience continuity over the weekend and on Mondays, fewer commitments are missed, and the business is less exposed when someone is unexpectedly absent. The broader point that broken handoffs are design problems rather than personal failings is made in why broken handoffs are systems problems.

The human dividend

For the person leaving, the dividend is time and presence. When the handover is reliable, the weekend can be fully theirs. They do not need to keep half an eye on the phone in case a question arrives that only they can answer. What they do with that time is their business: family, sport, rest, a second job, or nothing at all.

For the person covering, the dividend is a kind of predictability. They know what they are holding. They can make decisions without the discomfort of interrupting a colleague's weekend or guessing at a customer's history.

The dividend is shared, which is part of why it matters. A reliable handover is one of the few improvements that returns something to two people at once.

The trade-offs

  • Shared visibility reduces privacy. When customer conversations move into shared mailboxes and calendars become visible, people lose some control over their own working space. That needs clear norms, and private matters need a private place.
  • Handover discipline is extra work. Keeping shared lists current asks something of people every day, not just on Friday.
  • Relationships can feel diluted. Customers who value dealing with one named person may notice when replies come from a shared address.
  • The coverer carries more. A better handover makes cover possible; it does not make it free. The person covering still does the work, and that deserves recognition.

Why it matters

The end of the week is where the design of work meets people's lives most directly. If the work can only be held by the person who started it, it follows them home. If it can be passed on cleanly, they can put it down.

Mellorca's reading is that a reliable handover is less about the quality of a Friday conversation and more about whether the work was visible all week. Build that, and Friday afternoon stops being the hardest hour.
