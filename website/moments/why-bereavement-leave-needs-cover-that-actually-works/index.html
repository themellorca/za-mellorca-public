Why Bereavement Leave Needs Cover That Actually Works

A death in the family cannot be scheduled, so the cover has to be ready before it happens. Why unplanned leave fails without it, and what a workable plan looks like.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-01

Most absences can be planned around. Annual leave is booked weeks ahead, parental leave has a due date, and even surgery usually comes with a date in the diary. A death in the family has none of that. It arrives on an ordinary Tuesday, and the person affected has to leave immediately, with no time for a handover and in no state to write one.

This piece looks at what happens in small and mid-sized businesses when that call comes, and why the cover arrangements that work for planned leave often fail for this one. It is a hypothesis drawn from the structure of leave entitlements and from general research, not a finding about any particular employer. Individual experiences of grief differ widely, and nothing here assumes how any one person will feel or behave.

What the law gives, and what it assumes

The Basic Conditions of Employment Act provides family responsibility leave for covered employees who meet its qualifying conditions. Among the circumstances it recognises is the death of certain close family members. The entitlement is a modest number of paid days a year, and the details, including who qualifies and what proof an employer may ask for, should be checked against the Act and any contract or policy that gives more.

The law gives the right to be absent. It does not make the work go away. Between the entitlement and the reality sits a practical question that the Act cannot answer: who does the job on Wednesday?

How unplanned leave goes wrong

Consider an illustrative case in a Bloemfontein accounting practice of eleven people. A senior bookkeeper loses her father on a Monday morning and phones in. She is told, kindly, to take whatever time she needs. By Monday afternoon a colleague is calling to ask where the bank reconciliation for a client is saved. On Tuesday a partner sends a message asking only one small thing. By Wednesday she is checking her email from the family home while relatives arrive.

Nobody is being unkind, and every request is reasonable on its own. But the combined effect is that the leave she is entitled to has become partly work, and partly an anxious wait for the next message. She is physically at the funeral arrangements and attentionally at the office.

The cause is not a lack of sympathy. The cause is that the practice has no way of finding out what she was doing, what was due, and who could take it. The knowledge is in her head and in her folders. In a larger organisation, someone is usually appointed to fill that kind of gap. In a small one, the person who is away is the system.

What research says about grief and work

Published work on bereavement and the workplace is limited but consistent in several respects. A survey of organisations reported in a study of the impact of bereavement grief on the workplace found that bereavement leave was a common experience for employers, and its authors concluded that organisations should prepare more, both for the leave and for the return to work. Bereavement is, in other words, a predictable category of event even though each instance is unpredictable.

The World Health Organization's guidance on mental health at work describes how supportive working conditions, including manageable workloads and responsive management, affect wellbeing. For a person in acute loss, an uncovered workload is not an abstract risk factor. It is the reason they feel unable to stop.

The human tax: presence

The thing taken from the person is presence. Time with a dying relative's family, with children who have lost a grandparent, with the paperwork and the ritual of a funeral, is time that cannot be repeated. Being half-available to the office during those days costs something that does not return when the person comes back.

The safety and wellbeing cost is quieter. Someone who goes straight back to work because the alternative is the guilt of leaving colleagues stranded may be doing it against their own interest. Some people do prefer the routine of work in the early days of grief, and that preference should be respected. The point is that it should be a choice, and not something produced by a gap in cover.

What could realistically change

Cover for unplanned leave cannot be built after the event. It has to exist beforehand, as an ordinary feature of how work is shared. The related story when the out-of-office reply doesn't stop the work shows how an automatic reply alone is not cover, and the price of never fully switching off on leave describes what staying reachable costs.

The elements of a plan are modest. Every role has a named alternate. Critical deadlines for the next fortnight are visible to more than one person. Shared locations hold the work in progress. And there is a clear rule that nobody contacts a person on bereavement leave about work except through one agreed route, usually the manager, and only if something cannot wait. For roles whose knowledge sits with one person, the discussion in when parental leave means handing over an undocumented job applies with extra force, since there is no notice period to do it in.

Practical next actions

  1. Name an alternate for every role. Write down who steps in for each person, and check that the alternate knows they hold that responsibility.
  2. List the next fortnight's deadlines. Keep a shared view of what is due in the coming two weeks for each person, updated weekly, so the first call after bad news is answered from the list, not from memory.
  3. Set a no-contact rule. Agree that during bereavement leave, a single named manager is the only point of contact, and that work questions are answered by the alternate before anyone phones the absent person.
  4. Write the leave policy plainly. State the statutory family responsibility leave, what the business gives beyond it, what proof is asked for and how unpaid or extended time can be requested.
  5. Plan the return. Agree on a conversation before the person comes back about workload, a lighter first week and what has changed, and ask what they would prefer.
  6. Do a dry run. Pick a critical role and ask what would happen if the person were away from tomorrow for ten days. Fix the first thing that breaks.

The business outcome

A business with working cover absorbs an unplanned absence without scrambling. Clients are served, errors from rushed handovers are fewer, and colleagues do not carry resentment or guilt. Staff also learn what the business does when something terrible happens to one of them, and that lesson is remembered long after the particular event. It supports the loyalty the business hopes for, without asking for it.

The human dividend

The dividend is presence. A person who can put the phone down, knowing the work is covered, can be fully with their family and with their loss. What they need in that time differs from person to person: company, quiet, ritual, administration, or simply sleep. The cover does not decide what they do with the time. It only makes sure the time is theirs.

The trade-offs

  • It costs effort before any need exists. Cross-covering roles and keeping deadlines visible takes time every week for an event that may not occur for years.
  • Cover is imperfect. Alternates are slower, and some work will wait, which clients must sometimes be told.
  • Plans can feel cold. A written process for handling a death can seem clinical, so it should be built with staff and applied with warmth.
  • Statutory leave is short. Grief does not end when the allowance does, so the plan has to leave room for flexible hours, unpaid time or phased returns where the business can manage them.

Why it matters

A business cannot choose when its people will suffer a loss. It can choose whether those people will have to manage the office while they do. Cover that works before it is needed is a practical form of decency, and in a small business it is often the only form available.
