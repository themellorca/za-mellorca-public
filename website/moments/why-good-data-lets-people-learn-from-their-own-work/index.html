Why Good Data Lets People Learn From Their Own Work

People improve fastest when they can see the results of what they did. Why clean, timely data is a learning tool as much as a control tool, and how to share it fairly.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-14

Most people learn a job by doing it, noticing what happened and adjusting. That loop needs one ingredient that many workplaces do not supply: a view of what happened. A claims handler, a dispatcher or a bookkeeper may finish hundreds of items a month and never see how those items turned out. The numbers exist somewhere. They are collected, cleaned and summarised for a manager, and the people whose work produced them hear a verdict, if at all, weeks later.

This piece looks at good data as a learning tool for the person doing the work, not only a control tool for the person overseeing it. It is a hypothesis drawn from general guidance on reporting and working conditions, not a finding about any particular South African employer.

The loop that reporting often breaks

A learning loop has four steps: act, observe the result, compare it with what was expected, and try something different. In a small shop, the loop closes by itself. The baker sees which loaves are left at closing time. In a larger or more digital operation, the observing step moves out of sight. The result lands in a system, then in a report, then in a meeting that the doer may not attend.

By the time the result returns, it is too old to act on. A person told in March that February's error rate was high cannot remember which February jobs were the problem. They can only feel judged. The information that could have taught them has turned into a grade.

Three habits usually sit behind this:

  • Reports are built for the reader who pays, not the person who does. The monthly pack answers the manager's questions and arrives on the manager's schedule.
  • The data is trusted only after someone has cleaned it. Raw figures are held back until they have been checked by hand, which can take most of a week.
  • Visibility is treated as risk. Showing staff their own numbers feels like inviting argument, so it is easier to keep the figures upstairs.

What good data would change

Good data, in this sense, means three things: it is recent enough to remember, specific enough to act on, and trustworthy enough that nobody spends the meeting disputing it. Microsoft's documentation on dashboards describes them as a single page that gives a view of important information, built from the underlying data and refreshed as that data changes. The same idea can be put to a different use than executive reporting. A small view that a team member opens each morning, showing yesterday's work, is a feedback tool.

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Pretoria accounting practice where junior staff capture supplier invoices for clients. Every month the senior bookkeeper reviews a sample and sends back corrections. The juniors learn that their work contained errors, but not which kind, how often or on which clients. Suppose the practice instead lists each correction the same week, tagged by type: wrong VAT code, wrong date, duplicate. A junior who sees that most of their corrections are VAT codes on one client can ask the right question, check the client's supplier list and fix the cause. The learning is quicker, and the senior bookkeeper stops being the only person who understands the pattern.

Why autonomy matters here

The World Health Organization's guidance on mental health at work names low control over one's work among the conditions that can harm wellbeing. A person who cannot see the result of their work has little real control over it, because control depends on knowing what to change. Feedback data is a modest, practical way to restore some of that control. It lets the person steer.

The human tax: optionality and confidence

The first cost of missing feedback is optionality. If you cannot see the effect of your choices, you cannot tell which choice was better, so you tend to repeat what you did last time. The range of things you are willing to try shrinks. People settle into routines that may be mediocre because they have no evidence that anything else would be better.

The second cost is confidence. Without feedback, people fill the silence with guesses. Some guess too well of themselves and miss real problems. Others guess too poorly and assume the worst every time a manager schedules a chat. Neither group is learning. Reliable, regular numbers cut the guessing, which is why they can make a team calmer as well as better.

Doing this fairly

Giving people their own data is not a licence to rank them in public. Two cautions apply.

First, privacy and fairness. Figures about an identifiable person are personal information, and the Protection of Personal Information Act sets conditions for how it is processed. A reasonable starting rule is that each person sees their own figures by default, and that comparisons across people are shared only after the team has agreed how they will be used. The aim is learning, not shaming.

Second, measures must reflect the work. A count of items closed per hour will teach people to close easy items quickly. Choose measures that point to the real objective, such as corrections needed, repeat contacts or jobs finished by the promised date, and say plainly what the figures do not capture.

Where the data comes from matters

Feedback is only as good as the records behind it. If the weekly figure has to be rebuilt by hand, as described in the report someone rebuilds by hand every Friday, it will arrive late and tempt people to doubt it. If problems are only found after publication, as in why data quality problems are found after reports are published, the first lesson staff learn is not to trust the numbers. And where people have no time to look, the note on the employee who never has time to learn is a reminder that a dashboard nobody has ten minutes for teaches nothing.

Practical next actions

  1. Pick one role and one result. Choose a single job where the person rarely sees how their work turned out, and name the one result that would help them most, such as errors found later or jobs completed late.
  2. Shorten the delay. Find out how long it takes for that result to reach the person. If it is more than a week, work out the smallest change that would bring it down to a few days, even if the figure is rough.
  3. Show people their own numbers first. Share each person's figures privately before any team comparison, and ask what they notice. Their questions will show you which measures make sense.
  4. Tag errors by type. Instead of a bare count, record a short reason for each problem. Patterns are what people can learn from.
  5. Agree what the data is for. Write one sentence, for example "these figures are for each person to improve their own work, and not for pay decisions", and keep to it. Review it with the team after a month.

The business outcome

Teams that can see their own results tend to find problems earlier, which cuts rework and the cost of corrections after the fact. They also depend less on a single reviewer, so the business is less exposed when that reviewer is away. The data gets cleaner too, because people who use it notice errors in it and have a reason to report them.

The human dividend

The dividend here is optionality: more room to try, adjust and choose. A person who can see what their work produced can experiment with a different approach and know within days whether it helped. That is also a small route to growth that does not depend on a course, a promotion or someone else's attention. What each person does with that room is theirs to decide, whether it is mastering the current role, moving sideways or simply working with less worry.

The trade-offs

  • Cleaning data takes effort. Making figures timely and trustworthy may need process changes upstream, and those cost time before they save it.
  • Early numbers can mislead. A week of figures is noisy, and people may overreact. Show trends, and say how much movement is normal.
  • Individual data is sensitive. Anything about a named person needs a clear purpose, limited access and attention to the privacy rules.
  • Not everyone wants a scoreboard. Some people find constant numbers stressful, so offer the view and let people choose how often to look.

Why it matters

A business that collects data only to inspect people wastes much of its value. The same records, shared in time and in a form the doer can use, turn routine work into a steady source of improvement. The people closest to the work usually see the fix first, once they can see the result.
