Why self-service can feel like respect

Self-service is usually sold as a cost saving. Done well it also gives customers and staff something quieter: the ability to get an answer or make a change without asking permission or waiting for someone else.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-08

Self-service has a mixed reputation. Anyone who has fought a parking machine, a phone menu or a badly built portal knows how it can feel like being handed the work and told it is a favour. Yet there are moments when self-service feels very different: checking a balance at midnight without calling anyone, downloading a payslip without asking HR, rebooking an appointment in thirty seconds.

The difference is not the technology. It is whether the design treats the person as capable of managing their own affairs and gives them something real in return for doing so. This piece looks at when self-service reads as respect, when it reads as cost-shifting, and what a small business can do to land on the right side of that line.

Two kinds of self-service

It helps to separate two things that share a name.

Self-service that transfers work. The organisation removes a person from the process and asks the customer or employee to fill the gap. The form is long, the rules are unclear, and when something goes wrong there is nobody to ask. The saving is real, but it belongs to the organisation.

Self-service that returns control. The person gets access to information or an action that previously required an intermediary, at the time that suits them, with a clear way to see what happened. The saving is shared: the organisation handles fewer routine requests, and the person no longer waits.

The same portal can be either, depending on how it is built and what happens when the person gets stuck.

Why autonomy is the right word

The human agency theme in this series is about people having a say in how, when and by what route their affairs get handled. Self-service touches it directly.

Usability research has long treated user control as a basic design principle. The Nielsen Norman Group's usability heuristics include the idea that people need a clearly marked way out of an unwanted state, and that systems should keep people informed about what is happening. Those principles were written about interface design, but they explain why the same service can feel respectful or patronising. When the person can see where they are, undo a mistake and reach a human if needed, they are being treated as a competent adult.

The UK Government's service manual makes a related point in its guidance on user needs: services should be built around what people are trying to do, rather than around the organisation's internal structure. A person trying to update a bank account for their salary is not interested in which department owns the change.

An illustrative scenario

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Cape Town company with sixty staff and a two-person HR and payroll team.

Every month the same requests arrive. Staff want copies of payslips, leave balances, tax certificates and confirmation letters for bank or rental applications. Each request is small. Together they take up a large share of the payroll team's week, and each employee waits a day or two for something they feel they should be able to just look up.

In this scenario the company turns on the employee self-service features already included in its payroll or HR system. Staff can download payslips and see leave balances themselves. Standard letters are generated on request. The payroll team still handles exceptions, corrections and anything involving judgement.

Nothing about this is technically ambitious. What changes is the tone of the interaction. The employee no longer has to ask for what is rightfully theirs, and the payroll team no longer has to behave like a gatekeeper of it. Mellorca's article on standard letters typed by hand describes the cost side of that same pattern.

The human tax: autonomy and dignity

Without self-service, people pay two taxes. The first is autonomy: they cannot act on their own affairs without someone else's help or approval, even for things that are theirs by right. The second is dignity: having to ask, and wait, for something routine can feel like a small reminder of who holds the keys.

That second point should not be overstated. Many people prefer talking to a human, and for some tasks that is exactly right. A person disputing a charge, bereaved customers, or anyone not comfortable with digital tools may find forced self-service far worse than a queue. The concern is the person who would gladly help themselves and is not allowed to.

This links to when digital-only leaves some customers behind, which deals with what happens when self-service is the only route. Respect, here, means offering the option and keeping a human route open.

What makes self-service feel respectful

  • It covers the routine things completely. A portal that shows some information and hides the rest forces people to contact someone anyway.
  • It shows status. A customer who can see that a request is received, in progress or complete does not need to ask. The Government Digital Service guidance on user needs and the NN/g heuristics both stress visibility of what is happening.
  • It lets people correct mistakes. Undo, edit and cancel options signal trust. Their absence signals the opposite.
  • It leaves a human route. A visible way to reach a person, with a reasonable expectation of reply, turns self-service from a wall into a shortcut.
  • It protects the person's information. POPIA's section on security measures requires responsible parties to protect the integrity and confidentiality of personal information. Self-service that is easy but insecure is not respectful. Proper sign-in, and limiting each person to their own records, matters.

Where to start in a small business

You rarely need a new platform. Start by listing the questions your team answers most often: where is my invoice, what is my balance, what is the status of my order, can I have a copy of my certificate. Then check what your existing systems already offer. Many accounting, payroll and booking tools include customer or staff portals that are switched off or unused. Mellorca's guide on how to find the manual work nobody is measuring is a useful way to build that list.

Microsoft's documentation on Bookings, for example, describes how customers can book and manage their own appointments online, which is a typical case where the person stays in control of their own diary and the business avoids a round of calls.

The first consequence, and the business outcome

The first consequence is that routine requests stop landing on a person. Customers and staff get answers when they need them, and the team that used to respond can spend its time on cases that need judgement.

The business outcome is fewer interruptions, faster responses and a more consistent answer, since the information comes from the record itself rather than from someone's memory.

The human dividend

The dividend is autonomy, along with the time that comes from not waiting. A person can handle a routine matter at midnight, on a Sunday or on a bus, without needing anyone's availability or goodwill. For the team on the other side, it means fewer small, repetitive requests competing with the work that actually needs them.

The trade-offs

  • Some people will always prefer a person. Forcing self-service on them is cost-shifting, not respect.
  • Self-service shifts effort to the user. If the design is poor, the person ends up doing unpaid work and then calling anyway.
  • Security and privacy take care. Opening records to individuals requires sound access controls and clear limits.
  • Human contact can shrink. Routine requests are sometimes the only moments staff and customers talk. Relationships need other moments to survive.

Why it matters

Self-service is neither good nor bad in itself. It becomes respect when it hands people something they were previously made to ask for, and when a person remains available for the cases that deserve one. It becomes a burden when it is just the organisation's workload with a friendly name.

Mellorca's interpretation is that the test is simple: would the person choose this route if they had the alternative? If the answer is often yes, the design is probably respecting them.
