The Employee Who Dreads Being the Only One Who Knows

Being the only person who understands a task feels like job security until you need a day off. What sole knowledge costs the person, and how to share it.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

There is a particular dread that belongs to the person who is the only one who knows. It is not the fear of being wrong. It is the fear of being away: the stomach drop when a child falls ill at seven in the morning, when a funeral is on a Tuesday, or when a flu takes three days. Every absence comes with a quiet calculation of what will not get done and who will phone.

This piece looks at that experience from the employee's side. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on job demands and support, not a finding about any specific South African firm, and the examples are illustrative.

How one person becomes the only one

Nobody sets out to create a single point of knowledge. It builds up through ordinary steps. A person is good at a task, so the task is left with them. They learn its quirks, such as which customer needs the credit note dated a certain way, or which supplier portal times out after ten minutes. The quirks never get written down because there is always something more urgent. After a few years the task is a small private craft.

From the outside this looks like reliability, and often the employee takes pride in it. The business is not wrong to value it. The difficulty is that the value is paid for with the person's freedom to be absent, and the business carries a risk it has never priced.

What the dread costs

The costs show up in three places.

  • Presence. The employee is physically on leave but mentally still at work. Phones stay on, laptops come along, and the holiday is spent half-watching for messages. The note on when the out-of-office reply does not stop the work describes this in more detail.
  • Energy. Carrying the only copy of important knowledge is tiring. The person rehearses what could go wrong, checks twice and works ahead of absences, which means extra hours before and after any time off.
  • Dignity. Being treated as irreplaceable can slide into being treated as a fixture. Requests for leave are met with a sigh. Sick days attract guilt. The person's judgement is valued, but their ordinary human limits are not.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards for work-related stress give support as one of the conditions that shape whether pressure becomes harmful. Its page on support in the management standards describes systems that let employees get help and resources from colleagues and managers. A job where nobody else can step in is, almost by definition, a job with the thin end of that support.

What the business risks

The risk for the business is the mirror image. A single illness, resignation or family emergency can stop a process outright. The guide to what happens when the only person who understands the system leaves covers the extreme case, and the cost article on catching up after absence because of poor handover covers the milder, more frequent one: the backlog and rework that wait for the person's return.

There is also a legal and human dimension. South African employees have statutory entitlements to leave under the Basic Conditions of Employment Act, set out on the government's Basic Conditions of Employment Act page. A business that arranges its work so that taking leave is practically impossible has a gap between what employees are entitled to and what they can actually use.

An illustrative case

Imagine, as a hypothetical example, a Pietermaritzburg security-services company with fifty guards on rotating shifts. The scheduling is done by one administrator, using a spreadsheet with colour codes that only she understands: green for a guard on a site contract, orange for someone covering, purple for a standby arrangement that the client agreed by phone. When she is out with a bad back for two days, supervisors guess at the roster, two sites are short and a client notices.

The administrator returns to complaints and an apology she does not owe. The following winter she comes to work with a fever rather than risk it again. The business has not asked her to. The spreadsheet has.

Sharing what only one person knows

The aim is not to make the person disposable. It is to let their knowledge exist somewhere other than inside them, so that they are valued for judgement and experience and not for being a single point of failure. Good sharing starts small: a written list of the weekly steps, a second person who has done each step once, and a standing agreement about who covers what. The reflections in what a reliable handover gives back on a Friday afternoon show how much calmer the end of the week feels when this is in place.

Practical next actions

  1. Ask the person, not about them. Have a private conversation: which tasks would stop if you were away for a week, and what would you want to be written down first?
  2. Write the weekly steps in plain language. A simple numbered list of what is done, in what order, with screenshots of the awkward parts. Let the expert review it, so it is accurate and credited to them.
  3. Run a cover test. Ask a colleague to perform one task using only the notes while the expert is present but silent. Fix every gap they hit.
  4. Name a backup for each critical task. One named person, not a vague team. Give them time to practise before they are needed.
  5. Schedule a planned absence. Once cover is tested, encourage the person to take a real day off and see how it goes. Treat the result as information, not as a verdict.
  6. Review leave patterns. Look at who rarely takes leave or works while on leave. Those are the people most likely to be the only one who knows.

The business outcome

When knowledge is shared, absences stop being emergencies. Colleagues can cover a day or a week without a flurry of phone calls. New staff can learn a task faster, and the business can see which steps are worth improving, because they are now written down where others can read them.

The human dividend

The dividend here is presence and predictability. Presence means being fully at the funeral, the clinic appointment or the family lunch, with no part of the mind left at the desk. Predictability means knowing that a sick day will not unravel the week. Dignity follows from both: the person is valued for what they bring, not for how tightly they are bound to the work.

The trade-offs

  • Writing things down costs scarce time. The expert is usually the busiest person, so the documentation effort has to be protected or it will not happen.
  • Some people feel exposed. Sharing knowledge can feel like giving away the thing that makes them valuable. The business needs to show that it values them for more than the secret.
  • Notes are never complete. Experienced judgement is hard to capture, and a backup will still be slower than the expert.
  • Cover has a cost. Training a second person takes their time away from their own work.

Why it matters

Being the only one who knows can look like security, but it often works more like a leash. Loosening it benefits the employee first, who gets a life outside the task, and the business second, which gets resilience it did not have to buy. A short list of steps, a trained backup and a tested day off are modest tools, and they change what absence means.
