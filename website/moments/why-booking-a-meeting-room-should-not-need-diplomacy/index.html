Relief from Friction

Why Booking a Meeting Room Shouldn't Need Diplomacy

When room bookings depend on who asks, who is senior and who sends the best message, time and goodwill drain away. How a visible, simple booking system fixes it.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

There is a particular kind of message that appears in office group chats on a Monday morning. "Is anyone using the boardroom at 2? I know it's booked for Chris but if he's not using it, could we possibly have it for twenty minutes? No stress if not." It is polite, it is hedged, and it takes several rounds to resolve. By the end, three people have spent more attention on who may sit in a room than they will spend on the conversation held inside it.

This piece looks at that small negotiation and at what a simple, visible booking arrangement can take away. It is a hypothesis drawn from general guidance on workplace relationships and workload, and the example is illustrative, not a study of any particular South African office.

When the room becomes a favour

A meeting room is a shared resource with a fixed supply. When there is no agreed way to claim it, claiming becomes social. People ask the office manager, who has a mental diary. They check the whiteboard on the door, which may be wrong. They look at who else is hovering. The booking goes to the person who is senior, persistent or good at being gracious about it, and the others learn to ask only when they have to.

That has three effects. The first is time: a booking that should take ten seconds takes ten minutes of messaging, spread across several people. The second is energy: nobody enjoys asking for a favour that should be a routine, and the quiet worry about being a nuisance accumulates. The third is fairness. Junior staff and new joiners, who are least likely to push, end up holding their conversations in corridors, on the stairs or in the car park.

HSE's management standard on relationships describes an organisation that promotes positive behaviours at work to avoid conflict and ensure fairness. Room allocation seems minor next to the bullying and harassment the standard is mainly concerned with, but it is one of the daily places where fairness is either built into the system or left to personality.

What the booking arrangement has to do

A good arrangement is dull. It does four things and does them without a conversation:

  • Shows availability. Anyone can see, from their desk or phone, whether the room is free at a given time, without asking a person.
  • Takes a claim in seconds. Booking the room is part of creating the meeting, not a separate errand.
  • Treats everyone alike. The same rules apply whatever someone's title, with any exceptions written down in advance.
  • Releases unused time. A booking that is not honoured frees up, so the room does not sit empty behind a name.

Most businesses that use Microsoft 365 or Exchange already have the pieces for this. Microsoft's documentation describes room mailboxes, which are assigned to a physical location such as a conference room, and which users can reserve simply by including the room in a meeting request. It also describes room lists, so that rooms can be grouped by building. Microsoft's own guidance there adds a practical warning: never set a room mailbox as the organiser of a meeting, and include it only as an attendee or location. Google Workspace and other calendar tools have equivalent resource features. The point is not the brand; it is that the room appears in the same place where people already create meetings.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Pretoria accounting practice with fourteen staff and two meeting rooms. Bookings are made through the practice manager, who keeps a paper diary and fields requests by chat. Partners ask by calling out across the office. Trainee accountants do not ask at all; they use the kitchen table when they need to review a file with a client who has arrived.

The practice manager spends a noticeable part of each week on room questions. She is also the single point of failure: when she is on leave, double-bookings appear and a client is once shown into a room where a partner is on a call. Nobody is unkind about it. Everyone is simply spending effort that a calendar would take over.

The practice adds both rooms as resources in its existing calendar, names them clearly, and writes four rules on one page. Rooms are booked by adding them to the meeting invitation. Unaccepted bookings release after fifteen minutes. Client meetings take priority over internal ones, and that is the only priority rule. Anyone may book either room, including trainees. Within a few weeks the paper diary is retired and the practice manager's questions turn into an occasional reminder about the rules.

What simple rules protect

The rules matter as much as the tool. A booking system without rules reproduces the old politics in a new interface, with the senior person's recurring block of every Tuesday afternoon sitting permanently in the calendar. The rules that tend to help are those that a team can state in a sentence: a maximum length for a recurring booking, a release time for unused slots, a clear statement of what outranks what, and one named person who settles disputes.

HSE's guidance on demands is about making sure workloads and working patterns are matched to what people can cope with. The room is a small part of that. A day in which every meeting begins with a search for somewhere to hold it is a day with more demands than the work requires, and the cost is carried by whoever does the searching. Our piece on the minutes between meetings that never add up follows the same arithmetic across a whole day.

The trade-offs

Open booking has downsides. A few people may reserve rooms they never use, or book recurring slots far ahead, and the rest pay for it. Release rules need an owner, and someone has to enforce them without making it personal. A booking tool may cost money or set-up time, particularly for a business that does not already use a suitable calendar. And if a room is genuinely too scarce, even a fair system will leave people without one; in that case the better question may be whether some meetings need a room at all, which our piece on the meeting that could have been a shared record explores.

Practical next actions

  1. For one week, note every time someone asks a person, not a system, whether a room is free. Count the messages and the minutes.
  2. Add each room as a bookable resource in the calendar your team already uses, with a clear name and capacity, and test it with one real meeting.
  3. Write four rules on one page: how to book, when unused bookings release, what outranks what, and who settles disputes. Share the page with everyone, including new joiners.
  4. Set a limit on recurring bookings, such as a review every month, so that standing blocks do not become permanent ownership.
  5. After a month, ask staff who used to avoid asking whether it is easier, and adjust the rules where they say it is not.

A room that anyone can claim in ten seconds does not feel like a policy. It feels like the office working, and that is the whole aim.
