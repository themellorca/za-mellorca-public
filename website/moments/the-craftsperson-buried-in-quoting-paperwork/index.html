The Craftsperson Buried in Quoting Paperwork

A skilled tradesperson spends evenings measuring up, pricing materials and typing quotes, many of which never become work. Why quoting swallows craft time and how templates and better information can give it back.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-13

The joiner finishes a day in the workshop, eats supper, and opens the laptop. Three quotes are waiting: a kitchen, a set of built-in cupboards and a staircase repair. Each needs measurements transferred from a notebook, material prices checked against two suppliers' latest lists, labour estimated and everything typed into a document that looks professional. It is after ten when the last one goes out. Experience says that perhaps one of the three will become work.

This piece looks at the craftsperson whose skill is crowded out by quoting, and at ways to give the time back. It is reasoned from general research on job demands and consumer-protection principles, not from a study of South African trade businesses, and it is not legal advice.

Why quoting takes so long

Quoting looks like a small task until you count its parts. A site visit to measure and understand the job. Checking current material prices, which change often. Calculating quantities and waste. Estimating labour and travel. Writing a clear description of what is and is not included. Formatting it all into something a customer will trust. Following up when the customer does not reply.

For an owner-operator, nobody else can do most of this. The skill that makes them good at the craft is the same skill needed to price it. So quoting falls into the gaps: evenings, weekends, rainy days. The craft itself gets the daylight hours, but the paperwork takes the rest.

Consider a hypothetical two-person tiling business. The owner might visit several sites a week to measure up, then spend evenings building quotes from scratch in a word processor, copying prices from supplier price lists and retyping standard wording about preparation and grout. Most quotes never become work, but each takes the same effort.

Why clear quotes still matter

None of this means quoting should be rushed or vague. South Africa's Consumer Protection Act, as summarised on the government's page, aims to promote fair consumer markets and to improve the information consumers receive. A clear quote that sets out the price, what is included and the conditions protects both the customer and the trade business, and reduces disputes later. The aim is to make clear quotes faster to produce, not to make them less clear.

What the research says about demands

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standard on demands calls for demands that are adequate and achievable within agreed working hours. Many owner-operators have no agreed hours, which is part of the problem: work that does not fit the day spills into the evening by default. Quoting is the most common spill.

The human tax: dignity and time

The time cost is the evenings and weekends that go to paperwork. The dignity cost is subtler. People who choose a trade usually do so because they like making and fixing things. Spending a large share of their working life on admin, much of it for jobs that never happen, can feel like a betrayal of that choice. The Moment on craft businesses spending more time on software than craft looks at the broader admin load. Quoting is often its largest single part.

The Moment on the Saturday morning spent on invoices describes the same spill at the other end of the job, when the work is done and the bill must be sent.

Making quoting faster without making it worse

Most quotes in a trade business are variations on a small number of job types. That makes them good candidates for templates. A template for each common job, with standard wording for inclusions, exclusions and conditions, removes most of the typing. A price list kept current in one place, linked to the template, removes the supplier-list hunting. Standard items, such as square metres of tiling or a standard cupboard unit, priced per unit with known waste, turn calculation into counting.

Quoting tools for trades can do much of this, but a well-built spreadsheet or the quote feature in many accounting packages can go a long way. The guide on calculating the real cost of a repetitive task helps decide how much to invest. And when a quote is accepted, the discovery note on accepted quotes that do not create fulfilment work shows why the quote should feed directly into the job.

Practical next actions

  1. Time your quotes for a month. Note how long each quote takes, from site visit to sending, and whether it became work. This shows the real cost per job won.
  2. Build templates for your top three job types. Write standard wording for inclusions, exclusions, payment terms and conditions once, and reuse it.
  3. Keep one current price list. Hold material and labour rates in one place and update them on a fixed day each month, so every quote uses current prices.
  4. Price in standard units. Where possible, price per square metre, per unit or per standard item, with known allowances for waste and travel.
  5. Qualify before you quote. For larger jobs, have a short conversation about budget and timing before the site visit, so effort goes to the quotes most likely to proceed.

The business outcome

Quotes go out faster, which often wins more work. Prices are more consistent and more likely to reflect current costs, protecting margin. Fewer disputes arise, because standard wording makes inclusions and exclusions clear.

The human dividend

The dividend is time. Evenings and weekends return to the person, and more of the working week goes to the craft they chose. That is not only more efficient. It brings the job back toward what drew them to it in the first place.

The trade-offs

  • Templates take setup time. Building them needs a few focused hours, which are hard to find in a busy season.
  • Standard pricing misses unusual jobs. Complex or unusual work still needs careful individual pricing.
  • Tools cost money. Quoting software adds a subscription that must earn its keep.
  • Speed can feel impersonal. Some customers value a tailored quote, and the template should leave room for a personal note.

Why it matters

A craftsperson's skill is the heart of their business and often of their identity. When quoting paperwork consumes the hours around the work, it slowly takes the craft out of the craft. Making quotes faster is a practical way to give skilled people more of the working life they chose.
