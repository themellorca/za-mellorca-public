When craft businesses spend more time on software than craft

A baker, a furniture maker or a jeweller starts a business to make things well, then finds the week fills up with invoices, quotes and system upkeep. Why that happens and what to change.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-09

Most people who start a craft business did not dream of a better invoicing workflow. They wanted to bake, build, stitch, brew, throw pots or set stones. The business was the vehicle that let them do that work for a living. Yet many owners describe a slow reversal: five years in, the working week is mostly screens, and the making happens in the gaps, early in the morning or on a Sunday.

This piece looks at why that reversal happens, why it matters more for a craft business than for most, and what a small, realistic change looks like. It is a hypothesis about a common pattern, not a verdict on any particular workshop.

Why the craft is the product

In a business that sells a made thing, the maker's hands, eye and judgement are not an overhead. They are what the customer is paying for. Craft production is described as work done by skilled people, often to order and in small quantities, with the maker closely involved in each item. That closeness is the strength of the model and also its weakness: when the skilled person is also the person who answers email, issues invoices and captures stock, every hour of admin is an hour taken directly out of the product.

Compare that to a larger firm, where the same admin would be spread over several roles. In a studio of three, it lands on whoever is least busy, which in practice means whoever is most willing, which is often the owner. The skill and the software share one pair of hands.

How the admin accumulates

Nobody decides to spend a day a week on software. It builds through a series of reasonable steps.

  • A tool for each new need. An online shop needs a store platform, the store needs a payment gateway, the gateway needs a reconciliation routine, and the accounting package needs the figures typed or exported across. Each tool solved something; together they form a small, unplanned system.
  • Regulation arrives with growth. SARS's guidance for small businesses shows how registrations and obligations build up as a business grows, from income tax to VAT and employee taxes. Each one adds a calendar date, a login and a set of records to keep.
  • Customers expect more channels. Orders arrive by message, email, an online form and a market-stall notebook. All of them have to end up in one place before anything can be made.
  • Nobody removes anything. The spreadsheet that once tracked orders is kept 'just in case' after the shop platform took over, so now there are two records that have to be kept in step.

The pattern is similar to the one described in how much manual work is hiding inside a business: individually small tasks, none of which looks worth fixing, together consuming a large share of the week.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a small furniture workshop outside Pietermaritzburg with two craftspeople and a part-time bookkeeper. The owner, a cabinet maker, takes custom orders for dining tables and cupboards. A typical week used to be four days at the bench. Now it goes more like this.

Monday morning goes on replying to quote requests, each of which needs timber prices checked against a supplier's latest list. Tuesday is spent chasing deposits and capturing them against orders in two places, because the accounting package and the order spreadsheet do not talk to each other. Wednesday has an hour lost to a card machine that will not reconcile with the online shop. By Thursday the owner is behind on the one thing only they can do, which is cut and fit the joinery for a client's wedding-gift table, and the work moves to Saturday.

Nothing here is wrong in isolation. The owner is being careful with money, careful with customers and careful with SARS. The cost is that the thing the business exists to do is now the thing that gets squeezed.

The human tax: dignity and time

The first cost is time, and it is easy to measure. If admin and software upkeep take two days of a five-day week, then a business that thinks of itself as a workshop is in practice a back-office with a bench attached. The cost of high-value people doing low-value admin is usually framed in rand terms, and for a maker the rand figure is real: the hours that could have been billable making time are being spent on tasks a bookkeeper could do for less.

The second cost is dignity, and it is harder to see. A person who has spent years developing a skill is entitled to spend most of their working time using it. When they cannot, because a payment has to be matched or a form re-entered, the work stops feeling like their own. It can feel as though the business has quietly swapped the owner's craft for a clerk's job. The article on why purpose gets lost in administrative noise describes how this erodes motivation even when the numbers look fine, and the skilled professional reduced to copy and paste covers the same experience in a larger organisation.

Workload research draws a useful distinction here. The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards treat the demands placed on people, including the volume and pattern of work, as a central factor in work-related stress. They do not say that all demands are bad; they say that unmanageable or poorly matched demands are a risk. For a maker, demands that pull away from the thing they are best at are especially poorly matched.

What could realistically change

The aim is not to eliminate admin. It is to move it so that the owner's making time is protected and the admin that remains is simple enough to be done by someone else, or by a single well-chosen system.

  1. Time the week for a fortnight. Split it into making, selling, admin and fixing tools. Most owners are surprised by the proportions. Do not change anything yet; the point is to see.
  2. Pick one place where an order lives. Every request, whatever its source, gets entered once into a single list. If an online shop is the main route, let it be the record; if not, a single shared sheet will do. Retire the duplicates.
  3. Connect the two things that matter most. Often that is the shop and the accounting package. Even a simple export-and-import routine, done weekly, is better than retyping each sale. For a deeper view, when copy and paste is really an integration problem explains how to tell which handoffs are worth connecting.
  4. Hand the repeatable work to someone who enjoys it. A part-time bookkeeper or an administrative assistant for a few hours a week can often cost less than the making time they free. Capture the rules (how to quote, how to chase a deposit) in a single page they can follow.
  5. Protect a making block. Choose mornings, or two full days, where nothing but making happens and messages are answered afterwards. The quiet power of choosing when to respond is a good model for how to set this up without losing customers.

The business outcome

The workshop that makes more, at the same quality, earns more without raising prices. Quotes go out faster, deposits are tracked reliably, and the SARS calendar stops arriving as a surprise. Just as important, the owner can see where time actually goes and make deliberate choices about growth, rather than discovering at the end of the year that the business grew only in admin.

The human dividend

The dividend is time and optionality. Time is the plain one: more hours at the bench, the oven or the kiln. Optionality is subtler. An owner who is not drowning in upkeep can decide to take on a larger commission, teach a class, say no to a client who is not a fit, or simply close early on a Friday. Without that room the business is something that happens to the owner; with it, it becomes something the owner chooses.

The trade-offs

  • Fewer tools means fewer features. Consolidating onto one platform may remove a clever add-on that someone liked.
  • Setting things up takes making time first. A day spent tidying records is a day not at the bench, and the payback comes later.
  • Some admin is permanent. Tax and compliance cannot be wished away; they can only be made simpler and less dependent on one person.
  • Handing work over costs money and trust. A bookkeeper or assistant is a real expense, and it takes time to be comfortable letting someone else touch the customer relationship.

Why it matters

A craft business is a way of earning a living from skill. When most of the week goes to software and paperwork, the business keeps running but the reason for having it fades. Moving admin out of the maker's hands, or simplifying it until it is small, is how the work gets to be the work again.
