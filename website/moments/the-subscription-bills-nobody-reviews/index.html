The subscription bills nobody reviews

Small recurring charges pile up unseen until they squeeze a business's margin. Why subscriptions go unreviewed, and a review short enough to actually happen.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-09

Somewhere in most small businesses there is a list nobody has read in a year. It is the list of everything that leaves the bank account on the first of the month, or on the fifteenth, or once a year without warning: software licences, cloud storage, a design tool, a second video-call platform, a security product that someone trialled and forgot, a membership that belonged to a colleague who has left. Each charge is small. Together, they form a steady drain that nobody chose and nobody is watching.

This piece looks at why subscription bills so often go unreviewed, what that does to a business's breathing room, and how to make a periodic review small enough to actually happen. It is a hypothesis about a common pattern, not an accusation against any particular owner.

Why small recurring charges escape attention

Subscriptions are designed to be easy to start and easy to forget. General reference material describes the subscription business model as one in which a customer pays a recurring price for continued access to a product or service, and in which the seller benefits when the customer remains subscribed. That is a sound model for many suppliers. It also means that inertia is part of how the model works.

In a small business, several things make the forgetting worse.

  • No single owner. The tool was bought by whoever needed it, on whichever card was at hand. Nobody is responsible for the whole list.
  • Amounts below the line of attention. A charge of a few hundred rand does not trigger an approval, so it is never discussed. A dozen of them is a different matter.
  • Foreign-currency billing. Many tools bill in dollars, so the rand amount changes slightly each month. The variation makes charges harder to recognise and easier to wave through.
  • Annual renewals. A yearly charge appears once, long after the decision to buy, and arrives with no context.
  • Departures. When a person leaves, the tools registered in their name may remain active and billed. Mellorca's article on orphaned software subscriptions after an employee leaves covers this specific leak.

What the evidence can and cannot say

No South African survey is cited here, and it would be a mistake to invent a percentage of wasted spend. What can be said is narrower.

Reference material on software as a service describes a model in which software is licensed on a subscription basis and hosted centrally, usually paid per user or per tier. That structure means cost scales with headcount and features, so a business that grows, shrinks or changes its tools without adjusting its subscriptions pays for a configuration it no longer uses.

Cash flow, as general references define it, is the movement of money into and out of a business, and its timing matters as much as its total. Small, regular debits reduce the margin between income and obligations every month, and they do so most painfully in the lean months when the margin is thinnest. A business with irregular income, as many South African small businesses have, feels fixed costs more sharply than the average annual figure suggests. That is an inference, not a measured finding.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Port Elizabeth marketing agency with nine staff. The owner pays most business expenses from a single credit card, and the bookkeeper captures the statement at month end, coding each line by supplier name.

When the owner finally asks for a list of recurring charges, the bookkeeper finds thirty-one. Several are expected: the accounting package, email hosting, the accounts payable portal. Others are not. Two project management tools are in use by different teams. A stock photo plan renewed in February for a campaign that ended last year. A transcription service charged in dollars by someone who left in the spring. A password manager billed for fifteen seats when there are nine employees. A domain-privacy add-on for a domain the agency no longer uses.

No single item is outrageous. The total is more than the monthly cost of a part-time employee. More to the point, the owner cannot answer the simplest question: which of these would we miss if it disappeared tomorrow? Nobody had ever been asked.

The human tax: financial resilience

The cost is not mainly the money, though the money matters. It is the loss of resilience. A business that carries unexamined fixed costs has less room to absorb a late-paying client, a quiet quarter or an unexpected repair. The owner who lies awake over cash flow is often worrying about a margin that is thinner than it needs to be because of costs they did not choose. The Mellorca article on the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake describes how that feels from the inside, and when late invoices become late salaries shows where it can end up.

There is also a quieter cost: a vague, unexamined sense that the business is leaking, with no way to tell where. That uncertainty is itself a burden.

What could realistically change

The goal is not a procurement department. It is a review short enough that it will be done.

  1. Build the list once. Take twelve months of bank and card statements and pull out every recurring charge. Record the supplier, the amount in rand, how often it is billed, who uses it and who decided to buy it.
  2. Give each item an owner. A name beside each subscription. If no one will claim it, that is useful information.
  3. Ask one question per line. Did anyone use this in the last ninety days? Sort the answers into keep, reduce or cancel.
  4. Put renewal dates on a calendar. Annual renewals are where a review has the most leverage. The article on how software renewal dates can force better technology decisions explains how to use them as decision points.
  5. Repeat quarterly, for fifteen minutes. The first review is the long one. After that, the task is to look for what is new. Mellorca's analysis of automatic software renewals without a value review and why cheap software can become expensive operations offers more on where to look.

The business outcome

Recurring costs become known and deliberate. Money freed up by cancelling unused tools can go to something the business actually needs. Duplicate tools are consolidated, and staff changes trigger a check on what that person had access to and what is being paid for in their name.

The human dividend

The dividend is financial breathing room: a wider margin between what comes in and what must go out, and the calm of knowing what the business is committed to paying. For an owner, this is often the difference between reacting to the month and planning it.

The trade-offs

  • Cancelling can backfire. A tool that looks idle may be used quarterly for an important task. Ask before cutting.
  • Annual plans cost less per month but reduce flexibility. Locking in a discount may be the wrong move for a business that is unsure of its needs.
  • The review takes time. The first pass is tedious, and the savings may be modest.
  • Switching has its own cost. Moving data and retraining people can outweigh a small saving.

Why it matters

Subscription bills nobody reviews are not a disaster in any one month. They are a slow reduction in a business's room to manoeuvre. A short, regular look at the list costs little and gives an owner back some of the breathing room they had not realised they were paying away.
