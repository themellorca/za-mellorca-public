The Team That Fixes the Same Mistake Every Week

Every Friday the same report is wrong in the same way, and the same people correct it. Why recurring errors persist, what repeated correction does to a team, and how to design the error out.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-13

Every Friday morning, the same thing happens. The weekly sales report arrives, and two of the branch codes are mapped to the wrong region. The finance assistant knows exactly which ones. She opens the spreadsheet, moves the figures, saves a corrected copy and sends it on. It takes twenty minutes. She has done it for eighteen months. Nobody has fixed the mapping, because the correction always gets done.

This piece is about recurring errors that become routine corrections, and what that ritual does to the people who perform it. It is reasoned from general human-factors guidance, not from measured data in South African firms.

Why recurring errors survive

A one-off error gets investigated. A recurring one gets absorbed. The first time it happens, someone corrects it. The second time, they recognise it. By the tenth time, the correction is part of the process. It is reliable, it is fast, and it hides the problem from everyone except the person doing it.

Three conditions keep it going:

  • The cost is spread thin. Twenty minutes a week never feels worth a project, even though it adds up to more than two working days a year, plus the risk on the weeks the correction is missed.
  • The cause sits elsewhere. The mapping table is maintained by someone in another department, or by an outside supplier, so the person who sees the error cannot change it.
  • The correction becomes knowledge. The fix lives in one person's head. When they are away, the wrong report goes out, and someone notices only later.

What human-factors guidance says

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance on human failure is written for safety-critical industries, but its logic applies to office work. It distinguishes slips and lapses, mistakes and violations, and stresses that slips cannot be trained away: improved design is the way to make them less likely. It recommends building error-tolerant systems, preferably by redesigning the task or equipment, and involving workers in designing tasks and procedures. It also warns against stopping at operator error in investigations, instead of asking why the failure happened.

A weekly correction is a design problem dressed as a routine. The error comes from a step that reliably produces it, such as a mapping table, a template with a wrong default or an import that drops a field. Fixing the step removes the error for everyone. Correcting the output removes it only for this week.

The HSE's Management Standard on control adds that employees should have a say in how their work is done. The person who corrects the error every week usually knows exactly where it comes from. What they lack is the authority or time to fix it.

The human tax: dignity and time

The time cost is clear. The dignity cost is less obvious. Correcting the same error every week sends a message: the business would rather you fix this forever than spend the effort to fix it once. The work is necessary, but it is not valued, and it is not visible. When the correction is done, nobody notices. When it is missed, someone is blamed.

The Moment on why system-caused errors end up logged as human error describes that blame. Over time, the team may simply stop raising the issue, as described in why teams stop suggesting improvements. They have learned that correction is expected and change is not.

From correcting to designing out

The change is to treat every recurring correction as a defect to be traced. Ask where the wrong value first enters, who owns that step, and what change would stop it. Often the fix is small: a corrected mapping table, a validation rule that rejects a bad code, a template default changed. Sometimes it needs a supplier or another department to act, which needs a manager to make the case.

The guide on finding rework inside a business process sets out a method for spotting these loops, and the impact analysis on fixing preventable mistakes counts what they cost.

Practical next actions

  1. Ask for the correction list. In a team meeting, ask everyone to name any correction they make more than once a month. Write down each one with how long it takes and how often.
  2. Trace one error to its source. Pick the most frequent correction and follow the wrong value back to where it first appears. Name the step and its owner.
  3. Fix the step, not the output. Agree a change at the source, such as a corrected table, a validation rule or a template change, and set a date for it.
  4. Involve the person who corrects it. Ask the team member who knows the error best to describe what correct looks like and to check the first few runs after the fix.
  5. Keep a simple recurring-error log. Record each new recurring error when it is first noticed, with an owner and a target date, and review the log monthly until it is empty.

The business outcome

Reports are right the first time. Decisions are made on correct numbers even when the usual corrector is away. Time spent on weekly fixes moves to work that adds value, and the business carries less hidden dependence on one person's memory.

The human dividend

The dividend is time and predictability. The team stops spending part of every week on the same repair, and can trust the report instead of checking it line by line. The person who used to correct it has their knowledge used to remove the problem, which is a different and more respectful kind of recognition.

The trade-offs

  • Root causes take time to find. Tracing an error can take longer than correcting it many times, and the payback comes later.
  • Some causes are outside your control. Errors in supplier or customer data may need negotiation, not just a fix.
  • Changes can introduce new errors. A fix should be tested and watched for a few cycles.
  • Routines feel safe. Some people find comfort in a known correction, and may need reassurance that the new process will hold.

Why it matters

A team that fixes the same mistake every week is being asked to compensate for a design that nobody will change. Removing the mistake at its source is a small act of respect: it says that their time and attention are worth more than an endless repair.
