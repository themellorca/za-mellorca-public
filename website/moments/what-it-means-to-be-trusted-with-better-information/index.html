What It Means to Be Trusted With Better Information

Reports that reach only the top leave everyone else guessing. What changes when the people doing the work see the figures too, and what has to be true first.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

In many small and mid-sized businesses the numbers travel one way. Staff capture them, a bookkeeper or manager compiles them, and the owner receives a report. The people who produced the figures rarely see what became of them. When a customer is late, a delivery slips or a target is missed, they hear about it as a verdict, not as a figure they could have acted on earlier.

This piece looks at what it means for an employee to be trusted with better information: current, accurate figures about their own part of the work, shared as a matter of course. It is a hypothesis built from general research on job control and support, not a finding about any particular South African employer.

Information as a form of trust

Sharing a figure says something. It says that the person is expected to understand it, to question it and to do something sensible with it. Withholding the same figure says the opposite, even when the reason is innocent: the report is awkward to produce, the owner is protective of margins, or nobody has thought about it.

Consider a hypothetical dispatch team in a regional distributor. Every Monday the owner reviews a sheet showing late deliveries by driver. The drivers never see it. They learn about a bad week from a raised voice, and the supervisor spends Tuesday defending decisions that were reasonable given what each driver knew on the road. Nobody in this picture is acting in bad faith. The information simply sits in the wrong place.

The same pattern shows up wherever there is reporting: the debtors clerk who does not see the age analysis she feeds, the shop-floor lead who does not see scrap rates, the receptionist who never learns how many appointments end in no-shows. Each holds part of the picture and none holds the figure that would let them improve it.

What the research says about control and support

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standards for work-related stress treat control as one of six areas that shape how people cope with work. Its standard on control describes the aim that employees can have a say in the way they do their work, are encouraged to use their skills and initiative, and are consulted over their work patterns. Having a say is hard to do well without seeing the current state of the work.

The companion standard on support lists, among its states to be achieved, that employees know how to access the resources they need to do their job and receive regular and constructive feedback. A figure that arrives weeks late, or only in a manager's summary, is weak feedback. A figure that is current and visible is feedback in a form that people can use without waiting to be told.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work names low job control among the working conditions that pose a risk to mental health, alongside excessive workloads. It also describes decent work as giving a sense of confidence, purpose and achievement. Better information is not the whole of control, but it is one of the practical preconditions for it.

Where trust with information goes wrong

Trusting people with information is not the same as handing everyone every number. Three mistakes are common.

  • Sharing without context. A raw figure with no definition invites argument. If two people mean different things by "late", a shared report produces a quarrel instead of a decision.
  • Sharing without safety. If the first use of a visible figure is to find someone to blame, people will learn to stop recording it honestly. Transparency that feels like surveillance does the reverse of what it promises. The note on when monitoring replaces trust in the reporting line explores that boundary.
  • Sharing what should not be shared. Salaries, personal records and customer details have their own rules. South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act sets conditions on how personal information is processed, so what a team sees should be limited to what its work needs.

The aim is relevant information, shown at the right level, without the detail that exposes other people.

The human tax: dignity and confidence

The first cost of being kept in the dark is dignity. A capable adult who is asked to hit a target but not shown the running total is being managed like a component, not worked with like a colleague. The message may be unintended, but it is received.

The second cost is confidence. People who cannot check their own work against a figure tend to defend it, or to second-guess it, because they have nothing firmer to stand on. Many decisions that look like reluctance are really people protecting themselves from being wrong in front of someone who can see more than they can.

There is also a quieter cost to the person at the top. An owner who is the only reader of the numbers becomes the only person who can act on them, which is how a Monday sheet turns into a week of interruptions. The piece on the difference between being informed and being in control makes the same point from the owner's side.

What could realistically change

Better information does not need a new system to begin. It needs a decision about who sees what, a plain definition of each figure and a rhythm for looking at it. A shared sheet that refreshes daily, a weekly one-page summary, or a simple dashboard in the accounting package can be enough. The harder part is agreeing what the figure means, and that conversation often exposes assumptions that were never written down.

Where reports are assembled by hand, the first benefit of automation is usually not speed. It is that the figure can be trusted enough to be shown. The note on why reliable information comes before real agency sets out that order of events, and what changes when people can see their own numbers describes the effect on ordinary working days.

Practical next actions

  1. List who sees which report. For the five reports your business relies on, write down who produces them, who reads them and who never sees them. The gap between producers and readers is the starting point.
  2. Pick one figure per team. Choose a single measure that a team directly influences, such as late deliveries, open queries or days to invoice. Write one sentence defining it and agree it with the team before anyone sees a result.
  3. Show it before it is judged. Share the figure for a month with no consequences attached, and ask the team what they notice. Use the first month to test whether the number is right, not to assess people.
  4. Set the limits in writing. Decide which details stay restricted, such as individual pay or customer information, and check the rule against the Protection of Personal Information Act where personal data is involved.
  5. Ask for decisions, not reports. After a month, ask each team what it would change given the figure. Act on at least one suggestion visibly, so the information is seen to be worth sharing.

The business outcome

When the people closest to the work can see the figures, problems tend to be caught earlier and at lower cost, and fewer of them have to travel up to the owner. Meetings shift from reporting what happened to deciding what to do. The owner reads fewer rescue messages because the team can often see for itself what is drifting.

The human dividend

The dividend is autonomy. A person who sees how the work is going can choose how to respond, which task to take first and when to raise a concern, without waiting to be told. That choice is a form of respect, and it tends to show up as steadier confidence in the person who has it. What people do with their added room is their own affair, whether that is doing the job better, finishing earlier or taking on something harder.

The trade-offs

  • Visibility can sting. A figure that reveals a weak week for one person can feel exposing, so introduce it with care and with a clear purpose.
  • Bad data does harm faster when shared. A wrong number shown to everyone erodes trust in the whole exercise. Check the figure before widening access.
  • Privacy limits are real. Not everything can be shared, and the boundaries need to be explained so that restriction is not mistaken for secrecy.
  • Some people prefer not to see. Not every employee wants more figures. Offer access without making a performance of it.

Why it matters

Information is one of the cheapest forms of respect a business can extend, and one of the easiest to withhold by default. Trusting people with better information is not a gift. It is a working arrangement in which the person closest to the problem can see it, and in which the person at the top can stop being the only one who does.
