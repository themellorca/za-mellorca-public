The teacher who became a data-entry clerk

When a trained educator spends the week capturing marks, attendance and forms, the loss is not only hours. A look at what repeated capture does to professional dignity, and what can realistically be changed.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-08

Most teachers can say, quite precisely, why they chose the job. A subject they love. A particular teacher who changed their life. A conviction that a child who is seen and taught well will do better than one who is not. Very few say they chose teaching because they wanted to capture marks into a spreadsheet at nine at night.

This piece uses teaching as a lens, because the pattern is easy to see there, but the same thing happens to nurses, social workers, engineers, bookkeepers and technicians. A person trained for skilled, judgement-heavy work gradually becomes the human link between systems that do not talk to each other. The question here is what that does to a person's sense of their own work, and what a business or institution can do about it.

An illustrative week

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Grade 5 teacher at a well-run school in Gauteng, with about thirty-five learners in the class. The teacher is good at the job. Parents like them. Learners ask for them by name.

In this scenario the week looks like this. Attendance is marked on paper in the morning, then keyed into the school's administration system after break. Assessment marks are recorded in a mark book, typed into a spreadsheet, then re-entered into the report-writing tool at the end of the term. Incident notes go into one form, parent communication into a group chat, and a monthly summary for the department is assembled by hand from all of the above.

None of those tasks is absurd. Schools do need attendance records, assessments and communication with parents. The difficulty is that the same facts are typed three or four times, by the person least suited to being a typist, in the hours when they would otherwise be marking, planning or resting.

What the evidence does and does not tell us

It would be too easy to claim that research proves capture work destroys professional identity. It does not. What the evidence shows is narrower, and still useful.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards for work-related stress treat control and role clarity as two of the main areas where working conditions affect wellbeing. The standard on role asks whether people understand their role and whether the organisation avoids giving them conflicting roles. The standard on control concerns how much say people have in the way they do their work. These are general workplace standards, not teacher-specific findings, but they give a vocabulary for what many people describe informally: the job they were hired to do and the job they actually do have drifted apart.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index adds a picture of how a modern working day is spent. In its analysis of Microsoft 365 data, people spent more of their time communicating than creating, and large shares of surveyed workers said they struggled to find the time and energy to do their job. The data comes from knowledge workers in office software, so it describes teachers only loosely. It is better read as evidence that coordination work expands to fill the time available.

Neither source proves that any one teacher, or any one bookkeeper, feels diminished. That is a hypothesis, and it is worth treating as one. The concern is the person whose training sits unused while the keyboard gets the hours.

The human tax: dignity and time

The time tax is easy to count. If a teacher spends five hours a week re-capturing information that already exists somewhere in digital form, that is roughly two hundred hours across a school year. Those hours come from marking, preparing, supporting struggling learners, or rest.

The dignity tax is harder to count and should be described carefully. For some people, being paid and trained for professional judgement but spending most of the day on transcription can feel like being used below their capability. It can also change how they see themselves. The job becomes something to get through, rather than something that expresses what they can do. Others experience nothing of the kind, so treat it as an operational signal, not a universal feeling.

A related theme appears in the skilled professional reduced to copy and paste, and the wider effect on purpose in why purpose gets lost in administrative noise. This piece focuses on the specific case where the person's professional title and daily activity no longer match.

Why the clerk work lands on the professional

It is rarely a deliberate decision. Several ordinary forces push the work onto the person closest to the facts.

  • They are the source of the information. The teacher knows who was absent, so the teacher records it. The system was designed around the record, not around the person.
  • Nobody owns the connection between systems. If attendance, marks and reports live in separate tools, someone must carry data between them. It tends to be whoever is nearest.
  • Capture is cheap to request and expensive to carry. A department that asks for one more monthly summary rarely sees the hours that summary costs.

Mellorca's guide to the difference between necessary human work and avoidable manual work offers a practical test: is this task needed, and does it need a person with this person's skills to do it?

What could realistically change

The fix is seldom a single new product. In a school, a clinic, a workshop or a small firm, the sequence tends to be similar.

  • Capture once, at the source. If a fact is entered where it first arises, such as attendance on a tablet or marks in a shared register, it should not be typed again. Where tools cannot share data, a scheduled import or a simple export-and-load routine is often enough.
  • Question each recurring report. Ask who reads it, what decision it supports, and whether the data could be pulled from records that already exist. Some reports can simply stop.
  • Give the connection between systems an owner. A named person, ideally not the most highly trained one, is responsible for how data moves from one tool to another. Mellorca's article on how to find the manual work nobody is measuring describes a way to make that work visible first.
  • Ask the professional. The person doing the capture usually knows which steps are redundant. Involving them also restores a little of the control that the HSE's standard on control describes as important.

The first consequence, and the business outcome

The first consequence is simple: the trained person spends more of the week doing the thing they were trained for. In a school that may mean more feedback to learners. In a firm it may mean more client work, better quality checks or more time with a junior colleague who needs coaching.

The outcome for the organisation is that its most expensive skills are used on the work that needs them, and that capture errors, which creep in whenever the same fact is typed several times, become less frequent.

The human dividend

The dividend here is time returned, and with it a measure of dignity: the chance to spend working hours on work that matches the person's training and own sense of purpose. For one person that might be teaching. For another it might be going home at a reasonable hour because the capture no longer follows them there. The aim is not to prescribe what recovered time should be used for, only to make the choice available again.

The trade-offs

  • Someone still has to do the work. Moving capture from a teacher to an administrator relieves one person and loads another. That may still be the right trade, but it should be a conscious one.
  • Integration costs money and attention. Connecting systems takes budget and disruption that many schools and small firms struggle to find.
  • Some capture protects accountability. Records of attendance, assessment and incidents exist for good reasons. The aim is to reduce duplicate typing, not to weaken the record.
  • Data protection still applies. Moving learner or customer information between tools has to respect the Protection of Personal Information Act, and that deserves proper thought rather than a quick export to a personal spreadsheet.

Why it matters

A skilled person turned into a clerk is a quiet loss. Nobody resigns on the day it happens, and the organisation can look perfectly healthy. Yet the capability it hired, and the motivation that came with it, gradually go unused.

Mellorca's interpretation is modest. Some administration is necessary and deserves respect. But a great deal of it exists only because systems are disconnected, and that is a design problem rather than a fixed cost of doing business. Removing it will not guarantee anyone a sense of purpose, but it can stop standing in the way.
