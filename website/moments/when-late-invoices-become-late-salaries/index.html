When Late Invoices Become Late Salaries

Salaries are due on a fixed date; customer payments are not. How a small business's own invoicing and collection habits can decide whether a late client becomes a late payday for its staff.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-07

Payday is one of the few moments in working life that almost everyone shares. Debit orders are timed around it. Rent, school fees, transport money and the grocery run are planned against it. For many households the date is not a rough guide; it is the hinge on which the month turns.

So when a small business tells its staff that salaries will be a few days late this month, the message travels a long way. It reaches people who did nothing to cause the delay and who have far less room to absorb it than the business does.

This piece looks at one route by which that happens. Not the business that is failing, and not the employer who is careless with people's pay, but the ordinary, solvent small business whose salaries depend on customers paying on time, and whose own way of invoicing and collecting makes that dependence tighter than it needs to be.

A fixed obligation meeting an unfixed income

On one side of the month, the obligations are clear. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act requires an employer to pay remuneration no later than seven days after the end of the period for which it is payable. SARS expects the PAYE deducted from those salaries to be paid within seven days after the end of the month in which it was deducted. These dates do not move because a client is late.

On the other side, income is far less predictable. Public figures give one view of how far apart the two can drift. According to a SAnews report on National Treasury's third-quarter 2025/26 data, national and provincial departments had 90,856 invoices older than 30 days still unpaid, worth R15.5 billion, and paid a further 89,499 invoices late. The Public Service Commission's Anele Gxoyiya, quoted in that report, linked non-payment to small businesses having to borrow, cut staff or close.

Those figures concern government as a customer, and they should not be read as a description of every client relationship. What they illustrate is the structural shape of the problem: a small business can carry fixed, legally timed obligations to its people while waiting on income that arrives when someone else decides.

Mellorca has already written about what the end of the month can feel like from the owner's chair, in the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake. This piece turns the camera around. It asks what happens further down the line, to the people who are paid last and told last.

Where the avoidable part of the gap comes from

A business cannot make a slow customer pay. But it can make its own part of the cycle slower or faster, and in many small firms that internal part is larger than it looks.

  • Invoices go out late. Work is finished on the 3rd, but the invoice is prepared on the 18th, when someone finally has time. The customer's 30-day terms only start then.
  • Follow-up depends on memory. Nobody is formally responsible for chasing overdue accounts, so it happens when the owner remembers, often close to payday.
  • Nobody sees the collision coming. Salaries, PAYE and supplier payments are known weeks ahead, but they are rarely laid against expected receipts in one view. The shortfall is discovered rather than forecast.

Each of these mechanisms has its own commercial cost, explored in Mellorca's work on late invoicing and manual billing and on cash collection that depends on someone remembering to follow up. The human question here is different: when the gap is discovered late, who absorbs it?

An illustrative month

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Bloemfontein electrical contractor with eighteen staff. Most are paid monthly on the 25th. About a third of the firm's income comes from two larger clients with 30-day terms, one of whom is regularly late.

Job cards come back to the office in batches, so invoices for work done early in the month often go out in the third week. Overdue accounts are chased when the owner checks the bank balance, usually around the 20th. In a month when the larger client slips, the owner finds out on the 22nd that salaries cannot all be paid on the 25th without an overdraft that is already close to its limit.

Several things then happen at once. The owner phones the client, the bank and perhaps a supplier. Someone has to decide whether everyone is paid a few days late or whether some people are paid first. And somebody has to tell the staff.

This is a hypothesis about how the pressure forms in businesses like this, not a measured finding. But the mechanism is concrete enough to test: how many days pass between work completed and invoice sent, and how many days before payday the shortfall becomes visible?

The human tax: financial resilience, passed down the line

For the employees, the tax is financial resilience. A salary that arrives four days late can mean a returned debit order and its fee, a conversation with a landlord, borrowing from family or a short-term loan at a high cost. People with savings may barely notice. People without them may notice for months. It would be wrong to claim that every employee experiences this the same way, but it is fair to say that the delay lands hardest on those with the least margin.

There is a second, quieter cost on both sides of the conversation. Being told your pay will be late can unsettle trust in the job itself. Having to tell people their pay will be late is, for many owners, one of the hardest parts of running a business. Neither experience is improved by learning about the shortfall three days before payday.

It is worth being plain about the law, too. A late customer does not change an employer's obligations under the Basic Conditions of Employment Act. Better systems do not create that obligation, but they make it far easier to meet.

What could realistically change

None of the useful interventions require a large project, and several are not technology at all.

  • Invoice at completion, not at convenience. Agree that finishing the job triggers the invoice, whether through a job system or a simple daily routine. Every day saved here is a day earlier that money can arrive.
  • Give collection an owner and a calendar. A named person, reminders at fixed intervals, and an escalation step well before payday.
  • Put payroll on the same page as receivables. A short forward view that lays salaries, PAYE and key supplier payments against expected receipts. Accounting tools can help: Microsoft's documentation for Business Central, for example, describes a cash flow forecast that combines receivables and payables to show when a surplus or deficit might occur. A disciplined spreadsheet can do the same job in a small firm.
  • Decide in advance what happens in a short month. Whether that is a standing overdraft facility, a reserve built in better months, or an agreed order of payments, the decision is calmer when made early.

These steps cannot fix a customer who pays late. They can separate the delay that comes from outside from the delay the business adds itself, and they can move the moment of discovery from the 22nd to the 5th.

The first consequence, and the business outcome

The immediate change is earlier sight. If a shortfall is visible three weeks ahead, the owner can chase a debtor while there is still time for payment to clear, arrange finance on ordinary terms, or move a discretionary purchase.

The business outcome is fewer emergencies and fewer broken promises. Salaries are paid on time more often, and when they cannot be, staff are told early and honestly rather than at the last minute.

The human dividend

The dividend here is financial breathing room, and it belongs mostly to people who never see the invoicing system. An employee whose salary arrives on the date it was promised can keep the small commitments a household is built on. That is not wealth, and it is not a promise that pay will never be late. It is predictability where predictability matters most.

For the owner, the dividend is fewer of the conversations nobody wants to have, and more of the ones that can be had in good time.

The trade-offs

  • Firmer collection can strain relationships. Regular reminders may feel abrupt to long-standing clients, and some owners rightly prefer to handle sensitive accounts personally.
  • Earlier visibility brings earlier worry. Seeing a shortfall three weeks out is better than seeing it three days out, but it is not comfortable.
  • Someone has to do the work. If collection simply becomes another task for the owner, the gain is smaller.
  • Reserves cost something. Money held back for a short month is money not used for growth.

Why it matters

A late invoice is a commercial problem. A late salary is a household problem. The distance between the two can be shorter than it looks, and much of what closes it is ordinary operational discipline rather than anything dramatic.

Mellorca's reading is narrow. Where a business's own invoicing and collection add days to the cycle, tightening them will not guarantee a good month. It can, however, protect the one date in the month that many employees have built everything else around.
