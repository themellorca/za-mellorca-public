Work and Dignity

Why Repetitive Work Hides Real Talent

When a capable person spends their week on routine capture and checking, the business never sees what else they can do. How to make room for it.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

Every business has people whose best abilities it has never seen. They are not hiding anything. They simply spend forty hours a week doing tasks that do not call on those abilities, and nobody has had a reason to look further. The accounts clerk who could spot patterns in the figures is re-keying invoices. The receptionist who explains things beautifully is filing. The talent is real, but the job description gives it no place to appear.

This piece looks at how repetitive finance and admin work conceals what people can do, and what a business can change so that capability can surface. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on job design, not a finding about any specific South African firm, and the scenarios are illustrative.

How routine work narrows what the business can see

Managers form their view of a person from what the person does. If the work is capturing supplier invoices, the evidence the manager gathers is about accuracy and speed. It says nothing about whether the clerk can explain a variance, design a better layout, or deal calmly with an angry customer. Those abilities need a task that asks for them.

There is a second effect. Repetitive work leaves little room for discretion, so the person has nothing to show except compliance. Job design research often discusses this under the idea of job enrichment, which gives people more varied, complete and autonomous tasks; the overview of job enrichment summarises where the idea comes from and how it has been used. The point is not that all routine is bad. It is that a job made only of routine has no place for the rest of the person.

The cost to the person

For the employee, the cost is mostly invisible. They are doing the work well, they are paid, and nobody complains. But the working day teaches them something about themselves: that the business wants their hands and not their mind. Over time, three things tend to follow.

  • Dignity. A person who knows they could do more, and is never asked, can quietly come to doubt that they could.
  • Optionality. Their next step is unclear. Without visible evidence of broader skill, promotion, a new role or a move elsewhere becomes harder to argue for.
  • Engagement. Effort drifts towards the minimum needed. This is often misread as a lack of ambition when it is a reasonable response to a job with no room in it.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards for work-related stress list control among the main conditions of a healthy job: how much say people have in the way they do their work. The page on control in the management standards describes employees having a say in how their work is done and being able to use their skills. Work with almost no discretion scores poorly on both.

The cost to the business

The business pays for the same thing from the other side. It pays a skilled person to do a task a better-designed process could do, and it never discovers the analyst, trainer or process improver in its own payroll. The cost articles on high-value employees doing low-value admin and accepting repetitive admin that was never redesigned put numbers and structure around the same pattern. The related piece on the skilled professional reduced to copy and paste looks at it for people who already hold professional qualifications.

An illustrative case

Consider, as a hypothetical example, a Polokwane agricultural-supplies wholesaler with twenty-five staff. A credit controller spends most of her week reconciling customer payments against statements by hand, line by line, because the bank file and the accounting package label references differently. She is fast and accurate, and no one thinks of her as anything other than dependable.

When the owner asks, as an experiment, whether she can explain why a handful of accounts are always late, she produces within a week a clear note showing that most late payers share one delivery route and one invoice-format problem. The insight was always available. The week was simply never free enough to produce it.

Making room for what people can do

The fix is not to hand everyone a grand new title. It is to reduce the routine enough that other abilities have a chance to appear, and to ask questions that call on them. Sometimes that means redesigning the routine work. Sometimes it means a small, bounded project. Sometimes it is just asking.

Practical next actions

  1. List what each person does in a typical week. Split it into routine capture or checking, judgement calls, and anything involving other people. If the middle and last categories are nearly empty, the role is hiding its holder.
  2. Cost the routine. Use the method in how to calculate the real cost of a repetitive task to see how many hours a month go on one task, then ask whether a setting, an import or a simpler form could remove half of them.
  3. Ask an open question. Put it to the person directly: what do you notice about this process that nobody has asked you about?
  4. Offer a small, bounded project. Two to four weeks, with a clear question, such as why certain accounts pay late. Protect the time, and treat the output as a real deliverable.
  5. Return the time on purpose. When a routine task is simplified, decide in advance what the freed hours will be used for, or they will be absorbed by more routine.
  6. Record what you learn. Note the strengths the project revealed. Use them in reviews, role design and succession thinking.

The business outcome

A business that surfaces hidden capability gets better analysis, fewer recurring errors and a bench of people ready for larger roles. It also tends to keep them, because people are less likely to leave a job that is growing than one that has stopped asking things of them.

The human dividend

The dividend is optionality together with dignity. Optionality means the person has real choices about where their career can go because they have visible evidence of what they can do. Dignity means the business has noticed, in practical terms, that they are more than the task. What the person chooses to do with that freedom is their own call; some will want a bigger role, others simply a more interesting week.

The trade-offs

  • Not all routine can be removed. Someone still has to capture, check and file, and some people prefer steady work to variety.
  • Redesign takes time and money. Fixing the routine may need a tool change or a process rewrite that competes with other priorities.
  • Raised expectations. Showing people what they can do may create hopes for promotion or pay that the business cannot yet meet.
  • Not every hidden talent is a fit. A project may reveal that the person's strengths lie outside what the business needs, which is useful to know but may be awkward.

Why it matters

The talent in a business is rarely missing. It is more often unused because the work gives it nowhere to go. Reducing the routine and asking better questions costs less than hiring, and it tells people that their abilities are of interest to the people they work for.
