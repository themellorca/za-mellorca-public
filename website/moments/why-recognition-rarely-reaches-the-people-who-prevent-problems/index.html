Why Recognition Rarely Reaches the People Who Prevent Problems

The person who fixes a crisis is thanked. The person whose checks meant there was no crisis usually is not. Why prevention goes unseen and how to make it visible.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

A server fails on a Monday morning and the IT contractor restores it by lunch. The owner thanks them publicly. Three months earlier a quiet colleague had set up the backup that made the restore possible, tested it twice and wrote the instructions the contractor followed. Nobody remembers that. The disaster that did not happen leaves no mark, and so neither does the person who stopped it.

This piece looks at why recognition tends to follow rescue and not prevention, and what that does to the people who do the preventing. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on feedback and support at work, not a finding about any particular South African employer.

The invisibility of a problem that never happened

Prevention has a structural disadvantage: its success looks like nothing. A reconciled account, a checked delivery note, a tested backup, a reminder sent before the deadline, a pump serviced before the dry season. The better the work, the quieter the result.

Rescue is the opposite. It has a clear start, a visible hero and a story with an ending. People talk about the night the system was down and who stayed to fix it. They rarely talk about the nights when the system did not go down.

Consider a hypothetical office manager at a small logistics firm who spends one afternoon a month checking that customer insurance certificates, driver licences and vehicle roadworthy documents are current. In the year she does this, no vehicle is stopped at a roadblock, no claim is declined, no customer contract is queried. At year end the review meeting covers the one late delivery. Her afternoons are not mentioned, because they have no incident to attach to.

How recognition systems favour the visible

Most workplaces recognise what they can see, count or tell as a story. That tilts recognition toward several kinds of work:

  • Heroic recovery over routine care. A weekend spent restoring service is easy to praise. The monthly check that made it unnecessary is not.
  • Output over avoidance. Sales closed and invoices sent can be counted. Disputes avoided and errors caught before they spread cannot, unless someone chooses to count them.
  • The recent over the cumulative. A manager remembers last week's crisis more easily than a year of quiet reliability, and a review cycle often reflects that.

There is also a quiet incentive problem. If the best way to be noticed is to be seen fixing something, people learn that letting small problems grow to a visible size is better for their standing than preventing them. Few people do this deliberately, but over time the system can reward it all the same.

What the research says about feedback and support

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standards standard on support lists among its states to be achieved that employees receive regular and constructive feedback, and that they know what support is available and how to get it. Feedback that arrives only when something has gone wrong, or only for dramatic recoveries, falls short of that description. Constructive feedback has to be able to land on quiet good work as well.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work describes decent work as providing a sense of confidence, purpose and achievement. Achievement that no one names is harder to feel. A person whose daily care keeps others out of trouble may start to wonder if the care matters, particularly when colleagues who cause and then solve problems collect the thanks.

The Management Standards on role add that people should understand their role and responsibilities and that requirements should be clear. If prevention is part of someone's role but absent from how that role is described and judged, their effort sits outside the way the organisation keeps score.

The human tax: dignity

The main cost is dignity. Being a dependable person who is never noticed because nothing goes wrong is a strange kind of invisibility. The work is taken for granted, then the worker is taken for granted, and a sense builds that the job is thankless. Small businesses are especially prone to this, since they have few formal review points where quiet reliability could be named.

A second cost falls on the business. People who feel unseen eventually stop doing the unseen work, or leave. When they go, the problems they were preventing return, and the business learns what the checks were worth only by losing them. The piece on the team leader who quietly shields the team from bad tools follows the same pattern for the person who absorbs trouble before it reaches others.

What could realistically change

The fix is not a bigger reward. It is making prevention visible. A business can do this with small, regular habits that cost little.

Start by recording the near-misses: the error caught at the check, the expiring document renewed in time, the supplier query resolved before it became a dispute. A running list of "things that did not happen" turns an invisible contribution into a concrete one. The note on the morning check that overnight jobs ran shows how a routine check protects the day, and what a week without firefighting would feel like describes the experience of the people who benefit.

Then change what is celebrated. If the only stories told in meetings are rescues, prevention will stay quiet. Naming the person whose check caught an error before it reached a customer sends a different message about what the business values. Where recognition is tied to pay or progression, make sure the criteria include reliability and avoidance, not only visible output.

Practical next actions

  1. Keep a near-miss log. Ask the team to record each problem caught or avoided, who did the preventing and roughly what it would have cost. Review the log monthly.
  2. Name the preventer in the room. When a crisis is averted or a check works, say so at the next team meeting, with the person's name and what they did.
  3. Add prevention to the role description. For each job that includes routine checks, state them in writing so that doing them is part of what good performance means.
  4. Review how rescues are rewarded. Look at the last year's bonuses, thanks and promotions. If every one went to a recovery, add a way to recognise the work that made recovery unnecessary.
  5. Ask preventers what they need. Ask the people who run your checks what would make them easier or more effective, and act on one suggestion. It is a form of recognition in itself.

The business outcome

Businesses that see prevention keep it. Checks continue, documentation stays current and the people who do the quiet work remain in post. The near-miss log also becomes an early guide to where the next problem may arise, which is useful information even if nobody ever intended to collect it. Over time, fewer emergencies mean fewer heroes are needed.

The human dividend

The dividend is predictability, for the business and for the person. For the business, it means fewer surprises. For the person, it means their steady work is acknowledged, so they can count on being seen without having to wait for a failure to prove their worth. That recognition is a form of dignity, and what each person does with it, whether continuing to build quietly or asking for a broader role, is theirs to decide.

The trade-offs

  • Counting what did not happen is imprecise. Estimates of avoided cost are rough, and a log should be treated as a prompt for discussion, not an audit.
  • Some people dislike attention. A quiet worker may prefer a private word to public praise, so ask how they want to be thanked.
  • Recognition can crowd out reward. Thanks without fair pay or progression can feel hollow, and may be worse than silence.
  • Logs take effort. A near-miss record that no one reads becomes another chore, so keep it short and use it.

Why it matters

Every business depends on people whose best work shows up as nothing happening. If recognition goes only to those who are seen putting out fires, the business slowly teaches its staff to prefer fires. Making room to notice the quiet work is a modest habit, and it keeps the people who do it.
