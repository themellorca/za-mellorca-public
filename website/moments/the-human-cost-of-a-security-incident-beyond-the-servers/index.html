Why Security Incidents Carry a Human Cost Too

After a breach, the people involved carry shame, fatigue and lost confidence. How a business can plan for that human side and recover faster.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-14

When a business has a security incident, the first questions are technical. What was reached? What was taken? Is it contained? Those questions matter. But in the hours and days that follow, a second set of questions goes unasked: how are the people involved coping, who is awake at three in the morning, and what does the rest of the team now believe about their own safety at work?

This piece looks at the human cost of security incidents, from a phishing click to a ransomware outage, and what a business can do to make that cost smaller. It is a hypothesis drawn from general incident-response guidance and research on workplace wellbeing, not a finding about a named South African employer.

An incident is also a people event

Incident-response guidance usually describes a technical lifecycle: prepare, detect, respond, recover, learn. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology sets this out in its incident response recommendations, which place preparation and lessons learned alongside detection and containment as parts of one continuing risk management activity. The UK National Cyber Security Centre's incident management guidance likewise treats the response as an organisational capability, with planning, roles and communication, not only a technical task.

The people side runs through each stage. In preparation, someone decides who will be phoned and when. In response, a few individuals work long hours under pressure with partial information. In recovery, the whole team lives with restricted systems and extra checks. In review, someone is asked what went wrong, and the question can easily sound like who.

Where the human cost falls

The cost is spread across several people, and it is rarely counted.

  • The person who clicked. The employee who opened the wrong attachment often feels responsible for everything that follows. Shame makes people slower to report the next incident, which makes the next incident worse.
  • The responders. The IT lead, the manager and the supplier contact work through the night, make decisions with high stakes and little sleep, and then carry on with the day job.
  • The people who cannot work. When systems are down, colleagues sit idle or fall back on paper, uncertain whether wages, orders or customer promises are at risk.
  • The owner. A small-business owner may be carrying personal financial exposure and a sense that they should have prevented it.
  • Customers and suppliers. The business must tell them something, and the choice of words is a human task done under stress.

What the wellbeing research says

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work names risks to wellbeing that include heavy workloads, long hours, low control and a lack of clarity about roles. An incident compresses all of them into a short time. Responders have extreme workloads and no control over the pace, and many of them are improvising roles that nobody agreed in advance.

The same guidance points to a culture in which people can raise problems without fear. That bears directly on security. A business that blames the person who clicked will hear about fewer clicks, and the incidents will be found later and cost more.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Gqeberha accounting practice of fourteen people. On a Monday morning an assistant opens an email that appears to come from a client, and her screen freezes. She is mortified and tells nobody for forty minutes. By the time the practice owner hears, the file server is encrypting. The owner and the part-time IT contractor work until two the next morning. The practice is partially restored in three days, with the tax deadline for several clients falling in that week.

The technical cost is real. The human cost is quieter and lasts longer. The assistant avoids email for a month. The owner stops sleeping properly. Two colleagues begin to check the doors and the router as if the practice itself were no longer safe. A year later, they remember the week more clearly than the clients' returns.

The human tax: safety and confidence

The first cost is safety and wellbeing, in the sense of the person's sense of being all right at work. Incidents feel like intrusions, and people experience them that way even when no personal data is lost. The second cost is confidence. People lose trust in the tools, in their own judgement and sometimes in each other. The note on constant alerts becoming background anxiety describes how a steady diet of warnings can wear down the sense of being in control, and an incident intensifies that effect.

There is a related cost in the piece on why being blamed for system errors wears people down: the person who is held to account for a failure of design, such as a weak filter or a missing second approval, carries a burden that belongs to the system.

What could realistically change

The business cannot remove all incidents, but it can make the response humane as well as effective. That begins before anything happens. A plan that names who decides, who talks to customers, who looks after the responders and who covers their usual work means that nobody has to invent roles at midnight. The article on when a death reveals who held the passwords shows what is lost when this knowledge exists only in one head.

It continues during the incident. Rotating responders, scheduling rest, feeding people and telling the wider team plainly what is known and unknown all lower the strain. Honest, early communication matters more than a polished message. It continues afterwards, with a review that asks how the system allowed the error, not who made it, and that finishes with changes the business actually makes.

Practical next actions

  1. Write a one-page incident roles sheet. Name who decides, who communicates, who contacts suppliers and who is their backup, with phone numbers kept somewhere that survives an outage, including on paper.
  2. Make reporting safe. Tell the team in writing that reporting a suspicious click quickly is the right thing to do, and that nobody will be punished for it. Then act that way the first time it happens.
  3. Plan for the responders. Agree shifts, breaks and food for a long incident, and decide in advance who takes over the responders' normal duties.
  4. Prepare plain-language messages. Draft short templates for staff, customers and suppliers, so that the words are not written in a panic.
  5. Hold a blameless review. Within two weeks, meet to ask what in the process, tools or training allowed the incident, and record two or three concrete changes with owners and dates.

The business outcome

A business that plans for the human side recovers faster, because people report early, responders make fewer tired mistakes, and the rest of the team knows what is happening. It retains staff who might otherwise leave after a bad experience, and it protects the owner's judgement at the moment it matters most. It also shortens the time to notice the next incident, which is the best defence there is.

The human dividend

The dividend is predictability. People who know in advance what will happen if something goes wrong, who will do what and what is expected of them, can face an incident with less dread. They know they will not be blamed for reporting, that responders will be relieved, and that life will return to its usual routine. What they do with the calm is their own affair.

The trade-offs

  • Blamelessness has limits. Repeated reckless behaviour still needs a conversation, and a fair process must tell the difference between a slip and a pattern.
  • Planning costs time. For a small team the time is hard to find until after the first incident.
  • Openness has risks. Telling staff and customers what happened can raise legal and reputational questions, so a business should take advice on what to disclose and when, including its obligations under the Protection of Personal Information Act.
  • Support is not therapy. A business can offer rest, honesty and access to help, but it cannot treat distress that needs a professional.

Why it matters

Security is often described as protecting systems, but in practice it protects people: their jobs, their clients, their reputations and their peace of mind. A business that counts the human cost of an incident will plan for it better, respond to it more kindly and recover from it faster than one that sees only the servers.
