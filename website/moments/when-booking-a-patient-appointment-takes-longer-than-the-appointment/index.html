Relief from Friction

When Booking a Patient Appointment Takes Longer Than the Appointment

A fifteen-minute consultation can take longer to arrange than to attend: phone queues, call-backs, missing medical aid details. How small practices can make booking easier for patients and staff.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-15

A patient needs a fifteen-minute follow-up with a physiotherapist. To book it, she phones the practice during her lunch break and waits in a queue. The line drops. She tries again later and is told to call back tomorrow because the diary for next week is not open yet. The next day she gets through, but the receptionist needs her medical aid number, which is at home. In total, arranging the appointment has taken more of her time, across three days, than the appointment itself will.

This piece looks at booking friction in small healthcare practices: why it happens, what it costs patients and staff, and what can change without losing the human contact patients value. It is a hypothesis built on vendor documentation, South African health and privacy law, and common practice, not a study of any particular practice.

Why booking is harder than it looks

Booking in a small practice is not just finding a free slot. The receptionist must match the right practitioner to the right appointment type and length, confirm medical aid and contact details, sometimes check whether a referral is needed, and fit it all around practitioners' leave and theatre days. Much of this happens on the phone, one patient at a time, while other patients wait at the desk.

An illustrative example, not a real case: a two-practitioner physiotherapy practice in George. One receptionist handles phones, walk-ins, payments and medical aid claims. Mornings are busy at the desk, so phone calls go unanswered, and patients leave voicemails that she returns in the afternoon, often reaching their voicemail in turn. Reminders are sent by hand the day before, when she remembers. Missed appointments are common, and each one is a lost slot that another patient could have used.

The human tax: time and energy

For patients, the cost is time and energy, often at moments when they have little of either: when they are in pain, caring for someone, or trying to arrange care around work. For some, the phone is the only route, and an unanswered line is a real barrier. For others, especially younger or working patients, the inability to book outside office hours is the main obstacle.

For the receptionist, the cost is a split day. Every phone call interrupts the patient at the desk, and every patient at the desk delays the phone. The neighbouring story the receptionist who keeps four booking systems in sync describes a version where several diaries must be reconciled by hand. Practice owners feel it too, through missed appointments and through being called into scheduling problems, which the practice owner who can't plan a single day off explores.

What could realistically change

Online self-booking takes routine appointments off the phone. Microsoft's Bookings overview describes a web-based booking page integrated with staff calendars, where customers choose a time that suits them, with email and SMS notifications to reduce no-shows. Many practice management systems used in South Africa offer comparable features designed specifically for healthcare.

Self-booking works best for straightforward follow-ups. New patients, complex cases and anyone who prefers to speak to a person still need a phone route, and that route works better when routine bookings are no longer clogging it. The story when digital-only leaves some customers behind is a reminder not to remove it.

Patient details are sensitive. Health information is special personal information under the Protection of Personal Information Act, and practices also work within the framework of the National Health Act. Any online booking tool should collect only what is needed to book, store it securely, and avoid asking for clinical details through a public form.

Practical next actions

  1. Count the calls. For one week, tally how many calls are routine bookings or reschedules, and how many go unanswered.
  2. Offer self-booking for follow-ups. Let existing patients book standard follow-up appointments online, and keep new-patient and complex bookings on the phone.
  3. Automate reminders. Send an SMS or email reminder automatically the day before, with a simple way to cancel or reschedule.
  4. Ask for details once. Capture medical aid and contact details at the first visit and confirm them periodically, rather than at every booking.
  5. Protect a phone hour. Set a daily period when the receptionist answers calls without desk interruptions, and tell patients when it is.
  6. Check your privacy basics. Confirm that any booking tool stores data securely and does not collect clinical information through open forms.

The business outcome

Fewer missed appointments mean more consultations per week without extending hours. Phone lines are freed for patients who need them. The receptionist can give better attention to people at the desk. The practice also gains a more accurate diary, because cancellations come in earlier and slots can be refilled. The human dividend

The dividend is time, for patients and staff. A patient who can book a follow-up in two minutes at nine in the evening spends her lunch break eating lunch. A receptionist who is not constantly torn between phone and desk can be present for the person in front of her. These are small gains, but they shape how both groups feel about the practice.

The trade-offs

  • Online booking does not suit everyone. Some patients cannot or will not use it, so the phone route must stay good.
  • Self-booking can fill slots wrongly. Patients may choose the wrong appointment type, so keep online options simple and review bookings.
  • Reminders cost money. SMS reminders carry a per-message cost, which needs weighing against missed appointments.
  • Health data raises the stakes. A booking tool that mishandles patient information creates legal and trust risks.

Why it matters

Patients come to a practice for care, not for administration. When arranging care costs more effort than receiving it, something has gone wrong in the process, not in the people. Making routine booking easy, while keeping a human route for those who need it, gives time back to both sides of the reception desk.
