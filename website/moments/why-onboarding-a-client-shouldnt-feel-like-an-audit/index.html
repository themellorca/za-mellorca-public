Why Onboarding a Client Shouldn't Feel Like an Audit

New clients are often welcomed with a stack of forms, repeated document requests and weeks of back-and-forth. Why onboarding grows heavy, and how to keep it compliant without the interrogation.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-16

A small business owner decides to move to a new accountant. She is pleased with the decision. Then the welcome email arrives with a twelve-page form, a list of fourteen documents, and a request to have some of them certified. She sends what she can. A week later, someone asks for her identity document, which she sent on day one. Then for proof of address, which she also sent. By the time the first meeting happens, she feels less like a valued client and more like a suspect.

This piece looks at why client onboarding in professional-services firms so often grows heavy, and how to keep it compliant while making it proportionate and humane. It is a hypothesis drawn from regulatory and public-sector guidance. It is not compliance advice, and firms should follow their own regulatory obligations.

Why onboarding grows heavy

Onboarding forms tend to grow by accretion. A requirement is added after an audit. A question is added after a difficult client. A document is added because another firm asks for it. Nobody removes anything, because removing feels risky. Over time the process becomes a list of everything anyone ever wanted to know, applied equally to every client.

The process also leaks. Documents arrive by email, are saved to personal folders or not saved at all, and the next person to handle the client asks again. The client experiences that leak as repetition. Inside the firm, nobody sees the whole picture: each person believes they are asking for something reasonable, and only the client sees how many times the same request has arrived.

What proportionality looks like

Some firms are accountable institutions with obligations to identify and verify clients. Those obligations matter, and they are not optional. But the regulator's own framing is about proportion. The Financial Intelligence Centre's compliance page explains that compliance obligations follow a risk-based approach, and that the level of identification and verification must be determined in line with the client's risk profile. A low-risk client and a high-risk client are not meant to be treated identically.

Collecting information only once is a principle well established in public-sector data practice. The UK government's guidance on how to make better use of data lists only collecting the data you need, and only collecting it once, among the benefits of good data practice. A small firm can apply the same principle with a shared client record and a single document store.

The Protection of Personal Information Act sets conditions for processing personal information in South Africa. Collecting more than a firm needs, and scattering it across inboxes, increases the firm's exposure as well as the client's burden.

An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a five-person accounting practice in Nelspruit. Every new client receives the same onboarding pack regardless of size or service. Documents arrive by email and WhatsApp and are saved by whoever receives them. The practice administrator spends much of her week chasing missing items, some of which are already somewhere in the firm.

When the practice reviews the pack, it finds that a third of the questions relate to services most clients never use, and that the same documents are requested at two stages. It splits the pack by service and risk level, moves document collection into a single secure upload link tied to the client record, and checks the record before asking for anything. Onboarding becomes shorter, and the administrator's chasing drops.

The human tax: time and energy

For the client, the tax is time spent finding, scanning and resending documents, and the energy of a relationship that starts with suspicion. Our story on the friction of signing up for something at work shows how repetitive forms wear people down, even when each question seems reasonable on its own.

For staff, the tax is the chasing. Following up on missing documents is tedious, and asking a client for something they already sent is embarrassing. The story the time tax of re-explaining the same thing describes the same repetition from the inside.

Practical next actions

  1. Audit the pack. For every question and document, write down why it is needed, and remove those without a clear reason.
  2. Tier by risk and service. Create shorter packs for low-risk clients and simple services, in line with your compliance programme.
  3. Collect once, store once. Use a single client record and secure document store, and check it before asking for anything.
  4. Use a secure upload link. Replace email and messaging attachments with a secure upload tied to the client record.
  5. Explain the why. Tell clients briefly why each item is required, which turns interrogation into explanation.
  6. Measure time to first meeting. Track how long onboarding takes and set a target to shorten it.

The business outcome

A proportionate onboarding process starts paid work sooner, reduces administrator time spent chasing, and lowers the firm's data exposure. New clients form a better first impression, which matters for referrals. Compliance often improves too, because a well-organised record is easier to review than documents scattered across inboxes.

The human dividend

The dividend is time, and a better start. The client gives what is needed once, understands why, and moves on to the work they came for. The administrator spends less of her week chasing and more of it helping. The relationship begins with trust rather than with friction. The trade-offs

  • Obligations remain. Regulated firms must still meet their identification and verification requirements; simplification must not undercut them.
  • Higher-risk clients need more. Some clients will rightly face more questions, and the process should handle them without embarrassment.
  • Tools cost money. Secure portals and client-record systems require subscriptions and set-up.
  • Some clients prefer paper. A digital process should not exclude clients who cannot or will not use it.

Why it matters

The first weeks of a client relationship set its tone. An onboarding process that asks for what is needed, once, and explains why, tells clients they are respected. It also gives staff back time they currently spend chasing. Compliance and courtesy are not opposites; a well-designed process serves both.
