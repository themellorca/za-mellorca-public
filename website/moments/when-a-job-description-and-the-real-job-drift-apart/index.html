Work and Dignity

When a Job Description and the Real Job Drift Apart

The job on paper says one thing; the job people actually do says another. How roles drift as systems, people and customers change, why the gap matters, and how to bring the two back together.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-13

The job advert asked for a sales coordinator to process orders and support the sales team. Three years later, the person in that role also manages the online shop, answers delivery complaints, updates the product catalogue, runs the monthly stock report and trains new staff on the order system. None of that is written down. When review time comes, she is assessed on order processing.

This piece looks at the drift between a written job and the real one, why it happens and why it matters. It is reasoned from general guidance on role clarity and change, not from a survey of South African employers, and it is not legal advice.

How roles drift

Roles drift because businesses change faster than paperwork. A new system arrives and someone has to learn it, so it becomes part of their job. A colleague leaves and their tasks are shared out informally. A customer asks for something new and whoever answers takes it on. Each addition is small and sensible. Nobody revisits the job description, because there is never a moment that seems to call for it.

Drift goes in both directions. Some tasks are added; others quietly disappear because a system now does them. The written role may still describe work that no longer exists, while leaving out the work that fills the person's day.

Consider a hypothetical engineering supplies business with twenty-five staff. After moving to a new accounting system, the receptionist becomes the de facto system administrator because she was the most confident user. She now spends a third of her time on user access, report requests and troubleshooting. Her job title, pay grade and review criteria still describe reception work.

Why the gap matters

The gap has practical consequences:

  • Work goes unrecognised. Reviews, pay and promotion are based on the written role, so the real contribution is invisible.
  • Expectations conflict. Managers may expect the original duties to be done fully while the new ones also grow.
  • Cover fails. When the person is away, nobody knows the full list of what they do, because it was never written down.
  • Hiring misfires. When the person leaves, the replacement is hired against the old description and is unprepared for the real job.

What the guidance says

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standard on role says employees should understand their role and responsibilities, and that the organisation should provide information to help them do so. The standard on change says that during change, employees should be told how it is likely to affect their jobs, with training where needed. Most role drift is the result of change that nobody treated as change.

In South Africa, the Basic Conditions of Employment Act sets the baseline terms of employment, and employers provide written particulars of employment to staff. If a role has changed substantially, it is sensible to check with a qualified adviser whether the written terms still reflect the job.

The human tax: dignity

The main cost is to dignity. Doing work that nobody acknowledges is quietly demoralising. The person knows how much they contribute, but the formal record says otherwise. They may feel that raising it would sound like complaining, so they say nothing. Over time, the gap between how they see their job and how the business sees it can become a gap between them and the business.

Bringing the two back together

The fix is a regular, light review of each role, done with the person in it. It does not need to be a formal job evaluation. It is a conversation about what the person actually does, what has been added, what has fallen away, and what should be done about it: recognise the new work, reassign it, remove it or support it with training. The Moment on why documented processes help people grow into roles shows how written processes make roles easier to hand on.

Practical next actions

  1. Ask each person to describe their real job. Invite every employee to list the tasks they actually do in a typical month, with rough time on each.
  2. Compare with the written role. Put the list next to their job description. Mark tasks that are missing from the description and duties that no longer happen.
  3. Decide on each gap. For each added task, decide whether to recognise it in the role, move it to someone better placed or remove it with a system change.
  4. Update the record. Revise the job description and review criteria to match the agreed role. Where the change is substantial, check whether written employment terms need updating.
  5. Set a yearly review. Repeat the exercise once a year, and whenever a system change or departure shifts work between people.

The business outcome

The business knows what work it depends on and who does it. Cover and handovers work better. Hiring is based on the real job. Pay and recognition decisions rest on accurate information, reducing resentment and turnover.

The human dividend

The dividend is predictability. The person knows what is expected of them, what will count in their review, and that new work will be recognised, not simply absorbed. That clarity is a form of respect, and it gives them a sound basis for deciding where they want their role to go.

The trade-offs

  • Reviews take time. Even a light review needs a conversation and follow-up for every role.
  • Written roles can become rigid. Over-detailed descriptions can make people reluctant to help outside them.
  • Pay questions may follow. Recognising added work may raise fair questions about grading and pay that the business must be ready to discuss.
  • Some flexibility is healthy. In a small business, people often need to cover for each other, and the role should allow for that.

Why it matters

A job description is a statement of what the business thinks a person is for. When it drifts far from reality, the person's real contribution falls outside the frame. Bringing the two back together is a simple way of saying: we see what you do, and it counts.
