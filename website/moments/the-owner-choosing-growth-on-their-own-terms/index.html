The small business owner choosing growth on their own terms

Growth, standing still and selling up are all legitimate aims. What a small business needs in place for the owner to choose between them freely.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-10

Growth is usually spoken about as if it were a single, obvious goal. More customers, more staff, a bigger premises, a second branch. For many small business owners, though, the question is quieter and more personal: how much more do I want, and what am I willing to carry to get it? Some want to double. Some want to hold steady and work fewer hours. Some want to grow slowly for three years and then sell.

This piece looks at the owner who wants to choose growth on their own terms, and at what the business needs in place for that choice to be real rather than nominal. It is a hypothesis, reasoned from general research on workload and motivation and from how small businesses tend to operate. It is not a study of South African owners, and the example is illustrative.

Growth as a choice, not a treadmill

An owner who can choose growth has options. They can say yes to a large contract because they know the business can deliver it, or no because they know it cannot. They can add a service line, or decline to. They can take a month away, or not. The point is not which choice they make. It is that the choice is theirs.

Many owners find themselves with fewer options than their turnover suggests. The business may be profitable, but growth is limited by what one person can personally oversee: every quote, every hire, every payment. A new customer means more work for the owner. A new product means more questions only the owner can answer. In that situation growth does not feel like freedom. It feels like a tighter squeeze.

South Africa's small business development agencies, such as the Small Enterprise Development Agency, exist because small enterprises matter to the economy and face particular constraints. Policy and support programmes tend to speak in terms of growth and job creation. That is a legitimate public purpose, but an individual owner's aims may differ, and a good support conversation begins with what the owner actually wants.

An illustrative example

Consider a hypothetical South African building-maintenance company with twelve employees. The owner, who started as a tradesperson, has been offered a contract to maintain a group of commercial properties across a second province. It would nearly double revenue.

He hesitates. Not because he lacks ambition, but because he knows what the current business depends on. He quotes every job himself. He allocates every team each morning from memory. Customer complaints come to his phone. Month-end invoicing waits until he has reviewed the job cards. Doubling the contracts would double all of that, with the same two hands.

He has three real options. He can decline and stay as he is. He can accept and absorb the strain, hoping to hire later. Or he can spend a year making the business less dependent on his personal oversight before deciding. Each is legitimate. What he cannot do, at present, is choose any of them freely, because the first two are driven by the shape of his business rather than by what he wants.

This scenario is built to illustrate a mechanism. It does not describe a real company.

What stands between an owner and a free choice

Several ordinary conditions limit how freely an owner can choose.

  • Dependency on the owner. When knowledge, approvals and customer relationships live with one person, growth multiplies their load. The structural side is discussed in key-person dependency is a systems problem.
  • Limited visibility of margins. An owner who does not know which jobs or customers make money cannot judge which growth is worth having. See why knowing your margins changes what you can choose.
  • Cash-flow uncertainty. Growth consumes cash before it returns it. An owner unsure of next month's position will understandably hold back, a theme in the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake.
  • Processes that exist only in the owner's head. New staff cannot take on the work if nobody has written down how it is done.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's standard on demands says that people's workloads should be manageable within agreed working hours and that jobs should fall within what people can realistically do. It is written for employees, but it is a fair test for an owner as well. If growth would push the owner's role beyond what one person can sustain, the growth is not simply a matter of ambition.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work lists lack of control over workload among the risks to mental health at work. Self-employed people and owners are not exempt from the underlying idea. Having control over the pace and shape of the work matters, and growth that removes it carries a cost.

What changes when the business can carry more of the load

The enabling work is usually unglamorous. It is the groundwork for the earlier choice rather than a growth strategy in itself.

  • Write down the recurring decisions. Who may approve what, within which limits? A written rule is a way of handing off a decision without handing off control.
  • Make the numbers visible. Know the margin by job type, the cash position this month and next, and the customers who take the most effort.
  • Document the routines that run the business. The sequence of work is described in what business processes need documentation first.
  • Decide what not to grow. Saying no to certain kinds of work can be as valuable as adding capacity.

The aim is optionality: the real ability to say yes, no or later. A business that can do this has more room for different kinds of owner. One wants to expand into a second province. Another wants to keep the business the same size and stop working weekends. A third wants to build something saleable. The approach in how to build a business case for systems improvement can help frame the cost against the options it opens.

The self-determination angle

The self-determination research summarised at selfdeterminationtheory.org describes autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic psychological needs and reports that contexts supporting them tend to foster better engagement and wellbeing. In an owner's terms, autonomy is the sense that the direction of the business is theirs to decide. That sense can be strong in a small business where the owner calls the shots, and weak in one where the business has quietly taken over the owner's calendar.

It would be wrong to turn this into advice about what owners should want. Some owners will find that what they most value is the hands-on work, and will decline to build a business that does not need them. That is a defensible answer, and the framework should not label it as failure.

The trade-offs

  • Preparation costs money and attention before it pays back. Documenting, delegating and tidying records takes time that could have gone to billable work.
  • Letting go changes the owner's role. For some, the daily craft is part of the reward. A business that runs without them may be one they enjoy less.
  • Options are not guarantees. A more capable business can still fail to win the contract, or lose it. Optionality is the ability to choose, not a promise that choices will succeed.
  • Growth can still be the wrong goal. For some owners, the best use of a freer business is more time, not more revenue.

Interpreting the pattern

The Mellorca reading is that many owners are not short of ambition so much as short of room. When a business is built around one person's oversight, each increase in size is paid for personally, and the owner's choices narrow. Groundwork that spreads decisions, makes numbers visible and records how work is done widens them again.

This is a hypothesis and should be tested on the ground. A useful question for any owner is: if a good opportunity arrived next month, could I say yes freely, say no freely, or only say whichever the business forces on me?
