Relief from Friction

Why Getting Paid Shouldn't Require Persistence

Many small businesses get paid only after repeated reminders, calls and resent invoices. Why collection depends so much on stamina, and how to make paying you the easy path.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-15

For many small-business owners, being paid is less an event than a campaign. The invoice goes out. Thirty days pass. A polite reminder follows, then a resent invoice because the first went to the wrong person, then a phone call, then another. Eventually the money arrives, and the owner notes, with some weariness, that the customer was never unwilling to pay. They simply did not pay until pushed.

This piece looks at why collection so often depends on persistence, and how to design invoicing so that paying becomes the path of least resistance. It is a hypothesis drawn from vendor documentation, tax rules and public policy elsewhere, not a measurement of payment behaviour in South Africa.

Why persistence becomes the system

Late payment is not always bad faith. Often the invoice is stuck somewhere in the customer's process:

  • It went to the wrong person. The invoice reached the person who ordered the work, not the accounts team that pays.
  • It is missing something. No order number, the wrong company name, or a VAT detail the customer's finance team needs before it can be processed.
  • It is waiting for approval. The customer's own approval chain is slow, and nobody there is chasing it.
  • Paying is awkward. Bank details are hard to find, references are unclear, or the customer must log in somewhere to pay.

Each of these is resolved by persistence: the supplier keeps asking until the problem surfaces. That works, but it puts the cost of the customer's process on the supplier.

An illustrative example, not a real case: a Rustenburg electrical contractor with eight staff. The owner invoices from her phone on site, and invoices go to whoever booked the job. Larger customers often reject invoices without a purchase order number, but she only finds out when she chases at forty-five days. Her Friday afternoons are spent on collections calls. She is good at it. She would rather not have to be.

The human tax: financial resilience and energy

The financial cost is direct. A small business that waits sixty days instead of thirty must fund the gap from its own cash, an overdraft or the owner's pocket. The story when late invoices become late salaries follows that chain to payroll, and the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake describes what it feels like.

The energy cost is in the chasing itself. Collections calls are uncomfortable for many owners, especially when the customer is also a relationship they value. Asking repeatedly for money already earned can feel like begging, which is a poor fit for someone who delivered good work on time.

This is not only a private concern. In the UK, the government created a Small Business Commissioner to help small firms resolve payment disputes with larger businesses, as described in its announcement on launching the commissioner. South Africa has debated a similar ombud for small enterprises. Whatever policy eventually provides, most of the improvement available to a single business lies in its own invoicing.

Making payment the easy path

The aim is to remove the reasons an invoice stalls, before it is sent. Collect the customer's accounts contact, order number requirements and invoicing details when the job is accepted, not after. Make every invoice complete: correct legal name, order number, a clear description, and, for VAT vendors, what SARS requires. The SARS page on value-added tax is the starting point for those obligations. Show bank details and the payment reference prominently.

Then let reminders run without the owner. Microsoft's Business Central documentation on collecting outstanding balances describes reminder terms with levels that set when each reminder goes out, a grace period so customers who have paid are not reminded, and the option to automate creating and sending reminders. Most small-business accounting packages offer comparable automatic reminders. Personal calls are then reserved for the cases that need them.

Practical next actions

  1. Collect billing details up front. When accepting a job, ask for the accounts contact, purchase order requirements and invoicing name.
  2. Check invoices before sending. Use a short checklist: correct customer name, order number, description, VAT details where applicable, bank details and reference.
  3. Invoice promptly. Send the invoice when the work is done, not at month-end, so the payment clock starts earlier.
  4. Switch on automatic reminders. Set reminders at agreed intervals before and after the due date, with a grace period for payments in transit.
  5. Ask why invoices stalled. When a payment is late, find out where it got stuck and fix that cause for the customer's future invoices.
  6. Agree terms clearly. State payment terms in quotes and confirmations, so the due date is known before work starts.

The business outcome

Cleaner invoices and automatic reminders usually shorten the time to payment and reduce the number of invoices that need personal follow-up. Cash flow becomes more predictable, which makes it easier to plan wages, stock and investment. Customer relationships improve too, because collections conversations become rare rather than routine. The related story the Saturday morning spent on invoices shows the cost of the alternative.

The human dividend

The dividend is financial breathing room and time. An owner who is paid closer to terms has a cushion that does not depend on her stamina for difficult calls. Her Friday afternoons, previously spent chasing, become hers to use, whether for quoting new work, training staff or leaving early. Getting paid stops being a test of persistence and becomes what it should be: the routine end of a job well done.

The trade-offs

  • Up-front questions can feel bureaucratic. Asking for billing details before starting may seem formal to small customers, so keep it brief.
  • Automatic reminders can feel impersonal. Word them politely, and let valued customers know they are standard practice.
  • Some customers will still pay late. Better invoicing removes friction but cannot fix a customer with real cash problems.
  • Software has costs. Automated reminders may require a paid accounting package or a higher tier.

Why it matters

Small businesses carry enough risk without also funding their customers' administrative delays. When getting paid depends on persistence, the people with the least cushion end up doing the most chasing. Designing invoicing so that paying is easy, complete and gently reminded shifts that burden back where it belongs, and gives owners back time and security they have already earned.
