The half-hour lost to finding the right file

The file exists; the question is which of its five copies is the right one. What searching for documents takes in time and quiet doubt, and the filing decisions that make finding it a non-event.

Mellorca Moments2026-10-04

The file definitely exists. You remember seeing it. It was the signed version of the supplier agreement, or the latest price list, or the spreadsheet with last year's figures that the auditor wants again. You search the shared drive and find four candidates with names like "final", "final v2" and "final USE THIS". You search your email and find a fifth attachment. You message a colleague, who is in a meeting.

Half an hour later, you have a file. You are fairly sure it is the right one.

That last sentence is the part worth dwelling on. The time lost to searching is real, and it is the part most often measured. But the experience also leaves something behind: a small residue of doubt about whether what you found is what you needed. This piece looks at both.

A familiar problem, widely reported

Difficulty finding information at work is one of the more consistently reported frustrations in workplace research. Microsoft's Work Trend Index found that 62% of surveyed people said they struggled with too much time spent searching for information. IBM's explainer on enterprise search cites a 2023 Gartner survey in which nearly half of digital workers said they struggled to find the information they needed to do their jobs, and a 2025 Forrester survey in which 74% of senior and executive managers said they had to use different platforms to find what they needed.

These are international surveys, and they measure perception rather than minutes. They do not establish how long a South African bookkeeper or project manager spends looking for documents. What they show is that the problem is common, persistent and not confined to large organisations with sprawling systems.

The commercial cost of this is set out in Mellorca's work on the productivity cost of employees searching for files, and the pattern behind it in why nobody can find the document they know exists. This piece asks what the same search takes from the person doing it.

Why the right file is hard to find

Lost files are rarely the result of carelessness. They are usually the result of a filing arrangement that grew without being designed.

  • Several places to keep things. A shared drive, personal desktops, email attachments, a cloud folder someone set up for one project, and WhatsApp.
  • Deep folder trees. Microsoft's guidance on SharePoint information architecture notes that folder structures nested more than one or two levels deep create a significant discoverability burden, and recommends using metadata, such as columns describing a document's type, client or status, to organise and find content instead.
  • No agreed source of truth. When the same document is saved in three places, nobody knows which copy is current.
  • Versions by filename. Without version control, people show history by renaming files, and the names stop meaning anything.

IBM describes document management in terms of the features that address these gaps: centralised storage, indexing, version control, access control, audit trails and search. Few small businesses need a large platform to get most of those benefits; many already pay for tools that provide them and use only a fraction.

An illustrative afternoon

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Durban architectural practice with fifteen staff. Drawings live in a project folder on a shared drive, but issued versions are also emailed to clients and contractors. Contracts are scanned and saved by whoever received them. Correspondence sits in individual inboxes.

A contractor phones to query a detail. The project architect needs the drawing revision that was issued, not the one currently being worked on. The folder holds several revisions; the email trail holds another. Comparing them takes time, and even then the architect checks with a colleague before answering, because sending the wrong revision could be costly.

In this scenario, the search is not just slow. It carries a risk, and the person searching knows it.

The human tax: time and cognitive load

The first tax is time, and it is usually spent in fragments: five minutes here, twenty there, rarely noticed because it is spread through the day.

The second tax is cognitive load. Searching means holding several possibilities in mind, comparing dates and names, and judging which copy to trust. Even after the search ends, some of that load lingers as uncertainty. Was that really the signed version? Did someone update the price list after I downloaded it? People differ in how much this weighs on them, but it is reasonable to infer that work done on top of uncertain information carries a little more strain than work done on information you trust.

There is a quieter social cost too. Repeatedly asking colleagues where something is can feel awkward, particularly for newer or more junior staff. Some will spend longer searching alone rather than ask, or will recreate a document from scratch, a pattern Mellorca has described in staff recreating lost documents.

What could realistically change

Most of the useful changes are decisions rather than purchases.

  • Decide where each kind of document lives. Contracts in one library, drawings in another, finance records in a third. Write it down in one short page. Mellorca's guide on deciding what should be the system of record applies to documents as much as to data.
  • Flatten folders and add a few labels. Fewer levels of nesting, with a handful of consistent labels such as client, document type and status, are easier to search and browse.
  • Use version history instead of renaming. Most current cloud storage keeps versions automatically; one file with a history is clearer than five files with clever names.
  • Mark what is final. A simple status for issued or signed documents removes much of the doubt.
  • Save once, link everywhere. Share links to the stored document rather than sending attachments that become independent copies.
  • Tidy periodically. A short quarterly review to archive dead project folders and remove duplicates keeps search results useful.

The first consequence, and the business outcome

The immediate change is that the right file is found faster, and found with confidence. Fewer questions go to colleagues. Fewer documents are recreated.

The business outcome is less wasted time, fewer errors from outdated versions, and a clearer record when a client, auditor or dispute requires one.

The human dividend

The dividend is time and attention. Time, because the search is shorter. Attention, because the person can act on what they find without carrying a background question about whether it is right. That combination, quicker and surer, is what turns a frustrating half-hour into a minute that nobody notices.

The trade-offs

  • Change disrupts habits. People who know where their own files are may find a new structure slower at first.
  • Labels need maintenance. If nobody applies them consistently, they become one more unreliable signal.
  • Centralising affects access. Putting documents in shared libraries means deciding who can see what, especially for HR and financial records.
  • Migration takes effort. Moving years of files is real work, and it is tempting to move everything rather than decide what is worth keeping.

Why it matters

A half-hour lost to searching does not look like much on any given day. Repeated across a team and a year, it is a meaningful share of working life spent on something that produces nothing, and done under a low hum of uncertainty.

Mellorca's interpretation is that finding the right file should be a non-event. When it is, people's time and attention go back to the work the file was needed for.
