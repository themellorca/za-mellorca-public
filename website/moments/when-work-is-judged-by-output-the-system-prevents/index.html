Work and Dignity

When Work Is Judged by Output the System Prevents

Targets and reports can punish people for results that slow systems, missing data and broken handoffs make impossible. How to measure fairly.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

A team lead looks at the weekly report and sees that one of her people closed fewer cases than everyone else. The number is accurate. What it does not show is that this person is the one who gets the cases that arrive without a customer reference, that her screen takes twenty seconds to load each record, and that she spends an hour a day waiting for another department to approve what she has prepared. The report measures output. It cannot see what stood in the way.

This piece looks at what happens to confidence and dignity when people are judged by results the system around them makes hard to achieve. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on measurement and job design, not a finding about any specific South African firm, and the scenarios are illustrative.

What a number leaves out

Every performance report is a simplification. It picks something countable, such as invoices processed, tickets closed or calls answered, and treats it as a stand-in for good work. That is useful until the stand-in is mistaken for the thing itself. A well-known saying, usually called Goodhart's law and discussed in this overview of Goodhart's law, captures the risk: once a measure becomes a target, it tends to stop being a good measure, because people and processes adjust to it.

The deeper problem is attribution. A figure for one person's output combines their effort with everything around them: the quality of the data they receive, the speed of the tools, the reliability of the handoff from the previous team, and the number of exceptions that land on their desk. When the system adds delay or error, the number falls, and the person is the one who is asked about it.

What it does to the person

Being measured against something you cannot fully influence has a distinctive effect.

  • Confidence. People start to doubt their own competence, even when they know where the blockages are, because the figure is the thing that gets discussed.
  • Dignity. Explaining that the tool was slow can sound like an excuse. The person is placed in the position of defending themselves against a number that was never fair to them.
  • Behaviour. Sensible people adapt. They pick the easy cases first, avoid awkward exceptions, or split work to make counts look better. The measure improves while the real work does not.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards for work-related stress include role clarity and control as conditions of a healthy job. Its page on role in the management standards describes employees understanding their role and the business ensuring they do not have conflicting roles. A person asked to hit a target that the process makes unreachable is in an unclear and conflicted position by any reasonable reading.

What it does to the business

When the report blames the person, the real cause stays hidden. The slow screen, the missing reference, the approval queue and the duplicate record all remain, because nobody is looking at them. The business may even respond by adding pressure, more reporting or tighter targets, which increases the burden on the staff without addressing the cause. The companion piece on when monitoring replaces trust in the reporting line describes how this can sour working relationships, and the cost article on weekly manual progress reporting shows how much time the reporting itself can consume.

An illustrative case

Picture, as a hypothetical example, a Bloemfontein medical-supplies distributor. Order processors are ranked each week on the number of orders released. One processor consistently ranks last. When a manager sits beside her for an afternoon, she finds that most of the processor's orders come from hospital accounts where the purchase-order number is often missing, so each needs two or three phone calls before release. Her colleagues handle mostly retail accounts with complete data.

The ranking was never a measure of effort or skill. It was a measure of which accounts the system sent to whom. For months the processor had been quietly told she was slow.

Measuring the work and the system

The fix is not to stop measuring. Numbers are valuable, and a business without them is guessing. The fix is to measure both the output and the things that shape it, and to read the first in the light of the second. That means tracking waiting time, rework, exceptions and data completeness alongside the count of tasks done, and treating a poor output figure as a prompt to examine the process before the person. The guide to what to measure at each stage of a business workflow sets out how to choose measures that show where work is being delayed.

Practical next actions

  1. Pick one output figure that people are judged by. Write down what has to be true for someone to reach it: tools working, data complete, approvals timely.
  2. Sit with the lowest and highest performers for an hour each. Look at what each is dealing with. Differences in the mix of work are often larger than differences in effort.
  3. Add a "blocked" or "waiting" category. Ask staff to record when work is held up and why, even roughly. A tally on paper is enough to start.
  4. Report the causes next to the count. In each review, show the top three reasons work was delayed. Assign an owner to the most frequent one.
  5. Separate coaching from system fixes. Before a performance conversation, check whether the shortfall is explained by the process. If it is, fix the process first.
  6. Check for gaming. Look for signs that people are tuning their behaviour to the measure, such as avoiding complex cases, and adjust the measure instead of blaming them.

The business outcome

A business that reads output alongside its causes finds real bottlenecks faster, treats staff fairly and avoids pressuring people to solve problems they did not create. It also gets more honest reporting, because people have less reason to hide delays or inflate counts when delays are understood to be a feature of the process and not a personal failure.

The human dividend

The dividend is predictability and the restoration of confidence. Predictability means a working week where the standard being applied is one the person can meet, and a review that begins with the facts of the work and not with an assumption. Confidence follows when the person is no longer asked to explain a number that was never theirs alone. For a view of how recurring reports can consume time at the other end of the chain, see the report someone rebuilds by hand every Friday.

The trade-offs

  • Extra measures add effort. Recording delays and reasons takes time, and if it is not acted on it will be resented.
  • Some shortfalls are personal. Not every low figure is the system's fault, and a business must still be able to address genuine performance concerns.
  • Context can become an excuse. Without discipline, "the system" can be used to avoid accountability in either direction.
  • Fixing causes may cost money. A slow tool or an approval chain may need investment or a change in authority that is not available quickly.

Why it matters

A fair measure respects both the work and the person doing it. When a business judges people by output the system prevents, it spends its credibility on a number and its goodwill on the people behind it. Looking at the causes costs little, and it turns many performance problems into process problems that can be solved.
