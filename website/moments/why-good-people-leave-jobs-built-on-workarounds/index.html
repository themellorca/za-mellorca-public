Why Good People Leave Jobs Built on Workarounds

The most capable staff are often the ones who build the workarounds that keep a business running, and the ones who tire of them first. Why that wears people down and what makes them want to stay.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-13

Ask who keeps a small business running and the answer is often a person, not a system. They know that invoices from one branch need the VAT line retyped, that the stock report is wrong on Mondays until someone reruns it, and that a certain customer's orders must be checked by hand because the discount never applies. They built half of these workarounds themselves. They are good at their job. And one day they hand in their notice.

This piece looks at why the people who hold workarounds together are often the ones who leave, and what an owner can change before they do. It is reasoned from general research on job demands and skill use, not from turnover data in South African businesses.

Why the most capable people build workarounds

Workarounds are a sign of competence. When a system fails, someone has to notice, understand the failure and invent a way around it. That takes skill. The person who does it is usually the one who cares most about getting the result right, and who is least willing to let a customer or colleague suffer for a broken process.

So the workarounds collect around the best people. Each one is reasonable on its own. Together they form a second, invisible job, layered over the first. The Moment on the bookkeeper who carries every workaround in their head describes how that knowledge accumulates, and the job of being human middleware describes what it is like to be the link between systems that do not talk.

Why the same people leave

Consider a hypothetical distribution company with forty staff. Its operations coordinator has built a spreadsheet that reconciles orders from the online shop with the warehouse system, because the two do not connect. Every morning she runs it, fixes the mismatches and sends corrections. She raised the integration problem in her first year. It was agreed that it should be fixed, once things were quieter. Three years later she is still running the spreadsheet.

Her reasons for leaving, if she leaves, are unlikely to be a single grievance. They are more likely to be a slow accumulation:

  • The work does not use her skill. She is capable of improving the process, but spends her time compensating for it.
  • The effort does not compound. A proper fix would be done once. A workaround has to be done every day, forever.
  • The problem is not hers to solve. She can patch it but cannot change the systems that cause it.
  • Her expertise is in the wrong thing. She is becoming an expert in this company's failures, which is knowledge that has no value anywhere else.

What the research says

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standard on demands describes a workplace where demands are achievable within agreed hours and where people's skills and abilities are matched to the job. HSE's overview of the causes of work-related stress names demands, control, support, relationships, role and change as the main areas that cause stress when they are not managed properly.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work lists the under-use of skills and poor investment in career development among the psychosocial risks to mental health. A job that consists largely of maintaining workarounds touches several of these at once: demands that exist only because of failures, skills used for patching, and a career that is not moving.

The human tax: dignity and energy

The dignity cost is subtle. Workaround keepers are often praised as indispensable, but the praise is for compensating, not for creating. Their best ideas, the ones that would remove the workaround, go unimplemented. Being valued for absorbing a problem is not the same as being valued for your judgement.

The energy cost is more direct. A workaround requires attention every time it runs, because the person is the only check on whether it worked. That attention is spent before the real work of the day begins. Over months it becomes a background tiredness that time off does not quite cure, because the first day back means catching up on every patch that was missed.

Turning workarounds into fixes

The response that works is not more praise or a bonus, welcome though those may be. It is to treat each workaround as evidence of a problem and to fix the cause, with the person who built the workaround leading or advising the fix. That turns their knowledge from a burden into an asset and shows that their judgement counts. The guide on finding steps that exist only because the system is bad sets out a method, and the pattern on the work your staff do because your systems do not shows how to see the scale of it.

Practical next actions

  1. Ask for the workaround list. Sit down with your most relied-on staff and ask them to list every regular workaround they run, how often and how long it takes. Thank them for it.
  2. Rank by cost and risk. For each workaround, estimate the time it takes each month and what happens if it is missed. Pick the two that cost most or carry most risk.
  3. Fund one fix properly. Commit money and a date to remove one workaround at its root, whether through an integration, a configuration change or a simpler process.
  4. Put the person in charge of the fix. Ask the workaround's author to define what good looks like and to sign off the result. Their expertise becomes the design brief.
  5. Talk about what comes next. Once hours are freed, agree with the person what they will do with them, whether a new responsibility, a project or training.

The business outcome

Fewer daily patches means fewer errors when a patch is missed, less dependence on one person, and lower risk if they are ill or leave. The business keeps a capable employee whose knowledge of its weaknesses has become the starting point for improving them.

The human dividend

The dividend is optionality. When the workarounds give way to proper fixes, the person's time and skill are free for work that builds something. Staying becomes a choice made because the job is worth doing, not a default for someone too busy patching to look elsewhere. And if they do leave one day, they leave with skills that matter.

The trade-offs

  • Fixes cost money and attention. Some workarounds exist because a proper fix is expensive, and a small business has to choose.
  • Some workarounds protect customers. A manual check may catch problems a system would miss. Removing it needs care.
  • People leave for many reasons. Pay, location, family and ambition matter too. Fixing workarounds removes one pressure, not every one.
  • Redesign can sting. A person who built a workaround may hear its removal as criticism, which is why they should lead the fix.

Why it matters

A business that runs on workarounds is borrowing against its best people's patience. When that patience runs out, the knowledge walks out with it. Turning workarounds into fixes repays the loan, and tells capable people that the business wants their judgement, not only their endurance.
