When Information Lives Only in One Person's Language

Notes, relationships and instructions held in a single language leave colleagues stranded when that person is away. How to share what matters without flattening languages.

Mellorca Moments2026-11-12

A client phones with an urgent question about an order. The person who normally handles that client is away for the week. A colleague opens the shared folder and finds notes, but the notes are written in isiZulu, Afrikaans or Sesotho, in a style that is clear to the author and nobody else. Or the opposite: the client speaks one language, the only person who speaks it is on leave, and nobody else can follow the thread of the conversation.

This piece looks at what happens to a business when important information lives only in one person's language, and what it costs the people on both sides of the gap. It is a hypothesis drawn from general guidance on accessibility and language, not a finding about a named South African employer.

Information that can only be read by one person

South Africa has eleven official languages. The Constitution lists them in its founding provisions, and it requires the state to take practical and positive measures to elevate the status and advance the use of the indigenous languages. Businesses are not bound in the same way, but they work in the same reality: staff, customers and suppliers who live and think in different languages, and who move between them many times a day.

That fluency is a strength. The difficulty comes when a record, a rule or a relationship is held in one language by one person, and nothing else carries it. It happens in several quiet ways:

  • Notes written in the author's first language. A supervisor records a customer's special requirements in the language they use at home. It is perfectly clear to them.
  • Relationships held in one tongue. A long-standing supplier speaks to one employee in a language that colleagues do not share, so the whole relationship rests on that employee.
  • Labels, signs and system screens in a single language. A new employee, a temporary worker or a customer cannot read the instruction that matters.
  • Verbal knowledge. Instructions given aloud in one language are never written down, so they disappear when the speaker does.

What happens when that person is away

The gap shows itself on the day of an absence. Leave, illness, a funeral, a resignation, a training course: any of them removes the person, and with them the only reliable route to the information. Colleagues guess. Customers repeat themselves. The returning person finds a pile of small mistakes and an apology to write.

The effect is on predictability. A business that cannot give a customer the same quality of answer on Tuesday as on Monday has made its service depend on who is in the building. The article on the customer who gets a different answer from every person describes how that unevenness is felt from the customer's side.

An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Pietermaritzburg building-supplies firm with a small trade counter. The senior counter hand has served the same contractors for fifteen years. Many of them speak isiZulu, and he records their credit arrangements and delivery preferences in short notes in that language in a school exercise book. When he is off for two weeks after surgery, the relief staff cannot read the book. Two contractors are quoted the wrong credit terms. One goes elsewhere.

Nobody in this story did anything wrong. The notes were quick, accurate and kind to the author's memory. The business simply had no way to share what they said.

The human tax: access

The main cost here is access. Information that someone cannot read is information they cannot use, and a person who cannot use the information they need to do their job is shut out of part of it. The W3C's guidance on the language of a page explains that identifying the language of content allows assistive technology and other tools to present it correctly. The wider point is that language is a property of information that systems should know about, not an accident that people absorb.

The cost falls on several groups. Colleagues who cover an absence are set up to fail, and they carry the customer's frustration. The absent person is rung on leave, because they are the only one who can help, as the note on the price of never fully switching off on leave describes. Customers are told, in effect, that service depends on a particular person's presence. And employees whose first language is not the business's default language may find that their own knowledge is undervalued, because it is hard to share.

The article on why accessibility is also an operational question argues that access to information is part of how a business runs, not an optional courtesy.

What could realistically change

The aim is not to force everyone into one language. People think and speak best in the language they know best, and a business gains trust by honouring that. The aim is to make sure that the essential facts are available to the person who needs them, whichever language they speak.

A practical approach has three parts. The first is a shared record: customer terms, special instructions and supplier arrangements are held in a place everyone can reach, instead of in a notebook or a memory. The second is a common working layer: the key facts, such as the credit limit, the delivery day and the person to call, are also written in the language the team shares, even if the conversation with the customer takes place in another. The third is a named backup: for each relationship held in a particular language, a second person is introduced and, where possible, can follow it.

The guidance on what a reliable handover gives back on a Friday afternoon shows how a short, structured handover can carry this kind of knowledge across a gap. The HSE's guidance on shift handover gives the goal of handover as accurate, reliable communication of task-relevant information, which is as true across a language gap as across a shift change. Where the business relies on translation tools, it should treat their output as a draft to be checked by a fluent speaker, especially for anything about money, safety or legal terms.

Practical next actions

  1. Map the single-language holders. List the customers, suppliers or records that only one person can read or discuss in their language. Mark the ones where an absence would cause real harm.
  2. Move the notebook into a shared place. Ask the author to enter the essential facts for each key relationship into a shared record, in their own words first.
  3. Add a short common-language summary. For each entry, write three lines in the team's shared language covering terms, preferences and who to call.
  4. Introduce a backup to each key relationship. Arrange a call or visit with the second person present, and agree who answers when the first is away.
  5. Check signs and system screens. Look at the instructions people actually rely on, and decide which need a second language or a symbol, starting with safety and payment steps.

The business outcome

A business that shares what it knows gives steadier service through absences, onboards new staff more quickly, and suffers fewer errors in credit, delivery and special orders. It also keeps its customer relationships when a key person moves on, which is among the most valuable and least recorded assets in any small firm.

The human dividend

The dividend is predictability. A person who knows the essentials are held somewhere shared can take leave without being rung, and a colleague who covers can answer with confidence. Customers experience the same quality of service every day of the week. The person whose language and relationships carry the business is recognised for them instead of being quietly depended upon.

The trade-offs

  • Writing things down takes time. A short entry per customer is cheap, but a whole customer base is a real project.
  • Translation can distort. Meaning, tone and local expressions may be lost, so key terms should be confirmed by a fluent speaker.
  • Some relationships rest on trust in a person. A shared record supports a relationship, but it does not replace it, and a customer may still prefer their usual contact.
  • Privacy matters. Customer details held in a shared place need sensible access limits and care under the Protection of Personal Information Act.

Why it matters

A business that works in many languages is rich in knowledge, and that richness is wasted when it is locked in one person's head. Sharing it does not flatten the languages people speak. It means that when the one person is away, the rest of the business can still do right by the customer in front of them.
