Contracts & Obligation Management

Nobody owns the obligations that start after a contract is signed

Notice periods, reporting duties and renewal dates sit in signed PDFs nobody watches. Name an owner per contract, extract the obligations into a register and alert ahead of each deadline.

Mellorca Discovery2026-11-13

Picture the commercial manager at a Cape Town security-services company at the start of the new year. She has been asked to prepare for renewal season, and she opens the contracts folder to see what is coming up. Over two weeks, four problems come to light.

Three supplier contracts, for monitoring software, vehicle tracking and a call-centre service, renewed automatically for another year because the notice window closed ninety days before the anniversary and nobody was watching the date. A large customer contract promised a quarterly service report and a rebate if response times were missed. No report was ever produced, and the customer has now asked for the credits. An annual price increase clause was never applied to a group of long-standing contracts, so they have been billed at the original price for three years. And a batch of household contracts passed their fixed term and rolled onto month-to-month without the written expiry notice the Consumer Protection Act requires.

The operating condition is plain: once a contract is signed, the obligations in it, the dates, notice periods, reports, credits and price changes, have no named owner and no calendar. That matters to the CFO, the general counsel and the COO because the cost lands as unwanted renewals, missed revenue and penalties, and it becomes visible in renewal season, when many deadlines arrive together.

The problem in plain language

A contract is a set of promises. Some are made once, at signature, such as the price and scope. Others have to be performed over the life of the agreement: a notice that must be given before a certain date, a report that must be sent every quarter, a review that must be held each year, a price increase that must be applied on a set date, a service level that must be met and measured, a certificate or insurance that must be kept in force.

Most businesses put a great deal of effort into the part before signature. Lawyers draft, commercial teams negotiate, approvers sign off. When the contract is signed it is filed, perhaps in a shared folder, perhaps in a contract system. The people who negotiated it move on to the next deal.

The obligations stay behind. They are written in the contract, usually in clauses spread over many pages, and they have to be performed by people who were not in the negotiation. The account manager may not know that a quarterly report is owed. The finance team may not know that a price review is due. Procurement may not know that a notice must be given in a narrow window.

Nobody is told. Nobody is given a date. No one is named as responsible. So the obligation sits in a PDF until someone happens to read it, usually after the date has passed.

This differs from contract renewals that surprise finance and operations. That topic is about renewal dates. This one is wider: renewals are one kind of obligation, and the same gap that hides a renewal date also hides reports, credits, price changes and notices, which have to be performed and not just noticed.

What the buyer is actually trying to solve

Legal operations and commercial managers put this in practical terms. Which contracts are about to renew, and what do we have to do before they do? What did we promise our customers that we have not delivered? Which of our supplier contracts have notice windows open? Where do we find the obligations without reading every contract? Who is responsible for each one?

The outcome they want is a register of obligations for the contracts that matter, each with an owner, a date, the evidence that it was performed and the consequence if it was not. Alerts reach the owner early enough to act. Renewal season becomes a review of a list, not a search through folders.

Evidence and system mechanism

The law places some obligations on the business, and the clock is fixed

South African consumer law shows how a single post-signature duty can be unforgiving. Section 14 of the Consumer Protection Act, 68 of 2008, as published in the Government Gazette, says that for a fixed-term consumer agreement, not more than 80 and not less than 40 business days before the expiry date the supplier must notify the consumer, in writing or another recordable form, of the impending expiry date. The notice must cover any material changes that would apply if the agreement were renewed or continued, and the options available to the consumer. The text of the section is also reproduced on Acts Online. If nothing further happens, the agreement continues automatically on a month-to-month basis, subject to the notified changes, unless the consumer expressly directs the supplier to terminate it on the expiry date or agrees to a renewal for another fixed term.

The same section says it does not apply to transactions between juristic persons, whatever their turnover or asset value. For businesses that sell fixed-term contracts to individuals, the duty is real, and nobody else will perform it. For businesses buying from other businesses, the law does not provide this protection. The notice windows in a supplier contract are whatever the contract says, and missing them is the buyer's problem.

Either way, the structure is the same: a date that has to be calculated, a notice that has to be sent, a record that has to be kept, and a consequence that follows automatically. Business days exclude weekends and public holidays, so the calendar cannot be worked out by counting on a diary.

The scale of the cost is measured

Research from the contracting profession points to a large gap between what contracts promise and what is delivered. World Commerce & Contracting (WorldCC), the successor to the International Association for Contract and Commercial Management, says in its August 2025 contract management whitepaper that its research finds the average business loses almost 9% of value annually through poor contract management, with average value erosion of 8.6%, and 15% or more in more complex industries. The same page reports that contract-related data is, on average, scattered across 24 different systems, which makes commitments hard to track.

Deloitte's article on post-signature contract management draws on earlier research from the same profession. It says that the majority of the value lost through poor contract management occurs after signature, and that fewer than 30 percent of organisations have centralised or centre-led resources for post-signature management. Most effort goes before signature, where less of the loss occurs.

These figures are broad averages from large samples and are published by organisations that sell contract management services and research. They show the direction and scale of the problem and should not be applied to any single business without checking its own contracts.

Why this becomes urgent

Renewal season is the usual trigger. Many contracts are signed at the same time of year, because budgets, financial years and project cycles cluster. Their anniversaries cluster too. When the season arrives, dozens of dates, notices and decisions fall due within a few weeks.

At that point the business discovers what it has been missing. A notice window passed last month. A rebate is owed. A price clause was never used. The CFO asks for a list and finds that nobody has one. The only way to produce it is to read the contracts.

The pressure is highest when the signs have been building. Each missed renewal or unapplied clause from earlier years has quietly set the pattern, and the season exposes all of them at once.

Economic consequence

The cost of unowned obligations falls in several places:

  • Unwanted renewals. Supplier contracts that roll over for another term, with the cost of a year of service the business meant to end or renegotiate.
  • Missed revenue. Price increases, indexation and minimum volumes that were agreed but never applied or invoiced.
  • Penalties. Service credits, rebates and liquidated damages owed to customers because reports were not sent or targets were not measured, and regulatory consequences where a statutory notice was not given.
  • Negotiation pressure. Renewal talks that begin after the notice window, when the supplier knows the business has no choice but to accept the terms.
  • Search labour. Hours spent finding contracts, reading clauses and answering questions about what was promised, repeated for every audit, dispute and renewal.
  • Contractual risk. Disputes where the business cannot show that it performed, or where the other party relies on a clause the business did not know existed.

As an illustrative example only, suppose three supplier contracts renewed automatically for a further year at a combined cost the business had planned to cut by renegotiation. The saving is lost, the renewal terms are locked in, and the commercial manager spends weeks trying to negotiate an early exit. The amounts are invented, but the sequence is typical: one missed date turns a decision into a commitment.

The same lack of an owner appears in other places. A missed contract date is much like certificates that expire because nobody owns renewal: the date was known to someone at some point, and it was never assigned.

Root cause

The root cause is that signed contracts are filed without anyone being named responsible for their obligations, and the dates and duties are never extracted into a register. The contract becomes a document that has been completed, not a set of promises that has to be performed.

A second cause is that the knowledge sits with the wrong people. The lawyers and negotiators understand the clauses. The people who must act on them, in operations, finance, customer service and procurement, usually never read the contract. Nobody translates the clauses into tasks.

A third cause is that ownership ends at signature. The sponsor of the deal moves on. Staff change roles. Teams reorganise. Contracts outlive the people who understood them, and there is no handover. The pattern matches legacy applications that stay live without an owner: something continues to run, and nobody is responsible for it.

Practical intervention

Practical next actions

  1. Find the contracts. Build one list of the contracts that matter, starting with the highest in value, the longest in term and the most likely to renew automatically. Include customer contracts, supplier contracts, leases, licences and service agreements. Use finance records to find contracts that are not in the legal folder.
  2. Rank by risk and value. Sort the list by annual value, notice period, penalty exposure and regulatory sensitivity, and start with the top group. Do not try to cover every contract at once.
  3. Extract the obligations into a register. For each contract, record each obligation as a line: what is owed, to whom, the trigger date or period, any notice requirement, the owner, the evidence that it was performed and the consequence if it was missed. Include renewal and notice dates, reports, reviews, price changes, service levels, insurance and certificates.
  4. Name an owner and a deputy. For every obligation, assign a role, not only a person, and a deputy. The owner is the person who must act, usually in operations, finance or account management, not just legal.
  5. Calculate dates with a rule, not a diary. For notice periods defined in business days, such as the 80 to 40 business day window under section 14, calculate the dates in a system that knows public holidays. Record the date the window opens and the date it closes.
  6. Set alerts that arrive early. Send reminders to the owner and the deputy well before each window opens, again when it opens and again before it closes, and escalate to a manager if there is no reply.
  7. Send the statutory notices from a controlled process. For fixed-term consumer contracts, generate the expiry notice automatically within the window, keep proof of sending, and record the consumer's response so that month-to-month continuation is a conscious state and not an accident.
  8. Hand over at signature. When a contract is signed, hold a short handover meeting with the people who must perform, walk through the obligations, and confirm the owners in the register.
  9. Review the register regularly. Hold a short monthly review of everything due in the next period, and a fuller review before each renewal season. Update the register when people change roles, contracts are amended or the business is restructured.
  10. Keep the evidence. Store proof that each obligation was performed next to the register line, such as the sent report, the invoice with the price increase or the confirmation of insurance, so that a dispute can be answered quickly.

Diagnostic questions

  • Can we list, today, every contract that will renew in the next six months and the last date to give notice on each?
  • For our ten largest customer contracts, what have we promised to deliver after signature, and who is responsible for each promise?
  • Who owns the obligations in a contract once the deal team has moved on?
  • Which of our contracts are with individual consumers, and who sends the expiry notices?
  • If the person who negotiated a contract left tomorrow, how would we find what we owe under it?
  • When did we last lose money or leverage because a contract date or clause was missed?

What good looks like

There is a register of obligations for the contracts that matter. Every line has an owner and a deputy, a date calculated by rule, the evidence of performance and the consequence of failure. Alerts reach owners early and escalate if ignored. Statutory notices go out automatically with proof. A handover meeting takes place at every signature, and a monthly review covers the next period.

You can measure the result. The number of unplanned renewals falls to zero. The time to answer "what do we owe under this contract?" falls from days to minutes. Price changes and credits are applied when due. Renewal season begins with a list of known dates and decisions, and negotiations start before the notice window closes.

Method note

This article draws on the text of section 14 of the Consumer Protection Act as published in the Government Gazette and reproduced on Acts Online, a WorldCC research whitepaper on contract management and a Deloitte article on post-signature contract management. The scenario and the sums in it are illustrative and not measured. The research figures are broad averages reported by organisations that work in contract management, and should be treated as a guide and not a prediction for any one business. Section 14 does not apply to transactions between juristic persons, and the regulations made under the Act set further rules, such as a general maximum term for fixed-term consumer agreements, so confirm how they apply to your contracts with your legal adviser. The article does not claim that any contract system is needed to fix the problem. It is general information and not legal advice.
