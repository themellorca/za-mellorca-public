Project status stays green because teams report activity, not outcomes

Status reports measure tasks done and meetings held, so a project can be green for months while the result it was meant to deliver slips away. Report against outcome measures and evidence instead.

Mellorca Discovery2026-11-14

A mid-sized insurer in Johannesburg is replacing its policy administration system. For seven months the monthly status report is green. Workshops are held, requirements are signed off, the vendor delivers sprints on schedule and the training plan is drafted. In month eight the first full test with real policy data fails. Data migration has not been proved, two integrations have not been built and the business has not agreed how renewals will work. The project turns red overnight, and the go-live date moves by six months.

None of the green reports were false. Every activity reported was really done. But the reports measured whether the team was busy, not whether the project was moving toward the result it was funded to deliver.

The problem in plain language

Status reports give a project a colour, usually red, amber or green, and a list of what was done. The colour is meant to tell sponsors whether the project will deliver what it promised, on time and within budget.

In practice the colour is often set by the project team, based on whether planned tasks were completed. Tasks are easy to count and easy to complete. Outcomes, such as a migrated data set that reconciles, a process that runs end to end or a benefit that can be measured, are harder to show and come later. So status reports fill with activity, and the colour stays green until the outcome is tested and fails.

What the buyer is actually trying to solve

PMO heads ask why projects go from green to red with no warning. Sponsors ask why they cannot trust status reports. COOs and CIOs ask how to see real progress across a portfolio. The outcome they want is status reporting that reflects whether each project is getting closer to its intended result, with evidence that someone other than the project team can check.

Evidence and system mechanism

Outputs are not outcomes

Atlassian's Team Playbook play on goals, signals and measures draws the distinction plainly. Goals are the outcomes a team is working toward. Signals are indicators that show whether the project is on track. Measures are specific, measurable targets that define success. The play contrasts outputs, individual pieces of work such as creating an app icon, with outcomes, the overall result such as increasing app installs, and warns that focusing on outputs can create a false sense of accomplishment because completed tasks may not show whether the goal is being met. It also advises against binary measures that are simply done or not done.

Status reports that list completed tasks are reports of outputs. They are useful for managing the work but say little about whether the outcome is on track.

Over-optimism is a known pattern

The United Kingdom's National Audit Office, in its insight on over-optimism in government projects, notes that optimism bias in projects is not new but persists, that time and cost tend to be underestimated and benefits overestimated, and that an organisation's culture of challenge is one of the factors that contribute. It calls for decisions based on realistic estimates and assumptions, clear plans for known risks and explicit recognition of uncertainty. Although written about public projects, the pattern applies to any organisation where the people closest to a project also set its status.

Together these explain the mechanism. When status is self-reported and based on activity, optimism fills the gap between work done and results proved, and the colour stays green until reality intervenes.

Why this becomes urgent

Programme growth is the usual trigger. With one or two projects, sponsors know the details and can see through a green report. With a portfolio of ten or twenty, leaders depend on summary status, and a portfolio of green reports hides which projects are actually at risk. The cost becomes visible when several projects turn red late, budgets are overrun and benefits promised to the board do not arrive.

The same trap appears wherever activity is easier to count than results, as described in automation success measured by runs, not business outcomes.

Economic consequence

  • Late discovery of problems. Issues found in month eight cost more to fix than issues found in month two.
  • Budget overruns. Delays extend vendor contracts, contractor costs and the time two systems must run side by side.
  • Unrealised benefits. Savings or revenue the project was meant to produce arrive later or not at all.
  • Poor portfolio decisions. Money keeps flowing to projects that look healthy, while those that need help or should stop are not identified.
  • Loss of trust. Sponsors stop believing status reports and demand more meetings, adding overhead without adding insight.

Root cause

The root cause is that status is defined by the plan rather than by the outcome. If green means "tasks on schedule", a project can be green while nothing it has produced has been proved to work. The second cause is that status is set by the people doing the work, with no rule about what evidence a colour requires and little independent challenge. A third is that outcome measures were never defined when the project was approved, so there is nothing to report against except activity.

The result is similar to the problem of inconsistent definitions in reporting more widely, described in executives get different answers to the same KPI question: the same colour means different things on different projects.

Practical next actions

  1. Define outcome measures at approval. For each project, write down two to four measurable results it must deliver, such as a reconciled data migration, a process running end to end or a benefit target, and when each should be proved.
  2. Add early proof points. Break each outcome into intermediate proofs that can be shown early, such as a trial migration of one product line or one integration working with real data.
  3. Set evidence rules for each colour. Write down what a project must show to report green, amber or red, based on proof points achieved, not tasks completed.
  4. Report outcomes first. Restructure the status report so the first section shows progress against outcome measures and proof points, and activity comes after.
  5. Introduce independent challenge. Have the PMO, a peer project lead or an assurance reviewer check the evidence behind the status of larger projects each month.
  6. Track the history of colours. Record how often projects jump from green to red, and review those cases to improve the evidence rules.
  7. Make red safe. Make it clear to teams that early amber or red with a recovery plan is valued, and that late surprises are what leaders want to avoid.

Diagnostic questions

  • For each active project, what outcomes must it deliver, and have any been proved yet?
  • Who decides a project's status colour, and what evidence do they need?
  • How many projects in the last two years went from green to red within one reporting period?
  • Does the status report lead with outcomes or with completed tasks?
  • Does anyone outside the project check the evidence behind a green report?

What good looks like

Every project has agreed outcome measures and early proof points. Status colours follow written evidence rules, and the PMO or an assurance reviewer challenges the evidence on significant projects. Status reports show progress toward outcomes first. Projects turn amber early, when there is still time to act, and sudden jumps from green to red become rare. Leaders can see across the portfolio which projects are truly on track, and the roadmap reflects reality, supported by the kind of planning described in the digital systems architecture and roadmap service.
