## "Can you just sort that out?"

The operations manager of a property maintenance firm in Port Elizabeth walks through the office on Monday morning. In ten minutes she asks one coordinator to get a quote from the plumbing supplier, tells another to call a tenant about a leak, mentions to a technician that the generator at one complex needs a service, and reminds the receptionist to renew the vehicle licence on a bakkie. Everyone nods. By Friday, the quote has been requested, the tenant has not been called, the generator is forgotten and the licence is renewed a week late with a penalty. Nobody refused the work. It simply was never written down, and each person remembered it differently or not at all.

This is one of the most ordinary ways that work goes missing. Delegation by conversation is fast and natural, especially in smaller businesses where people sit close together. It also leaves no record of who owns a task, what exactly was asked, or when it is due.

## Why spoken assignments fail

A verbal request carries three weaknesses. First, it relies on both people remembering it in the same way. The manager believes she asked for the generator to be serviced this week; the technician heard that it should be looked at some time. Second, it competes with every other demand on the person's day, and anything not written down is the first thing to drop when urgent work arrives. Third, it is invisible to everyone else. If the person who received the task is off sick, nobody else knows it exists.

The same problem appears in phone calls, voice notes and the end of meetings, when someone says "I'll take that" and the meeting moves on. The related piece on [employees being unsure who owns the next action](/impact/employees-unsure-who-owns-next-action-unclear-accountability-labour-cost/) looks at accountability across processes; this article is about the moment of assignment itself, where the loss usually begins.

## What disappearing tasks cost

### Customer and tenant impact

Many verbally assigned tasks are promises to customers: a call back, a quote, a visit, a document. When those slip, the customer experiences the business as unreliable, and some will take their work elsewhere.

### Penalties and avoidable expenses

Licence renewals, compliance submissions, insurance documents and contract notices all have deadlines. A task that was mentioned but not recorded can turn into a late fee, a lapsed cover or a missed notice period that locks the business into another year of a contract.

### Management time spent re-asking

Managers who delegate verbally soon learn they need to check. They follow up in person, send reminder messages and ask in meetings whether things were done. That policing consumes hours each week, and the article on [checking whether recurring tasks were completed](/impact/checking-recurring-tasks-completed-management-policing-cost/) shows how this becomes a permanent overhead.

### Duplicated and conflicting work

When a task is mentioned to two people, or a manager forgets who they asked, the work may be done twice, or done in conflicting ways. Suppliers receive two quote requests; customers receive two different answers.

### Friction and blame

When a missed task surfaces, the conversation turns to who said what. Without a record, both sides believe they are right, and trust erodes on both sides.

## A hypothetical estimate

The following figures are illustrative assumptions. Suppose a manager assigns 25 tasks a week verbally and one in five slips by at least a week or is forgotten. That is five lost or late tasks a week, about 250 a year. If one in ten of those leads to a direct cost, such as a penalty, a lost job or an emergency call-out, averaging R3,000, the direct cost is about R75,000 a year. Add an hour a day of the manager's time spent following up, and the annual cost of informal delegation grows well beyond what a shared task list costs to run.

## Why businesses tolerate it

Writing tasks down feels bureaucratic, and many teams associate task systems with large projects rather than daily work. Managers worry that formal tasks will seem like distrust. Staff often do not want another app to check. And because most verbal tasks do get done, the ones that fail seem like individual lapses rather than a process problem.

## What a lightweight capture habit looks like

The fix does not need a project management system. It needs one shared place where every assigned task is captured with three pieces of information: who owns it, what done looks like, and when it is due. Most businesses already have a tool that can do this. Microsoft's guidance on [adding a task list to meeting notes](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/add-a-task-list-to-meeting-notes-94383070-5f2f-4954-8607-7b7ebff5d43e) describes follow-up tasks with assignees and due dates that stay in sync with a Planner plan, and appear in each person's assigned tasks. Customer relationship tools offer the same for customer-facing work: HubSpot's documentation on [creating tasks](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/tasks/create-tasks) describes tasks with an assignee, due date and reminder, linked to the relevant customer record so the history stays with the customer.

The habit matters more than the tool. When a manager asks someone to do something, either person records it in the shared list before the conversation ends. Meetings end with a quick read-back of tasks agreed. Each week, overdue items are reviewed openly. The guide on [making ownership clear at every stage](/insights/how-to-make-ownership-clear-at-every-stage/) covers the principles, and the article on [meetings that end without decisions](/impact/meetings-without-decisions-payroll-cost/) addresses a closely related cost.

## Practical next actions

1. **Track one week of verbal assignments.** Ask managers to jot down every task they hand out by conversation, then check how many were completed on time.
2. **Choose one shared task list.** Use a tool the team already has, such as the task features in your office suite or CRM, and avoid adding a new system if possible.
3. **Record owner, outcome and due date.** Make these three fields the minimum for every task, and keep descriptions short and specific.
4. **Capture before the conversation ends.** Agree that whoever gives or receives a task records it immediately, including tasks agreed in phone calls and meetings.
5. **Link customer tasks to customer records.** Put call-backs, quotes and visits against the customer in your CRM or job system so anyone can see what was promised.
6. **Review overdue tasks weekly.** Spend ten minutes in a team meeting on what is late and why, rather than chasing individuals throughout the week.

Spoken delegation is quick, but it moves the cost from the moment of asking to every day afterwards. Writing tasks down at the point of assignment keeps promises visible and frees managers from acting as the business's memory.
