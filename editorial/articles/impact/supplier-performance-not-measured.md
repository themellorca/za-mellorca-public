## The tolerated pain

Supplier management often collapses into invoice approval and incident escalation. Without measurable service levels, performance data and regular review, the buyer cannot distinguish acceptable delivery from chronic underperformance.

The invoice arrives every month for the same amount. Whether the supplier resolved incidents on time, delivered complete orders or met the agreed response times is rarely checked against it. Problems are raised when they hurt, discussed in a meeting, and then forgotten until the next one. Over a contract term, the business can pay full price for a service that has quietly drifted well below what was agreed.

## Why businesses tolerate it

Measuring a supplier takes deliberate effort. Someone must decide what good looks like, find where the evidence lives and keep a record month after month. Most contract owners are busy with their actual jobs, and the contract itself was often signed with vague service descriptions or with SLAs that nobody operationalised.

There is also a relationship instinct. Raising performance formally can feel confrontational, especially with a long-standing supplier. So the business adapts instead: staff learn workarounds, managers escalate informally and the gap between contract and reality becomes normal. The longer this continues, the harder it is to challenge, because there is no baseline to point to.

## Operational consequence

When supplier performance is not measured, internal teams become the buffer. Employees re-check deliveries, chase late responses, rework incorrect outputs and explain delays to customers. IT coordinators log the same recurring fault repeatedly. Operations managers spend supplier meetings arguing over impressions rather than reviewing evidence. None of this effort appears on the supplier's invoice, yet the business pays for all of it in salaried time.

Recurring failure also hides root causes. Without a record of which incidents breached which commitment, neither party can see patterns: the same system failing every month-end, the same delivery route arriving late or the same type of request being mishandled. Each failure is treated as a fresh event, so the fix is always a workaround rather than a correction, and the supplier is never asked to address the underlying cause because nobody can show that one exists.

## How it becomes money

- the business pays full price for service below expectation;
- employees absorb supplier failures through workarounds and manual recovery;
- recurring incidents consume management and support time;
- contract remedies cannot be used because evidence is incomplete;
- renewal decisions are made from perception rather than performance.

The UK government's commercial standard, [GovS 008](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/government-functional-standard-govs-008-commercial-and-commercial-continuous-improvement-assessment-framework/government-functional-standard-govs-008-commercial-html), expects contract managers to track delivery against contractual obligations, including key performance indicators, and to take preventative or corrective action within the terms of the contract where performance falls short. The principle applies well beyond the public sector: a remedy that cannot be evidenced cannot be enforced.

## A worked cost model

The figures below are illustrative assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic. They are not benchmarks for any supplier or sector.

| Input | Illustrative value |
| --- | --- |
| Managed IT support fee | R85,000 a month (R1,020,000 a year) |
| Priority incidents per month | 30 |
| Incidents resolved outside the agreed time | 12 a month |
| Internal workaround and recovery time per breach | 4 hours at R300 an hour (loaded) |
| Internal chasing time per breach | 1.5 hours at R400 an hour (loaded) |
| Service credit if fewer than 90% of incidents meet the SLA | 5% of the monthly fee |

**Absorbed internal cost per breach:** R1,200 workaround time + R600 chasing time = R1,800.

**Annual absorbed cost:** 12 breaches × R1,800 × 12 months = R259,200.

**Unclaimed service credit:** 18 of 30 incidents met the SLA, so attainment is 60%, below the 90% threshold. The credit would be 5% × R85,000 = R4,250 a month, or R51,000 a year. Without a performance record, it is never claimed.

**Total illustrative exposure:** R259,200 + R51,000 = R310,200 a year, equivalent to about 30.4% of the annual contract value. Management meeting time spent debating anecdotes is excluded, as is any customer impact, because neither can be valued without evidence.

The point is not the specific total. It is that the true cost of a supplier is the invoice plus the internal effort spent compensating for it, and only measurement makes the second number visible.

## Secondary consequences

The cost compounds over a contract term. A supplier that is never measured has little reason to prioritise your account over a client that reviews performance monthly. Service quality can drift slowly, and because there is no baseline, each small decline is absorbed as the new normal. By the time the business decides to act, it may be locked into a multi-year renewal with notice periods that have already passed.

Weak measurement also undermines switching. If the business tenders the service, it cannot tell prospective suppliers what volumes, response times and failure patterns they must handle, so bids are priced on assumptions and the new contract repeats the vagueness of the old one. And where suppliers support critical systems, unmeasured performance becomes a continuity risk: the business learns how dependent it is on a supplier only when that supplier fails at a bad moment.

Finally, unmeasured suppliers distort internal decisions. Teams that have learned to work around a poor service often ask for extra headcount or tools to compensate, so supplier underperformance quietly reappears as internal cost elsewhere in the budget.

## Commercial urgency

Urgency peaks before a renewal, a price increase or a material scope change, because each is a point where the business either uses evidence or signs again on perception. UK [guidance on key performance indicators](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/66b21af7a3c2a28abb50dd86/Guidance_-_KPIs_FINAL.pdf) under the Procurement Act 2023 shows what disciplined practice looks like: for public contracts above £5 million, authorities must generally set at least three KPIs and assess performance against them at least once in every twelve-month period, rating it from good to inadequate. A private business is not bound by that rule, but the logic carries over. If performance has not been assessed in the last year, the next commercial decision about that supplier is being made without the evidence it needs. Starting to measure now, even with a few simple indicators, gives the business a baseline before that decision arrives. Related risks are covered in [supplier contracts renewing without review](/impact/supplier-contracts-renewing-without-review/).

:::callout Commercial signal
If the supplier meeting is dominated by anecdotes because neither party has a trusted performance record, value-for-money control is weak.
:::

## Measure the outcome that matters

Good KPIs are not a large dashboard. They are a small set of measurable obligations tied to the business outcome: availability, response, resolution, accuracy, delivery timeliness, defect rate or another service-specific measure.

Current UK government commercial standards state that supplier performance should be monitored against contractual obligations and KPIs, with corrective action where requirements are not met.

## What better looks like

The contract defines measurable expectations; the operating process captures evidence; exceptions trigger review; repeated failures have owners and remediation dates; and commercial decisions use the same performance record.

## Practical actions

1. List critical supplier outcomes and contractual obligations.
2. Choose a small number of measurable KPIs and SLAs.
3. Define data source, owner, frequency and threshold for each measure.
4. Track failures, recurrence and corrective actions.
5. Review performance before invoices, renewals and material scope changes.

## Bottom line

Supplier performance that is not measured becomes a matter of opinion. A business should be able to show what it bought, how delivery is measured, where performance failed and what commercial response followed.
