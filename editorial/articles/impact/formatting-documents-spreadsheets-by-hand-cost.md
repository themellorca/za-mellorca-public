## The tolerated pain

A proposal is nearly ready, but the headings are in three different fonts because sections were pasted from older documents. The numbering restarts halfway through. Someone spends an hour making it look consistent. A spreadsheet exported from the accounting system arrives with merged cells, inconsistent date formats and no column headings, so an analyst spends the morning tidying it before any real work can start. A project tracker is colour-coded by hand, so every status change means recolouring cells.

None of this is difficult. It is just slow, repetitive and, crucially, repeated. The same tidying happens on the next proposal, the next export and the next status update. The output looks professional, but the effort creates little value beyond what the tools could have done automatically.

This article is about formatting, not about creating documents from scratch. The related cost of producing the same documents repeatedly is a separate problem, and the broader issue of skilled people absorbing clerical work is covered in [the cost of high-value employees doing low-value admin](/impact/high-value-employees-doing-low-value-admin/).

## Why businesses tolerate it

- **It feels like quality.** A well-presented document matters to clients, so time spent on appearance seems justified.
- **People were never shown the features.** Many capable professionals format by hand because nobody taught them styles, tables or rules.
- **Templates have decayed.** The company template was built years ago, has been copied and edited many times, and no longer behaves predictably.
- **The time is fragmented.** Twenty minutes here and an hour there never appear as a single cost.
- **Exports are messy by default.** Data coming out of business systems is rarely in the shape people need, so tidying it becomes part of the job.

## Operational symptoms

- documents that look different depending on who produced them;
- last-minute formatting before every client submission or board pack;
- spreadsheets with manual colour-coding that is out of date as soon as data changes;
- analysts spending the start of each task cleaning exported data;
- broken tables of contents, numbering and cross-references;
- errors introduced while reformatting, such as rows deleted or values overwritten.

## How the pain becomes money

- **Labour cost:** paid hours spent on appearance and tidying rather than content or analysis.
- **Error cost:** manual reformatting of numbers and tables introduces mistakes.
- **Delay cost:** proposals, quotes and reports leave later than they could.
- **Brand cost:** inconsistent documents look less professional to clients, despite the effort.
- **Opportunity cost:** the people doing the formatting are often the ones who should be writing, selling or analysing.

## An illustrative cost model

This example is **illustrative**. Every figure is an assumption for a hypothetical business. Picture a Pretoria consulting engineering firm with 30 professional and administrative staff who regularly produce reports, proposals and spreadsheets.

| Assumption | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Staff who regularly format documents or spreadsheets | 20 |
| Hours per person per week spent on manual formatting and tidying | 2.5 |
| Share of that time removable with styles, tables, rules and better templates | 60% |
| Average loaded hourly cost | R400 |
| Working weeks per year | 46 |

- Total formatting time: 20 × 2.5 hours × 46 weeks = 2,300 hours a year
- Removable time: 2,300 × 60% = 1,380 hours
- **Illustrative avoidable cost: 1,380 × R400 = R552,000 a year**

The figure is sensitive to the assumptions, especially hours per week. Before relying on it, ask a few people to log their formatting time for two weeks. Even if the real number is half the assumption, the cost of a short training session and a rebuilt template is small by comparison.

## When it becomes commercially urgent

- proposal and tender deadlines are regularly missed or met with all-nighters;
- clients comment on inconsistent or error-prone documents;
- a rebrand or new template is planned, which is the natural moment to fix the underlying structure;
- the business is hiring, so poor habits are about to be taught to new people;
- analysts say they spend more time cleaning data than analysing it.

## What better looks like

Most of the fix lies in features of tools the business already pays for. They are underused, not missing.

### Documents: use styles, not manual formatting

Microsoft's guide to [customising or creating styles in Word](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/word/customize-or-create-new-styles) explains that styles apply a set of formatting choices consistently throughout a document, and that when a style is modified, all text using that style updates automatically to match. A style can also be saved to the template so it is available in new documents. In practice, that means headings, body text, captions and tables are defined once. Nobody needs to fix fonts by hand, and a table of contents builds itself.

### Spreadsheets: use tables and rules

Microsoft's guide to [formatting an Excel table](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/excel/format-an-excel-table) describes built-in table styles that format headers, banded rows and total rows in one step, and custom table styles where the defaults do not fit, with the caveat that custom styles are stored in the current workbook only. Its guide to [conditional formatting](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/use-conditional-formatting-to-highlight-information-in-excel-fed60dfa-1d3f-4e13-9ecb-f1951ff89d7f) explains how colour scales, data bars, icon sets and rules apply formatting automatically based on cell values. A status column that turns red when a date passes no longer needs anyone to recolour it.

### Exports: shape data once

Where tidying exported data is a weekly ritual, the steps can usually be recorded once and replayed on each new export. If the same export is being reshaped for the same report every week, that is a sign the work belongs in an automated process, a theme explored in [the payroll cost of repeatable manual work](/impact/repeatable-manual-work-payroll-cost-automation/).

The common thread is separating content from presentation. People write the content and enter the data. The tool applies the formatting according to rules set once.

### Presentation still matters

None of this means appearance is unimportant. A tender response, an annual report or a client proposal deserves care, and some documents will always need a designer's eye. The point is to spend that care deliberately, on the documents where it changes the outcome, rather than spreading it thinly across every internal memo and tracker. A good test is to ask who will read the document and whether the extra hour of polish would change what they decide. For most internal documents, a consistent style applied automatically is more than enough. The human cost of skilled people reduced to mechanical tidying is captured in [the skilled professional reduced to copy and paste](/moments/the-skilled-professional-reduced-to-copy-and-paste/).

## Practical first steps

1. **Measure for two weeks.** Ask a sample of staff to note time spent purely on formatting and tidying.
2. **Fix the template.** Rebuild the main document template with proper heading, body, table and caption styles, and retire old copies.
3. **Run a short session.** Thirty minutes on styles, tables and conditional formatting often changes habits more than any tool purchase.
4. **Target the worst spreadsheet.** Convert the most heavily hand-formatted tracker into a table with rules.
5. **Look at recurring exports.** Identify any data that is cleaned the same way every week and automate the cleaning.

It helps to be clear about which work genuinely needs a person. The distinction is set out in [the difference between necessary human work and avoidable manual work](/insights/the-difference-between-necessary-human-work-and-avoidable-manual-work/).

## The bottom line

Manual formatting is a small task repeated thousands of times a year. Its cost is real but invisible, and it falls mostly on the people the business would rather have writing, selling and analysing. Styles, tables, rules and a working template cost little and return those hours.
