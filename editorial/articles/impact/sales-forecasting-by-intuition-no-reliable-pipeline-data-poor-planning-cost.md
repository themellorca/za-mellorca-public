## Last year plus ten percent

Every November, the owner of an industrial packaging manufacturer in Ekurhuleni sits down with his sales manager to plan the coming year. They take last year's sales, add a percentage that feels right, and adjust for the customers they are worried about or excited about. That number drives raw material purchasing, shift patterns, overtime budgets and the bank's working capital facility. In one year, demand runs well ahead of the plan: the factory runs short of board, pays premium prices for urgent supply and loses two large orders to a competitor with stock. The next year, the plan is raised, demand softens, and the business sits on R3 million of excess material while paying overtime it did not need in the first quarter.

The example is illustrative, but the approach is extremely common. The business does not forecast badly because its managers lack judgement. It forecasts by intuition because it has no reliable record of what is actually in the sales pipeline: which opportunities exist, what they are worth, how far they have progressed and when they are likely to close.

## What is missing

A pipeline-based forecast needs a small set of facts for each potential sale, kept current:

- **An estimated value**, even if approximate.
- **A stage** that reflects real progress, such as enquiry, quoted, negotiating or verbally agreed.
- **An expected close date** that is reviewed rather than set once and forgotten.
- **An owner** who is responsible for keeping the record up to date.

In many small and mid-sized businesses, these facts are scattered across quotes in email, notes in salespeople's diaries and conversations that were never recorded. A CRM may exist but is used as a contact list. Repeat business from existing customers, often the largest share of revenue, is not recorded as pipeline at all, because it is assumed to continue. The insight on [how bad CRM data gets created](/insights/how-bad-crm-data-gets-created/) describes how the gaps form.

## How guesswork turns into cost

### Stock and materials

Purchasing follows the forecast. Overestimates tie up cash in stock and raw materials, increase storage costs and raise the risk of obsolescence. Underestimates create shortages, urgent purchases at higher prices and lost orders.

### Staffing and capacity

Hiring, shifts, contractors and overtime are planned from expected volume. When the forecast is wrong, the business either carries idle capacity or pays premium rates to catch up, and service suffers in both cases.

### Cash and funding

Cash-flow plans and borrowing needs rest on expected sales. An unreliable sales forecast makes the cash forecast unreliable too, as the article on [cash-flow forecasts built on incomplete information](/impact/cash-flow-forecasts-built-on-incomplete-outdated-information-liquidity-planning-cost/) explains. Banks and investors also lose confidence in plans that repeatedly miss.

### Late reaction to change

Without pipeline data, the first sign of a slowdown is a weak month of invoicing, by which time the next quarter's pipeline has already thinned. With pipeline data, a falling number of new quotes or stalled deals shows the trend months earlier, when there is still time to respond.

### Targets nobody believes

Sales targets set from an intuitive forecast feel arbitrary to the team. When they are missed, nobody can tell whether the problem was the plan or the selling.

## A hypothetical estimate

The following figures are illustrative. Suppose a manufacturer with R60 million in annual sales buys raw material worth about 40 percent of sales value. If an intuitive forecast overshoots actual sales by R2.5 million over two quarters and purchasing follows the forecast, about R1 million of excess material builds up. At an annual carrying cost of 20 percent for financing, storage, handling and obsolescence, that excess costs about R200,000 a year. A similar undershoot would cost lost sales and premium purchasing instead. Your own figures will differ, but the asymmetry is the point: both errors cost money.

## Why intuition persists

Experienced owners and sales managers often do have good instincts, and intuitive forecasts are sometimes close. Recording pipeline data feels like administration, and salespeople see little personal benefit. CRM systems have been introduced before and abandoned. And forecasting is done once a year, so the cost of a poor method is not felt week to week. The discovery notes on [CRM updates happening only before forecast meetings](/discovery/why-crm-updates-happen-only-before-forecast-meetings/) and [dead deals staying open in the CRM](/discovery/why-dead-deals-stay-open-in-the-crm-and-distort-the-sales-forecast/) describe what happens when data is captured half-heartedly.

## Building the minimum data for a real forecast

The aim is not a sophisticated model. It is enough recorded evidence to replace pure judgement with judgement informed by data. Mainstream CRM tools are built for this once the data exists. HubSpot's guide to [setting up its forecast tool](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/forecast/set-up-the-forecast-tool) describes mapping each pipeline's deal stages to forecast categories such as pipeline, best case and commit, and showing a weighted amount that multiplies deal value by the deal's probability. Microsoft's guidance on [configuring forecasts](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/sales/configure-forecast) describes forecasts built from the sales pipeline to show revenue projections, highlight risk early and let managers adjust values for insights not yet in the opportunity records.

None of this works without the basic discipline of recording deals, keeping stages honest and reviewing close dates. Start small: record every quote over a set value, include expected repeat orders from key customers, and review the list weekly.

## Practical next actions

1. **Define a simple set of stages.** Agree four or five stages with clear entry criteria, so that "quoted" or "negotiating" means the same thing for everyone.
2. **Record every opportunity above a threshold.** Capture value, stage, expected close date and owner for all quotes and enquiries above an agreed amount.
3. **Include expected repeat business.** Add forecast orders from major existing customers, based on their history and any known changes.
4. **Review close dates weekly.** Move or close deals whose dates have passed, so the pipeline reflects reality rather than hope.
5. **Compare forecast with actual each month.** Track how far the forecast was from actual sales and use the gap to adjust stage probabilities.
6. **Connect the forecast to planning.** Use the pipeline-based forecast, adjusted by management judgement, to drive purchasing, staffing and cash plans, and update them quarterly.

Intuition is valuable, but it is not data. A business that captures the basic facts of its pipeline can plan stock, people and cash with far less waste, and can see a change in demand while there is still time to act.
