## The tolerated pain: paper first, keyboard later

Plenty of South African businesses still begin important processes on paper. A technician fills in a job card on site. A new employee completes an onboarding pack at reception. A driver signs a delivery note. A customer completes a credit application, a client intake form or a site inspection checklist with a pen. Hours or days later, someone in the office reads that paper and types the same information into the accounting package, the payroll system, the CRM or a spreadsheet.

The process works, in the sense that the information eventually arrives. But every form is handled at least twice: once to write it and once to capture it. Often it is handled more times than that, when the capturer cannot read the handwriting, finds a field blank or has to phone the person who completed it.

Mellorca has written about [duplicated data entry between systems](/impact/pain-employees-duplicated-data-entry/). This article deals with a specific and common variant: the process is designed to start on paper, so double handling is built in from the first step.

## Why businesses tolerate it

- **"We have always done it this way."** Paper forms predate the systems that now hold the data, and nobody has revisited the process since.
- **Signatures.** Many managers assume a document is only valid if it is signed in ink. That belief keeps forms on paper long after the data moved online.
- **Field conditions.** Teams working on sites, farms or in vehicles may have patchy connectivity, so paper feels safer than an app.
- **Cheap at the point of use.** A printed form costs very little. The capturing cost is borne later, by someone else, in a different budget.
- **Invisible labour.** Capture work is absorbed into an administrator's day and rarely measured as a distinct activity.

### The signature assumption deserves a closer look

South Africa's Electronic Communications and Transactions Act (ECTA) gives legal recognition to information in electronic form. Section 11 states that information is not without legal force merely because it is in the form of a data message. Section 12 provides that a legal requirement for writing is met by a data message that is accessible for subsequent reference. Section 13 covers signatures: where parties themselves require a signature, a method that identifies the person and indicates their approval, and is as reliable as appropriate for the purpose, can satisfy that requirement. Where a law requires a signature without specifying the type, an advanced electronic signature is needed.

As ENSafrica's commentary on the Act notes, some transactions are excluded from electronic conclusion, including sales of immovable property, long-term land leases, wills and bills of exchange. So the answer is not "nothing needs paper". It is that most routine operational forms, such as job cards, delivery confirmations, leave applications, inspection checklists and internal approvals, do not need to start on paper for legal reasons. Businesses should take their own legal advice for specific documents, but the assumption that ink is always required is frequently wrong.

## Operational consequence

Paper-first processes produce a recognisable set of symptoms:

- **Capture backlogs.** Forms pile up in trays and are captured in batches, so the system is always behind reality.
- **Transcription errors.** Misread handwriting, transposed digits and skipped fields enter the system and are then trusted as if they were correct.
- **Chasing incomplete forms.** A blank ID number or missing signature is only noticed at capture, days later, when the person who completed it is hard to reach.
- **Lost and damaged paper.** Forms go missing between site and office, get wet, or are filed before capture.
- **Delayed downstream work.** Invoicing waits for job cards, payroll waits for timesheets and onboarding waits for forms, so cash and decisions wait too.
- **Storage and retrieval effort.** Original paper must still be filed and found later, which adds a second records system alongside the digital one.

There is also a security dimension. Section 19 of the Protection of Personal Information Act requires a responsible party to secure the integrity and confidentiality of personal information through appropriate, reasonable technical and organisational measures, to identify foreseeable risks and to verify that safeguards work. Paper forms containing ID numbers, banking details or medical information, sitting in open trays or vehicle cubbyholes before capture, are a foreseeable risk that has to be managed.

## How the pain becomes money

- **Labour cost:** paid time spent typing information that already existed in written form.
- **Error and rework cost:** wrong account numbers, quantities or addresses lead to incorrect invoices, failed payments, returned deliveries and corrections.
- **Cash-flow impact:** when billing depends on paper job cards reaching the office, invoices go out later and cash arrives later. Mellorca covers this mechanism in [late invoicing and manual billing](/impact/late-invoicing-manual-billing-cash-flow-cost/).
- **Revenue leakage:** lost job cards or unrecorded extra work mean some billable work is simply never invoiced.
- **Risk exposure:** unsecured personal information on paper increases the consequences of loss or unauthorised access.

## A transparent cost model

The cost of a paper-first process can be estimated from internal data without any external benchmark:

> **Monthly double-handling cost**
> forms per month × average capture minutes per form ÷ 60 × loaded hourly cost of the capturer
> + forms needing follow-up × average follow-up minutes ÷ 60 × loaded hourly cost
> + value of work not billed because forms were lost or incomplete
> + financing cost of the extra days between work done and invoice issued

*Illustrative example.* A facilities maintenance company in Cape Town with 25 field technicians uses carbon-copy job cards. Technicians hand them in at the depot at the end of the week. An administrator captures them into the accounting system on Mondays and Tuesdays, then invoices are raised. The company times capture for a fortnight and records every card that needed a phone call to clarify. It also compares the date on each job card with the invoice date. The exercise shows three things in its own data: most of two administrative days a week go to capture; a noticeable share of cards need a follow-up call; and invoices are raised several days after the work is done. Two cards that never reached the depot are only discovered when a customer queries why they were not billed. The company can now put a rand figure on each of these lines using its own wage and debtor data.

## When it becomes commercially urgent

- capture staff are the bottleneck before invoicing, payroll or onboarding can proceed;
- volumes are growing and the business is considering hiring another administrator to keep up;
- customers dispute invoices because quantities or details were transcribed incorrectly;
- a lost or exposed form containing personal information becomes a POPIA concern;
- management wants same-day visibility of field work and cannot get it.

If the first response to growth is another capturer, the process is turning revenue growth into overhead growth, a pattern discussed in [why revenue growth requires more admin staff](/patterns/why-revenue-growth-requires-more-admin-staff/).

## What better looks like

The information is captured once, at the source, by the person who knows it, in a structured form that validates required fields before submission. It then flows to the systems that need it without retyping.

That does not require an expensive platform. Depending on the process, it may be a mobile form that works offline and syncs later, a web form linked to the CRM or accounting system, or an electronic signature on a delivery confirmation captured on a phone. The design principles matter more than the tool:

- make the most error-prone fields structured choices rather than free text;
- validate required fields at the point of capture, not days later;
- attach photos where they replace descriptions, such as a completed installation or a damaged delivery;
- route the data automatically to the next step, such as invoicing or approval;
- secure personal information from the moment it is captured.

## Practical remediation steps

1. **Inventory the paper forms** that are later captured digitally, with monthly volumes and who captures them.
2. **Time capture and follow-up** for two weeks to populate the cost model.
3. **Check the legal assumption.** For each form, confirm whether any law genuinely requires paper or a particular type of signature. Most operational forms will not.
4. **Pick the form closest to cash**, usually job cards, delivery notes or timesheets, and digitise that one first.
5. **Design for the field.** Test offline use, large buttons and photo capture with the people who will actually fill it in.
6. **Connect the output** to the system that needs it, so the office stops retyping.
7. **Retire the paper version** once the digital form is proven, rather than running both indefinitely.

Mellorca's [business automation](/services/business-automation/) work typically begins with this kind of inventory, because the paper forms closest to invoicing usually pay for the rest of the change.

## Bottom line

A paper form that is later typed into a system is a process designed to be done twice. The business pays for the capture, pays again for the errors it introduces and waits longer for the cash it supports. Capturing information once, at the source, removes a whole layer of administration and makes the data more reliable at the same time.
