## The tolerated pain

Files live across email, shared drives, chat, personal folders and multiple cloud platforms. Employees learn informal shortcuts: ask the person who usually knows, search several places, or recreate the document. None of these feels like a failure in the moment. Each one is a small, sensible workaround by a capable person trying to get on with the job.

That is exactly why the problem survives. Searching is productive when it is part of analysis: comparing sources, testing an assumption, looking for something genuinely new. It is waste when employees repeatedly hunt for routine information that the operating environment should make easy to find, such as the current price list, the signed version of a contract, the latest client brief or the approved template.

## Why businesses tolerate it

Poor findability rarely arrives as a decision. It accumulates. A new collaboration tool is added without retiring the old shared drive. Teams create their own folder structures. Attachments are emailed rather than linked, so several copies drift apart. Nobody owns the information architecture, so nobody is accountable for its quality. Microsoft's guidance on [SharePoint information architecture](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sharepoint/information-architecture-modern-experience) warns that deeply nested folders create a significant discoverability burden and recommends metadata and topic-specific libraries instead.

The cost is also hard to see because it never appears as a line item. Time spent searching is buried inside salaries that are paid regardless. Managers notice delays and frustration, but rarely connect them to the way information is stored.

The experience is common. In Microsoft's 2023 Work Trend Index, based on a survey of 31,000 workers across 31 markets, [62% of respondents said they struggle with too much time spent searching for information in their workday](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/will-ai-fix-work). That tells us the pain is widely felt. It does not tell us how many hours your own business loses, which still has to be measured locally.

## Operational consequence

The cost is distributed across many small interruptions. Work starts later, handoffs depend on memory, duplicate documents appear and specialists become human search engines for the rest of the organisation. The symptoms are recognisable:

- **Delayed starts:** a task cannot begin until someone finds the right input, so a ten-minute job takes forty.
- **Version confusion:** two people work from different copies of the same document, and the error is discovered only at review or, worse, by the customer.
- **Recreation:** it feels quicker to rebuild a proposal, spreadsheet or procedure than to find the original, so the business pays twice for the same work.
- **Specialist interruption:** experienced staff are repeatedly asked where things are, which fragments the time the business most needs them to spend on difficult work.
- **Slow onboarding:** new employees cannot become productive until they learn the informal map of where information really lives.

There is a risk dimension too. When information is scattered across personal folders and inboxes, the business cannot be confident who has access to what, or which version is authoritative when a decision is challenged.

## How it becomes money

Measure the problem using observed search and recreation time rather than an assumed industry percentage.

> monthly search cost = employees affected × average search hours per month × loaded hourly cost

Microsoft has published research indicating that information workers can lose meaningful time searching for or recreating information. Its white paper [Knowledge Sharing in a Changing World](https://cdn-dynmedia-1.microsoft.com/is/content/microsoftcorp/microsoft/final/en-us/microsoft-brand/documents/Knowledge-Sharing-in-a-Changing-World.pdf) reports Spiceworks Ziff Davis research suggesting organisations could potentially save employees five to eight weeks of productivity a year if they could efficiently find and use available knowledge. That survey was of US-based organisations and was published by a vendor with knowledge-management products to sell, so treat it as an indication of scale, not as your number. The exact percentage in any business should still be measured locally rather than copied into a business case.

### An illustrative cost model

The following example is **illustrative**. Every figure is an assumption chosen to show the arithmetic, not a benchmark.

| Cost element | Illustrative assumption | Annual cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Avoidable search time | 40 employees × 4 hours per month × R250 loaded hourly cost × 12 months | R480,000 |
| Recreated documents | 6 documents per month × 3 hours each × R250 × 12 months | R54,000 |
| Specialist interruptions | 2 specialists × 5 hours per month × R400 loaded hourly cost × 12 months | R48,000 |
| **Total** |  | **R582,000** |

The arithmetic: 40 × 4 × R250 = R40,000 per month, or R480,000 a year. Six recreated documents at three hours each is 18 hours, or R4,500 per month and R54,000 a year. Two specialists losing five hours each is 10 hours at R400, or R4,000 per month and R48,000 a year.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, four hours a month is a little over 11 minutes a working day, assuming about 21 working days; replace it with what your own staff report or what a short time study shows. Second, recovered time is capacity, not automatically cash. It becomes financial value only when it is redirected to billable work, faster delivery, avoided hiring or reduced overtime. If a realistic improvement recovered half the avoidable search time, the capacity released in this example would be worth R240,000 a year.

## When it becomes commercially urgent

If staff routinely ask colleagues where files are, recreate known documents or search several repositories before normal work can begin, information architecture is consuming paid capacity.

Urgency rises further when the business is growing, because every new employee multiplies the search burden; when a key person is about to leave, taking the informal map with them; when a customer receives the wrong version of a document; or when the business is preparing to introduce AI search or assistant tools. Those tools retrieve whatever they can reach, so a disorganised estate produces disorganised answers. For a related view of how dependence on one person compounds the problem, see [When One Employee Knows How Everything Works](/impact/key-person-operational-dependency/).

## Resolution

Better findability is mostly a design and discipline problem, not a software purchase. Buying another search or collaboration tool without fixing structure and ownership often adds one more place to look.

1. Identify the information employees search for most often.
2. Define authoritative repositories and ownership.
3. Standardise naming, metadata and permissions.
4. Improve search and navigation around real retrieval patterns.
5. Remove duplicate stores where practical.
6. Measure search time again after the change.

Start small. Ask ten people to log, for one week, every time they look for something and how long it takes. The list of most-searched items is usually short, and fixing the top twenty often delivers most of the value. Make sure each authoritative location has a named owner who keeps it current, and link to documents rather than attaching copies. The Insights article [How to Find the Manual Work Nobody Is Measuring](/insights/how-to-find-the-manual-work-nobody-is-measuring/) describes practical ways to surface this kind of hidden effort.

## Bottom line

The objective is not zero search. It is to stop routine operational knowledge from requiring detective work. When the business knows where its authoritative information lives, who owns it and how people actually look for it, paid time shifts from hunting to doing, and that capacity can be measured, redirected and defended in a budget.

**In summary:** scattered files turn salaried time into search, recreation and interruption. Measure it locally, model it transparently, fix ownership and structure first, and re-measure to prove the gain.
