## One customer, seven places

A long-standing client of an office furniture supplier in Johannesburg disputes an invoice for a boardroom fit-out. To respond, the account manager needs the original quote, the signed order, the agreed changes, the delivery note, the installation sign-off and the payment history. The quote is in her sent email. The order is a scanned PDF in a shared folder. The changes were agreed on WhatsApp with a project coordinator who has since left. The delivery note is in the warehouse system, the sign-off is a photo on a technician's phone, and payments are in the accounting package. Assembling the full story takes two days and four colleagues. The business eventually concedes part of the invoice because it cannot prove what was agreed.

The example is illustrative, but the pattern is common. Business information accumulates wherever it was created: in inboxes, spreadsheets, chat threads, personal drives, desktop folders, phones and a growing number of applications. Each location makes sense to the person who used it. Together, they leave the business unable to see or use its own information without a search.

## How information fragments

Fragmentation is rarely the result of a decision. It happens through ordinary habits:

- **Email as a filing system.** Agreements, approvals and customer history live in individual mailboxes rather than in shared records.
- **Spreadsheets as side systems.** Price lists, job trackers and customer lists are kept in workbooks saved locally or in personal cloud folders.
- **Chat for decisions.** Changes and approvals are agreed in messaging apps that are tied to personal phones.
- **Applications bought separately.** Each department adopts its own tool, and none is connected to the others.
- **No agreed home for each type of information.** Nobody has decided where contracts, customer records or project files should live.

The insight on [when a spreadsheet becomes infrastructure](/insights/when-a-spreadsheet-is-a-useful-tool-and-when-it-becomes-infrastructure/) describes one part of this, and the pattern of [business knowledge living in inbox history](/patterns/when-business-knowledge-lives-in-inbox-history/) describes another.

## What fragmentation costs

### Time spent searching and assembling

The most immediate cost is labour. Staff search inboxes, ask colleagues, open several systems and copy information into a new document just to answer routine questions. That effort recurs every time someone needs a complete picture of a customer, job or supplier.

### Disputes lost for lack of evidence

When agreements are scattered, the business may not be able to prove what was quoted, approved or delivered. Disputed invoices, warranty claims and contract disagreements are settled in the customer's favour because the evidence cannot be assembled in time, or at all.

### Errors from working with partial information

People act on the information they can find. A salesperson quotes an old price from a local spreadsheet; a technician arrives without the latest change; a finance clerk chases a debt that was already credited. The pattern of [nobody knowing which spreadsheet is the latest](/patterns/when-nobody-knows-which-spreadsheet-is-the-latest/) is a common example.

### Compliance exposure

Personal information spread across inboxes, personal folders and phones is harder to protect and harder to find. Under section 23 of the Protection of Personal Information Act, a data subject can ask a business to confirm whether it holds their personal information and to provide a record or description of it, including who has had access to it; see the summary of [section 23 on access to personal information](https://popia.co.za/section-23-access-to-personal-information/). A business whose information is scattered may not be able to answer such a request completely or within a reasonable time.

### Missed opportunities

Fragmented information hides patterns. The business cannot easily see which customers buy across product lines, which suppliers cause repeated problems, or which jobs overrun. Decisions are made on whatever data is easiest to gather, not on the full picture.

## A hypothetical view of the cost

The following figures are illustrative assumptions. If 30 staff each spend 30 minutes a day searching for or reassembling information that should be in one place, that is 15 hours a day, or about 3,300 hours a year. At a loaded cost of R200 an hour, the search effort alone costs around R660,000 a year. Add a few disputed invoices conceded for lack of evidence and the total rises further. Measure your own search time before relying on any estimate.

## Why it is tolerated

Each person's system works well enough for them. Moving information into shared locations feels like extra work with no personal benefit. Software decisions are made team by team, and nobody owns the overall information picture. The cost is spread across many small searches, so it never looks like a single problem. The article on [data trapped in one department's application](/impact/data-trapped-in-one-department-application-information-silo-cost/) covers the related problem of departmental silos.

## What a joined-up information approach looks like

The goal is not one giant system. It is a clear, agreed home for each important type of information, with shared access and links between records. Customer records and communication history live in the CRM; contracts and signed documents live in a controlled shared library with consistent naming; jobs live in the job system and link to the customer; approvals happen in a tool that records them, not in chat. Where systems must stay separate, they are connected so that key information flows between them.

Platforms are designed for this. Microsoft's overview of [Dataverse](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-apps/maker/data-platform/data-platform-intro), for example, describes integrating data from multiple sources into a single store with role-based security, so that apps, automation and reporting use the same data. Whatever the platform, the decisions about where each type of information belongs come first.

## Practical next actions

1. **Trace three recent questions.** Pick a dispute, a customer query and a management question, and record every place someone had to look to answer each one.
2. **Define a home for each information type.** Agree where customer records, contracts, job files, price lists and approvals should live, and write it down on one page.
3. **Move the highest-risk information first.** Start with contracts, signed orders and approvals, because these decide disputes and compliance.
4. **Stop new information going to personal locations.** Set shared folders and system records as the default, and switch off local-only saving where practical.
5. **Record decisions where they can be found.** Ask staff to log changes agreed by phone or chat against the relevant customer or job record the same day.
6. **Connect the systems that matter most.** Link CRM, job and accounting systems so that a customer's full history can be seen from one record.

Information that cannot be found is information the business has paid for twice: once to create it and again to search for it. Giving each type of information a home turns scattered records into evidence, insight and faster service.
