## The tolerated pain: paying twice for capability you already own

A reception team pays for an online booking service. Operations pays for a forms tool to collect site inspection checklists. HR pays for a survey platform for staff feedback. A manager pays for a simple workflow tool to route leave requests for approval. Each purchase solved a real problem quickly, and each one costs a monthly fee.

Meanwhile, the business already pays for an office productivity suite that includes tools for booking appointments, building forms and surveys, keeping shared lists and routing approvals. Nobody used them because nobody knew they were there, or because nobody had time to learn them. The business pays twice: once for the suite, and again for specialist tools doing work the suite could have handled.

This is a utilisation problem, not just a duplication problem. Mellorca has covered [paying for several applications that do the same job](/impact/pain-paying-for-multiple-applications-duplicate-software-fragmented-work/), which looks at overlap across separate products, and [buying software for every process problem](/impact/buying-software-for-every-process-problem/), which looks at the habit of solving process issues with purchases. This article focuses on the capability already sitting inside platforms the business pays for, and why it goes unused.

## Why businesses tolerate it

- **Nobody knows what the suite includes.** Most staff use a small part of their productivity platform: email, documents, perhaps video calls. The rest is invisible.
- **Specialist tools market themselves well.** A dedicated booking or forms product appears in search results, offers a free trial and solves the problem in an afternoon.
- **Buying is faster than learning.** A manager with a card can subscribe today. Learning the built-in alternative means waiting for IT or finding time to experiment.
- **Nobody owns the platform.** The suite is treated as a utility, like electricity, not as a set of business tools that someone should be getting value from.
- **Each cost is small.** A few hundred rand a month does not trigger a review.

## What is often already included

Using Microsoft 365 as an example, because it is widely used by South African businesses:

- **Appointment booking.** Microsoft's documentation describes Bookings as a web-based booking page integrated with Outlook, with email and SMS notifications to reduce no-shows. It lists Bookings as available in the Business Basic, Business Standard and Business Premium plans, among others.
- **Forms and surveys.** Microsoft describes Forms as a tool to create surveys, quizzes and polls, collect responses in real time and export results to Excel.
- **Approvals and simple workflows.** Microsoft's Power Automate documentation states that the approvals connector is a standard connector, and that Office 365 licences are among those that grant rights to use standard connectors. Approval flows can notify approvers, who respond from email or Teams.

Other productivity suites include comparable features. The specifics vary by plan, so check your own subscription. The point is that many businesses already own more capability than they use.

Built-in tools are not always the right answer. A specialist product may be justified when it does something the suite cannot, integrates with a core system, or is used heavily enough to repay the extra cost. Our article on [manual appointment scheduling](/impact/manual-appointment-scheduling-administration-cost/) shows why booking tools of some kind are often worth having. The question is whether the business made that choice deliberately or simply did not know about the option it already had.

## Operational symptoms

- Several small software subscriptions for forms, surveys, bookings, sign-off or task lists.
- Data from those tools exported and re-imported into the main suite, by hand.
- Customer or staff information spread across many vendors, each with its own login and security settings.
- Staff unaware of tools already included in their licence.
- Different teams solving the same kind of problem with different products.

## How the pain becomes money

- **Technology waste:** subscription fees for capability already paid for.
- **Integration and admin cost:** time spent moving data between the specialist tool and the main platform, and managing extra accounts.
- **Risk exposure:** business data held in more places, under more vendor terms, with more accounts to secure and remove when people leave.
- **Lost return on the main platform:** the suite is paid for in full but used in part, so its cost per useful feature is higher than it should be.

## An illustrative cost model

The figures below are *illustrative assumptions* for an unnamed South African property-services business with 35 staff. They are not vendor prices.

| Specialist tool | Assumed cost | Annual cost | Handling time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Online booking service | R900 per month | R10,800 | None |
| Forms and checklists tool | R1,100 per month | R13,200 | 2 hours a week exporting to spreadsheets |
| Survey platform | R6,500 per year | R6,500 | None |
| Approval workflow tool | R750 per month | R9,000 | 1 hour a week re-keying approvals |
| **Total** |  | **R39,500** | **3 hours a week** |

Checking the arithmetic: R900 × 12 = R10,800; R1,100 × 12 = R13,200; R750 × 12 = R9,000; R10,800 + R13,200 + R6,500 + R9,000 = R39,500. The handling time of 3 hours a week over 48 weeks is 144 hours; at an assumed R300 per hour that adds R43,200, bringing the illustrative total to R82,700 a year.

Moving to built-in tools would not save all of that, because migration and training take time and some specialist features may be lost. But it shows why a review is worth an afternoon. It also shows that the subscription fees are only half the story: in this example the hours spent moving data between tools cost slightly more than the tools themselves.

## When it becomes commercially urgent

- Card statements show a growing list of small software subscriptions.
- Staff regularly export data from one tool to use it in another.
- A security or data protection review asks where customer information is stored.
- The main platform's renewal is due and the business wants to know what it is getting for the money.

## What better looks like

- **A capability map.** A short list of what the main platform includes, written in business language: "we can take bookings", "we can collect forms", "we can route approvals".
- **A check-first rule.** Before buying a new tool, confirm whether the existing platform can do the job well enough.
- **A platform owner.** Someone responsible for getting value from the suite, not just keeping it running.
- **Short, practical training.** Showing staff what they already have is often cheaper than any new purchase.

Our guide [how to know when you have too many business apps](/insights/how-to-know-when-you-have-too-many-business-apps/) sets out further signals, and a [digital systems audit](/services/digital-systems-audit/) can map what is owned against what is used.

## Practical first steps

1. List every software subscription that is not the main productivity platform or a core business system.
2. For each, write down the job it does in one sentence.
3. Check whether the main platform, on your current plan, can do that job.
4. Where it can, run a short trial with the team that uses the specialist tool.
5. Cancel only after data has been moved and the team is comfortable, timing it with the tool's renewal date.

## Bottom line

Paying for specialist software to do work that an existing platform could already handle is a quiet, recurring waste. Each fee is small, but together they add cost, data sprawl and manual handling. The cheapest software a business can adopt is often the capability it is already paying for and has not yet switched on.
