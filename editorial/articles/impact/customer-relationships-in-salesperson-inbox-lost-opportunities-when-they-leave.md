## The resignation that takes the pipeline with it

A regional sales executive at a Midrand packaging supplier resigns on a Friday and works her notice month. On her last day she hands over a laptop, a phone and a list of "main accounts" typed into an email. Two weeks later her replacement discovers that three quotes were awaiting a decision, one customer had been promised a price review in March, and a long-standing buyer at a Gqeberha manufacturer only ever dealt with her by WhatsApp and email. Nothing in the company's systems records any of it. It all lived in her inbox.

The business did not lose these customers because anyone behaved badly. It lost momentum because the relationship history was held by a person rather than by the business. When she left, the context left with her, and every follow-up has to be reconstructed from scratch or abandoned.

This article looks at the revenue cost of relationships that exist only in an employee's mailbox, and at what it means for customer information to belong to the business. It is a sibling of the wider problem described in [when business knowledge lives in inbox history](/patterns/when-business-knowledge-lives-in-inbox-history/), with a specific focus on sales opportunities.

## What actually walks out of the door

When a salesperson leaves, the visible assets are easy to account for. The invisible ones are not:

- **Open opportunities.** Quotes sent, tenders in preparation, proposals waiting for a signature.
- **Promises made.** A delivery date, a discount if volumes grow, a site visit "after the budget cycle".
- **The shape of the relationship.** Who the real decision-maker is, who only influences, who dislikes email and prefers a call.
- **Timing knowledge.** When the customer's contract ends, when their financial year closes, when they usually reorder.
- **Informal channels.** Messages in personal chat apps and calendar invitations that no colleague can see.

A shared contact list does not carry any of this. Even a CRM entry with a name and a phone number is little help if the real history is in forty email threads. The pattern is close to the one described in [salespeople keeping customer records in personal spreadsheets](/impact/salespeople-personal-spreadsheets-customer-records-cost/), except that here the "database" is a mailbox.

## Whose information is it?

Many small businesses have never answered a simple question: who owns customer information, the employee or the company? In practice, the information a salesperson collects while working for the business is business information. It is also personal information about identifiable people, which brings the Protection of Personal Information Act into the picture. The Act, available on the [South African government's documents page](https://www.gov.za/documents/protection-personal-information-act), places duties on the "responsible party", which in most cases is the company and not the individual employee. The Information Regulator's [POPIA information page](https://inforegulator.org.za/popia/) explains the regulator's role in overseeing that compliance.

That has two practical consequences. First, a business that cannot say where its customer contact data sits, or who has copies of it, will struggle to answer questions about it. Second, a departing employee who retains a personal export or a private mailbox full of customer details is a compliance question as well as a commercial one. This article is not legal advice, and a business with specific concerns should speak to a qualified practitioner. The commercial point stands on its own: if the company cannot see the relationship, it cannot protect the revenue.

## How the loss turns into money

- **Stalled opportunities.** Quotes that were weeks from closing go quiet because the replacement does not know they exist.
- **Delayed ramp-up.** A new salesperson spends the first months reconstructing history, which is paid time with no pipeline attached.
- **Customers who feel forgotten.** A buyer who has to re-explain the account to a stranger may use the moment to ask competitors for a quote.
- **Follow-through failures.** Promised price reviews and renewal conversations are missed, which damages trust built over years.
- **Retention discounting.** To win the account back, management offers concessions that would not have been needed.
- **Recruitment repeat.** The same cycle restarts at the next resignation.

## An illustrative cost model

The numbers below are **illustrative assumptions** for a hypothetical Johannesburg industrial supplies company with six salespeople. They are not benchmarks.

| Assumption | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Salespeople leaving per year | 1 |
| Annual sales managed by that salesperson | R6,000,000 |
| Gross margin | 28% |
| Share of that revenue that stalls or is lost because of missing history | 10% |
| Weeks of reduced productivity for the replacement | 8 |
| Replacement's monthly cost to company | R55,000 |
| Reduction in productivity during that period | 40% |

- Gross margin lost on stalled or lost revenue: R6,000,000 × 10% × 28% = R168,000
- Paid time with reduced output: about R55,000 × 2 months × 40% = R44,000
- **Illustrative total: about R212,000 for a single resignation**

The model leaves out recruitment fees and the longer-term effect of customers who quietly drift away. It also does not count the opportunities that were never logged anywhere, which the business cannot value because it never knew they were there.

## Why the pain is tolerated

Good salespeople often prefer to keep relationships personal, and managers hesitate to insist on record-keeping for fear of annoying the people who bring in revenue. Updating a CRM feels like administration, a theme explored in [why CRM updates happen only before forecast meetings](/discovery/why-crm-updates-happen-only-before-forecast-meetings/). Meanwhile the inbox is already open all day, so email feels like a complete record even though only its owner can search it.

The cost is also deferred. Nothing goes wrong while the person is still there, so the exposure is invisible until a resignation, a long illness or a dismissal makes it real.

## What better looks like

The aim is not to remove personal relationships. It is to make sure the business holds enough of the record to carry them forward.

1. **Decide what the business must always know.** For each account: the key contacts, the open quotes and their values, the next agreed action with a date, and any promises made. Keep it short.
2. **Capture from the inbox, not around it.** Customer email can be linked or copied to the customer record in tools such as [Microsoft Dynamics 365 Sales](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/sales/overview), so the thread is attached to the account rather than the person.
3. **Use one place for quotes and next steps.** If a quote exists only as an attachment, it is not visible to anyone else.
4. **Review open opportunities weekly, as a team.** This makes visibility a habit rather than a hand-over event.
5. **Write a departure routine.** On resignation, list open opportunities, call the top accounts jointly with the successor, and move mailbox rules and shared contacts to the business. The approach in [how to design workflows that can survive staff changes](/insights/how-to-design-workflows-that-can-survive-staff-changes/) applies directly.
6. **State the ownership rule.** Put in the employment terms and the staff handbook that customer records and correspondence made for the business belong to the business, and explain where they should be kept.

## When it becomes urgent

- your best salesperson has handed in notice and nobody can list their open quotes;
- a customer phones to ask where their order is, and the only person who knew is unreachable;
- a competitor hires your account manager and your pipeline suddenly has no visible detail;
- a customer asks what personal information you hold about their staff, and nobody can say where it sits.

## Practical next actions

1. For each salesperson, ask them to list their ten most valuable open opportunities and see how many are visible to anyone else.
2. Check whether the business has access to a leaver's mailbox and how long it is retained.
3. Choose the minimum fields every account record must hold and make them non-negotiable for opportunities above a set value.
4. Pair each key account with a second named person who has met the customer.
5. Add a one-page customer-information rule to the staff handbook, reviewed by whoever advises you on POPIA.

## Bottom line

A relationship that lives in one person's inbox is an asset the company cannot see, protect or hand over. The loss is usually discovered at the worst time, during a notice period or after a resignation, and it is paid for in stalled quotes, slow ramp-up and discounts to win customers back. A short, enforced record of who the customer is, what is open and what happens next turns that personal knowledge into something the business owns.
