## Busier, but not more profitable

Three years ago, a facilities services company in Centurion had 40 staff, 60 contracts and a healthy margin. Today it has 95 staff and 150 contracts. Revenue has more than doubled. Profit has barely moved. When the owner looks at where the new people went, the answer is uncomfortable: a large share of them are not cleaning, maintaining or installing anything. They are scheduling, chasing, checking, reconciling, reporting and answering internal questions. The business grew, and the administration grew faster.

This is one of the most common ways that growth disappoints. The business becomes more complex with each customer, site, product and employee, and the cost of managing that complexity rises faster than the capacity to do paid work.

## How administration outpaces output

Several forces make administration grow faster than revenue:

- **Coordination scales with connections, not people.** Each new team member, site or customer adds new hand-offs, approvals and messages, and the number of connections grows faster than headcount.
- **Manual processes scale linearly at best.** A process that takes ten minutes per order costs twice as much at twice the volume. A process that needs someone to check another system scales worse, because checking takes longer as the data grows.
- **Exceptions accumulate.** Each large customer brings special pricing, reporting or service terms, and each one becomes a manual exception.
- **Compliance thresholds arrive.** Growth triggers new obligations. SARS's [guidance for small businesses](https://www.sars.gov.za/businesses-and-employers/small-businesses-taxpayers/) sets out registrations for VAT, PAYE, UIF and SDL, and notes that compulsory VAT registration applies once turnover passes a threshold. Each new registration adds recurring returns, reconciliations and records.
- **Reporting demand grows.** More managers want more numbers, and in many businesses those numbers are assembled by hand.

Patterns such as [why adding people adds more coordination than capacity](/patterns/why-adding-people-adds-more-coordination-than-capacity/) describe the same dynamic from a different angle.

## Where the margin goes

### Overhead headcount

The most visible cost is people hired to handle administration rather than to deliver. Some of that is necessary. The question is how much of it exists only because processes and systems did not change as the business grew. The existing article on [scaling by adding headcount](/impact/scaling-by-adding-headcount/) looks at this decision directly.

### Productive staff doing admin

A less visible cost is skilled staff spending part of their day on coordination. Technicians fill in job cards twice, project managers compile status reports, and senior people approve routine items. Vendor research suggests this is widespread: Asana's Anatomy of Work research, summarised in its article on [why work about work is bad](https://asana.com/resources/why-work-about-work-is-bad), reports that knowledge workers spend around 60 percent of their time on "work about work", such as searching for information, switching between tools and checking on progress, rather than on skilled work. Figures from a software vendor's own survey should be treated with caution, but the direction matches what most growing businesses observe.

### Slower delivery and lost sales

As coordination load rises, work waits longer between steps. Quotes go out later, jobs start later and invoices are issued later. Customers notice, and some go elsewhere. Cash takes longer to arrive.

### Management attention

Owners and senior managers in a complex business spend more time resolving internal problems and less on customers, pricing and strategy. That opportunity cost rarely appears in the accounts but often matters most. Decisions that should take a day take a month because the people who must make them are absorbed in day-to-day firefighting.

## A simple way to see it in your numbers

The following approach uses illustrative figures. Compare two simple ratios for the last three years: revenue per employee, and the share of payroll spent on roles that do not directly deliver to customers. In the example above, revenue per employee might have fallen from R900,000 to R800,000 while the non-delivery share of payroll rose from 20 to 32 percent. If the non-delivery share had stayed at 20 percent on a payroll of R30 million, the business would be spending R3.6 million a year less on administration. Not all of that is recoverable, but even a third of it is a significant margin improvement.

The useful point is not the exact percentage. It is whether administrative effort is rising faster than output. If it is, growth is diluting margin.

## Why it is tolerated

Growing businesses tolerate rising administration because growth feels like success, and the immediate problem is always the next customer. Hiring an administrator solves this month's pressure. Redesigning a process feels like a distraction. And complexity grows so gradually that there is never a single moment when it obviously needs attention.

## What better looks like

A business that scales well does not have no administration. It has administration that grows more slowly than revenue, because routine coordination is handled by systems, standard processes cover most cases, and exceptions are deliberately limited. Information is captured once and flows to where it is needed, approvals are proportionate to risk, and managers can see status without asking. The guide on [finding the manual work nobody is measuring](/insights/how-to-find-the-manual-work-nobody-is-measuring/) is a practical starting point.

## Practical next actions

1. **Calculate two ratios for three years.** Revenue per employee and the percentage of payroll in non-delivery roles. If the second rises while the first falls, administration is outpacing output.
2. **Map where administrative hours go.** Ask each team to estimate weekly hours on scheduling, chasing, checking, re-entering data and reporting.
3. **Find the five biggest recurring tasks.** Rank them by total hours, and look for the ones caused by disconnected systems or repeated data entry.
4. **Remove exceptions before automating.** Review special arrangements for customers and suppliers, and retire those that no longer earn their cost.
5. **Set a target for administrative growth.** Agree that administrative headcount should grow at no more than half the rate of revenue, and test each new admin hire against it.
6. **Fix one process a quarter.** Redesign and, where sensible, automate one high-volume administrative process every three months, and measure the hours released.

Growth should make a business more efficient, because fixed costs are spread over more revenue. When it does the opposite, the cause is usually operational complexity that was never redesigned, and the cost is paid in margin every month.
