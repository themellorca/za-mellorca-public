## The instruction that never reached payroll

A logistics business in Johannesburg employs about 120 people across a depot and two branches. Payroll is run by one experienced administrator. Changes reach her in every possible way: a WhatsApp from a branch manager approving overtime, an email from the operations director confirming a raise, a forwarded message about a garnishee order, a verbal comment that a driver resigned last Friday. Most months she catches nearly everything. One month she does not. A resigned employee is paid for a full extra month, two drivers miss overtime they worked, and a salary increase agreed in March is only discovered in June, when the employee asks why it has not appeared. Each error takes hours to unpick, and some cannot be fully recovered.

The example is illustrative, but the mechanism is common. Payroll is one of the most rule-bound processes in any business, yet the instructions that feed it often arrive through the least controlled channels.

## Why informal payroll instructions are risky

Each payroll change has several attributes that matter: who authorised it, when it takes effect, which employee it applies to, and whether it is once-off or permanent. An informal message usually captures only some of these. It may be ambiguous about dates, sent by someone without authority, or buried under dozens of other messages. There is no single list of what changed this month, so the payroll administrator relies on searching inboxes and chat threads, and on memory.

Informal channels also break the audit trail. When an employee disputes their pay or an auditor asks who approved an increase, the answer may sit in a personal phone that has since been replaced.

## Where the financial risk comes from

### Overpayments that are hard to recover

Paying a person who has left, continuing an allowance that should have stopped, or applying a raise twice all create overpayments. Recovering them means awkward conversations and, for people who have already left, may not be possible at all.

### Underpayments that damage trust

Missed overtime, late raises and forgotten allowances hurt employees directly. They create disputes, reduce morale and take management time to resolve. Employees are entitled to clear information about their pay: section 33 of the Basic Conditions of Employment Act, as reproduced in [this consolidated text of the Act](https://source.acts.co.za/basic-conditions-of-employment-act-1997/bcoe97_33__information_about_remuneration.php), requires employers to give written information on each pay day, including gross pay, each deduction and its purpose, the net amount paid and, where relevant, hours worked and overtime. A payslip is only as accurate as the instructions behind it.

### Tax and statutory errors

Incorrect pay leads to incorrect PAYE, UIF and SDL. SARS's page on [pay-as-you-earn](https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/pay-as-you-earn/) sets out that employers must deduct the correct tax, declare and pay it monthly, and reconcile it in the EMP501, and that late reconciliation can attract penalties. SARS also describes its [employment taxes validation](https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/pay-as-you-earn/employment-taxes-validation-etv) checks on employee tax certificates, including whether the PAYE, SDL and UIF amounts are correct. Payroll errors that originate in a missed message therefore travel all the way to the business's tax compliance.

### Fraud exposure

When payroll accepts instructions from chat messages and emails, it becomes easier for someone to impersonate a manager, request a bank detail change or add an allowance that nobody approved. Without a formal approval route, the payroll administrator has no reliable way to tell a genuine instruction from a fraudulent one.

### Hidden labour cost

Every payroll run starts with a search for changes. The administrator reads emails, scrolls through chats and phones managers to confirm details. After the run, time goes into corrections and queries. This work is invisible on the cost line but recurs every month.

## A hypothetical view of the cost

The following figures are assumptions for illustration. If a business with 120 employees processes 25 payroll changes a month and two of them are missed or wrong, that is 24 errors a year. If each takes three hours of payroll, HR and management time to investigate and correct, the business spends over 70 hours a year on avoidable rework. Add one unrecovered overpayment of R18,000 and the direct cost of informal instructions quickly exceeds the cost of a simple change form and approval workflow.

## Why it persists

Informal instructions feel fast and personal, and managers are busy. Payroll administrators are often reluctant to push back on senior people. In smaller businesses, there may never have been a formal process at all, because when there were twenty employees, the owner simply told the bookkeeper what changed. ## What a controlled change process looks like

A controlled payroll change process has one entry point, whether a simple form, an HR system or a shared request list. Each change records the employee, the type of change, the effective date, the amount and the approver. Approval rules are explicit: who may approve overtime, raises, allowances, deductions and terminations. Payroll processes only approved changes, and the list of changes for each period is kept with the payroll records. Bank detail changes are verified independently, never through the channel the request arrived on.

## Practical next actions

1. **List every channel that currently feeds payroll.** Include email, chat, phone, paper and verbal instructions, and note who uses each.
2. **Introduce a single change request.** Use a form or system entry that captures employee, change type, amount, effective date and approver, and stop accepting instructions any other way.
3. **Publish an approval matrix.** State who can approve each kind of change, and make sure managers and payroll staff both know it.
4. **Verify bank detail changes separately.** Confirm every banking change with the employee directly through a known contact method.
5. **Reconcile changes before each run.** Compare the approved change list with the payroll output and investigate any differences before payment.
6. **Keep the change log with payroll records.** Store each period's approved changes so that queries, audits and SARS reconciliations can be answered from evidence.

Payroll accuracy depends less on the payroll software than on the quality of the instructions that reach it. Moving changes out of chats and inboxes into one approved route protects employees, protects tax compliance and removes a monthly scramble that nobody budgets for.
