## The question that should never reach a person

A Port Elizabeth online retailer of kitchen appliances receives about 400 emails and calls a week. When the support lead reads a sample of 100, the pattern is plain: 31 ask whether an item is in stock, 24 ask when an order will arrive, 14 ask how to return a faulty item, and 9 ask whether a part is compatible with a model. Only a minority need anything a person must decide. The rest are basic questions whose answers already exist, but cannot be found on the website.

Each of those contacts is a small cost: perhaps six minutes of agent time to read, look up and reply. Multiply by several hundred a week and the business is paying for a team whose main function is to read the website to customers.

This is the pain of weak digital self-service. Customers want to help themselves, the business wants them to, and yet the route between question and answer is blocked by a menu, a search box that returns nothing, or a policy buried in a PDF.

## What customers are trying to find

When online customers contact staff, they are usually after one of a small set of answers:

- Delivery: how long it takes, what it costs and where an order is now.
- Returns and refunds: what is eligible, how to start, how long it takes.
- Pricing and stock: whether something is available and what the total will be.
- Account tasks: resetting a password, changing an address, getting a copy of an invoice.
- Product fit: sizes, compatibility, warranty and installation.
- Contact and trading details: opening hours, branch addresses, who to call about what.

If most of these are answered clearly on the site, the contacts that remain are the ones where a person adds value. If they are not, the contact volume is partly a measure of website gaps. This connects to [support teams answering the same questions daily](/impact/support-teams-answering-same-questions-daily-undocumented-knowledge-labour-cost/), which looks at the cost inside the team; here the focus is the customer-facing side.

## Why self-service fails

### The answer exists but cannot be found

Policies are written for lawyers, placed in the footer, and titled "Terms". Customers search for "returns" and find nothing. Search on the site may only match product names. The simplest test is to ask three people outside the business to find the return process within a minute.

### The answer is incomplete

A delivery page that says "3 to 5 working days" without saying where, from when, or what happens to orders placed on Friday afternoon causes the next email. Customers contact staff because they need one more detail, not because they want to talk.

### The answer is written in the company's vocabulary

Internal terms such as "RMA", "back-order" or "lead time" mean little to customers. Section 22 of the [Consumer Protection Act](https://www.thencc.org.za/consumer-protection-act) deals with the right to information in plain and understandable language, and while the Act is a legal framework rather than a content guide, the underlying principle is a good editorial rule: if the customer cannot understand the sentence, it has not informed them.

### The page is hard to use

Tiny text, poor contrast, forms without labels and navigation that cannot be used on a phone or with a keyboard stop some people from getting an answer at all. The W3C's [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/) explain how to make web content perceivable and usable by more people, including customers with disabilities. Accessibility work is also self-service work: a customer who can complete a task alone does not need to call.

### Nobody owns the content

The website was built once, and nobody has the job of updating it when the delivery supplier changes. Over time, content drifts out of date and customers learn to ignore it, which is related to the problem in [customers who do not know the status of their requests](/impact/customers-do-not-know-request-status-inbound-follow-up-and-lost-trust-cost/).

## What weak self-service costs

### Agent time

At 400 contacts a week and six minutes each, that is 40 hours a week. If, say, 60 percent of those could have been answered by the site, about 24 hours a week of paid time is spent on avoidable work. At an illustrative R200 an hour, that is R4,800 a week, or roughly R250,000 a year. The figures are examples, but they show how a small content problem becomes a payroll line.

### Slower answers for everyone

Easy questions fill the queue and make the difficult ones wait. A customer with a damaged order or a billing dispute is stuck behind forty people asking about opening hours.

### Abandoned sales

A visitor who cannot find delivery costs before checkout may not complete the purchase, and very few of those visitors ever ask. The cost is never seen as a complaint. A slow-loading page compounds the problem, as described in [slow websites and visitors who leave](/impact/slow-website-visitors-leave-before-enquiring-page-speed-revenue-cost/).

### Hidden demand for follow-up

Where tracking or status information is missing, customers write in to ask for it. Businesses that rely on staff to compose progress messages by hand pay twice, which is the subject of [manual customer progress updates](/impact/manual-customer-progress-updates-status-message-labour-cost/).

## Building self-service that people use

Begin with evidence, not a redesign. Export a month of contacts, tag each by question type and count them. The top ten questions will usually account for more than half the volume. Write a clear answer to each, and place it where the customer is when the question arises: the delivery estimate beside the price, the returns steps in the order confirmation email, the compatibility table on the product page.

Then check that search works. Google's guidance on [creating helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) describes writing for the reader's need rather than for the site owner's convenience. The same thinking applies to a help section: titles should match the words customers use, and each page should answer one question completely.

Measure results by contact volume per question type, not only by page views. If "where is my order" emails fall after you add tracking links to confirmation messages, the change has paid for itself. If they do not, the answer is still in the wrong place.

## Practical next actions

1. **Tag a month of customer contacts by question type.** Use a simple list and count the results to find the top ten questions.
2. **Write a plain-language answer for each of the top ten.** Keep each to one page or one short section, with the exact figures: cost, time, steps and cut-off times.
3. **Put answers where the question arises.** Add delivery details near the price, returns instructions in confirmation emails and order status links in every message.
4. **Test the site with three people who do not work for you.** Ask them to find the returns process, the delivery cost and your phone number on a mobile phone, and note where they fail.
5. **Check basic accessibility.** Review labels, contrast, text size and keyboard use against the WCAG guidance, starting with the checkout and contact forms.
6. **Name an owner for the help content.** Give one person the task of updating it whenever a policy, courier or price changes, and review it quarterly.
7. **Re-count contacts after six weeks.** Compare volumes by question type and keep working on whichever categories have not fallen.

Self-service does not mean hiding behind a website. It means giving customers the simple answers directly, so that staff time is available for the cases where a person makes the difference.
