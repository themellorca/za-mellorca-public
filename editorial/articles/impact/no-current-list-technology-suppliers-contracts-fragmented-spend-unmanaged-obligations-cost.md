## Nobody can answer a simple question

The new financial director of a manufacturing business in Pietermaritzburg asks a reasonable question in her first month: who are our technology suppliers, and what have we committed to? It takes three weeks to answer. The IT manager knows about the network support contract and the server hosting. Finance can see recurring debit orders but not what they are for. Sales pays for its CRM on a company card. The factory has a maintenance system bought by a former production manager. The printing contract auto-renewed last year for another five years. Two cloud storage subscriptions overlap. Nobody can find the signed agreement for the business's internet line, which carries a 90-day cancellation notice.

The example is illustrative, but the situation is typical. Technology purchasing in growing businesses is spread across departments, card payments and debit orders, and there is rarely a single, current register of who supplies what, on which terms, and who in the business is responsible for each relationship.

## Why the list does not exist

Technology used to mean a few large purchases managed by one person. It now includes dozens of subscriptions, cloud services, connectivity contracts, device leases, support agreements and add-ons, many of which can be bought by anyone with a card. Contracts are signed by whoever needed the service, filed in personal inboxes, and forgotten once the service works. When people leave, their knowledge of those arrangements leaves with them.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre makes the same observation about supply chains in its [supply chain security guidance](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/supply-chain-security), noting that many organisations lose sight of their supply chains, and that weaknesses can be introduced at any point in them. Its [supply chain security principles](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/supply-chain-security/principles-supply-chain-security) begin with understanding the risks and then establishing control and oversight, which is difficult to do without knowing who the suppliers are.

## Where the money and risk go

### Fragmented and duplicated spend

Without a register, departments buy overlapping tools, pay for licences nobody uses, and miss volume discounts that a consolidated relationship would earn. The existing article on [unused software licences](/impact/unused-software-licences-financial-waste/) shows how quickly that waste adds up.

### Missed notice periods and unwanted renewals

Many technology contracts renew automatically and require written notice weeks or months before the end of the term. If nobody knows the dates, the business is locked into another term on old pricing, often with an annual escalation. The piece on [supplier contracts renewing without review](/impact/supplier-contracts-renewing-without-review/) describes this cost in detail.

### Unmanaged legal obligations

Contracts carry obligations in both directions: minimum terms, service levels, data handling commitments and exit conditions. South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act requires a responsible party to ensure, through a written contract, that any operator processing personal information on its behalf maintains the required security safeguards; see [section 21](https://popia.co.za/section-21-security-measures-regarding-information-processed-by-operator/). A business that cannot list which suppliers hold its customer and employee data cannot check whether those contracts exist or say what they require.

### Security and continuity exposure

Every technology supplier is a potential point of failure or entry. If a supplier is breached, goes out of business or changes its terms, the business needs to know immediately what depends on it. Without a register, the first sign of a dependency is often the outage.

### Weak negotiating position

Suppliers know their own contracts well. A business that does not know its own terms, usage or alternatives negotiates renewals at a disadvantage.

### Slow answers when it matters

Auditors, insurers, large customers and potential investors increasingly ask which third parties hold the business's data and run its critical systems. Cyber insurance questionnaires and customer due-diligence forms often ask for exactly this list. Without a register, each request becomes a scramble across departments, answers are inconsistent, and the business may misstate its position on a form it has signed.

## A hypothetical view of the cost

The following figures are illustrative. Suppose a business of 80 people spends R1.8 million a year on technology across 40 suppliers. If 8 percent of that spend is duplicated or unused, the waste is R144,000 a year. If one auto-renewed contract for R6,000 a month runs two years longer than intended because the notice date was missed, that adds R144,000 more. The cost of maintaining a register is small by comparison.

## Why it is tolerated

A supplier register sounds like administration with no immediate payback. Ownership is unclear: IT sees it as finance's job and finance sees it as IT's. Many purchases are individually small, so none attracts attention. And the cost of not knowing only becomes visible at renewal, in an audit, or in a crisis. The discovery note on [why IT asset registers miss cloud and SaaS services](/discovery/why-it-asset-registers-miss-cloud-and-saas-services/) explains why existing asset lists often do not help.

## What a useful register contains

A technology supplier register does not need special software. A well-maintained shared spreadsheet or list is enough to start. For each supplier, record the service provided, the business owner responsible for the relationship, the systems or processes that depend on it, monthly and annual cost, payment method, contract start and end dates, notice period, renewal terms, whether the supplier processes personal information, where the signed contract is stored, and the support contact. The guide on [building a business application inventory that is actually useful](/insights/how-to-build-a-business-application-inventory-that-is-actually-useful/) describes a complementary list of the systems themselves.

## Practical next actions

1. **Extract twelve months of technology payments.** Pull debit orders, supplier invoices and company card transactions, and tag everything related to software, connectivity, hosting, devices and support.
2. **Build the first register from payments.** Create one row per supplier and fill in the service, cost and payment method you already know.
3. **Assign a business owner to each supplier.** Give each relationship a named person who is responsible for its value, terms and renewal decision.
4. **Find and file every contract.** Locate signed agreements, record end dates and notice periods, and store the documents in one shared, controlled location.
5. **Flag suppliers that hold personal information.** Check that each has a written agreement covering security safeguards as POPIA requires.
6. **Set renewal reminders.** Put notice dates in a shared calendar at least 60 days before each deadline, and review the register quarterly.

A business cannot manage what it cannot list. One current register of technology suppliers and contracts turns scattered spend into decisions, keeps notice dates from passing unnoticed, and shows clearly where the business depends on others.
