The managing director of a Port Elizabeth (Gqeberha) security services company reads the same report every month: revenue, contracts lost, overtime cost and customer complaints. Every number in it describes something that has already happened. When a large client cancelled in September, the report showed it in October. Looking back, the warning signs had been there since July: guard no-shows at that site had doubled, the site supervisor had changed twice and the client's facilities manager had stopped replying to monthly review invitations. All of that was known to someone. None of it was in the report.

That is the difference between lagging and leading indicators. Lagging indicators tell you the result. Leading indicators tell you whether the conditions that produce the result are holding. Most small and mid-sized businesses report almost entirely on the first kind, and so they find out about problems when it is too late to change the outcome.

## Lagging and leading, defined usefully

One of the clearest treatments of this distinction comes from an unexpected place: the UK Health and Safety Executive's guide to [developing process safety indicators (HSG254)](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg254.pdf). It describes leading indicators as active monitoring, a routine, systematic check that key actions or activities are being carried out as intended. Lagging indicators are reactive monitoring: they show when a desired outcome has failed or has not been achieved. The guide's central idea is "dual assurance", setting a leading and a lagging indicator for each critical control so that one confirms the control is operating and the other reveals when it has failed anyway.

The guide was written for major hazard industries, but the logic fits any business process. Revenue, churn, complaints and margin are lagging. The activities and conditions that drive them can be measured as leading indicators.

## Start from the outcome and work backwards

A leading indicator is only useful if it genuinely predicts the outcome you care about. The safest way to find one is to start with a lagging measure and ask what has to go right, repeatedly, for that result to be good.

For the security company:

- **Lagging:** contracts lost per quarter.
- **What has to go right:** guards turn up, supervisors stay, incidents are reported promptly, and the client relationship is maintained.
- **Leading candidates:** shift fill rate per site, supervisor tenure per site, average hours from incident to client report, and share of scheduled client reviews that actually took place.

The UK government's guidance on [setting performance metrics](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/measuring-success/how-to-set-performance-metrics-for-your-service) recommends a similar discipline: state what you expect to change, the benefit it should produce and the assumption behind it, then choose measures that test that assumption. A leading indicator is, in effect, a hypothesis about cause and effect, and should be treated as one.

## Properties of a good leading indicator

- **It moves before the outcome.** If it only changes after the result is in, it is a lagging measure with a different name.
- **Someone can act on it.** A rising no-show rate at a site prompts a call to the supervisor. A falling industry index does not prompt anything specific.
- **It comes from systems you already run.** Rosters, CRM activity logs, ticket systems and job cards hold most of the data. Indicators that need manual collection tend to die.
- **It is measured at the right level.** A company-wide fill rate of 97 percent can hide one site at 80 percent. Leading indicators are often most useful per site, team or customer.
- **It has a threshold.** Agree in advance what level triggers action, so the conversation is about response rather than interpretation.

## Common leading indicators by area

Every business is different, but these examples often prove useful:

- **Sales:** new qualified opportunities created per week, time from enquiry to first response, and quotes issued against target.
- **Customer retention:** missed service visits, unresolved tickets older than an agreed age, and reduced contact from the client's side.
- **Cash flow:** invoices not issued within two days of job completion, and the share of debtors who have not been contacted by day thirty.
- **Delivery:** jobs started late, work in progress older than normal, and rework rate.
- **People:** overtime per person, unfilled roles and training completion.

Our article on [what to measure at each stage of a business workflow](/insights/what-to-measure-at-each-stage-of-a-business-workflow/) shows how to place these within a process.

## Watch out for gaming

Once a leading indicator is reported, people will try to improve it, and not always in the intended way. If "client reviews held" becomes a target, a five-minute phone call may start counting as a review. Atlassian's team playbook on [goals, signals and measures](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/goals-signals-measures) separates signals, which show whether you are on the right path, from measures, which are specific targets. Keeping leading indicators as signals that prompt a conversation, rather than as targets tied to pay, reduces the temptation to game them. Pair each one with its lagging outcome so improvements that do not change results are visible.

## Fit them into the existing report

Do not build a separate leading-indicator dashboard that nobody opens. Add a short section to the report management already reads, placing each leading indicator beside the lagging result it is meant to predict. Review them in the same meeting. Our guide to [deciding what should be on an operations dashboard](/insights/how-to-decide-what-should-be-on-an-operations-dashboard/) covers layout and selection.

After six months, check whether each leading indicator actually moved before its outcome. Keep the ones that did. Replace the ones that did not. This review is where the real learning happens, because it tells you what actually drives results in your business rather than what you assumed. Our article on [why more dashboards do not automatically create better decisions](/insights/why-more-dashboards-do-not-automatically-create-better-decisions/) is a useful reminder to keep the set small.

## Practical next actions

1. Pick the three lagging measures management cares about most, such as revenue, customer losses and debtor days.
2. For each one, list what has to go right repeatedly for the result to be good, and choose one or two leading indicators that measure those conditions from data you already hold.
3. Set a threshold for each leading indicator that triggers a named person to act, and measure it per site, team or customer where possible.
4. Add the leading indicators to the existing management report beside the outcomes they are meant to predict.
5. After six months, keep the indicators that moved before their outcomes and replace the ones that did not.
