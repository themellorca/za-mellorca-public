A Durban medical supplies distributor knew its order-to-delivery process was too slow. Orders arrived by email, phone and a web form; a sales administrator re-typed them into the accounting system; the warehouse printed picking slips in batches twice a day; and credit checks happened after picking, so some orders were packed and then held. Everyone agreed the process needed redesigning. Nobody could agree how to do it without disrupting the hospitals and clinics that relied on next-day delivery.

This is the usual tension. The processes most in need of redesign are often the ones the business cannot afford to pause. The answer is not to wait for a quiet period that never comes, nor to switch everything over on a single Monday. It is to redesign in small, contained steps, testing each one in real conditions before it spreads.

## Map the current process first

You cannot safely change what you cannot see. Before designing anything new, map how the process actually runs today, including its variations and workarounds, with the people who do the work. Note where time is lost, where errors arise and which steps protect against real risks. Our article on [why every improvement project needs a current-state map](/insights/why-every-systems-improvement-project-needs-a-current-state-map/) explains why this step is so often skipped and why that is costly. For a lightweight method, see [how to map a business workflow without making it complicated](/insights/how-to-map-a-business-workflow-without-making-it-complicated/).

For the distributor, the map showed that most of the delay came from two places: re-typing orders, and the late credit check that sent packed orders back to the shelf.

## Break the redesign into separate changes

A redesign is rarely one change. It is usually a set of smaller ones that can be introduced independently. The distributor's redesign contained at least four:

- Move the credit check to the moment the order is captured.
- Replace the email and phone channels with a structured order form for regular customers.
- Send picking slips to the warehouse continuously instead of in two batches.
- Give customers an automatic confirmation with expected delivery date.

Separating them allows you to sequence them, test each one on its own, and stop any that do not work without abandoning the rest.

## Test each change small and fast

Healthcare improvement teams have refined a method for changing live processes where mistakes matter. The Institute for Healthcare Improvement's [Model for Improvement](https://www.ihi.org/resources/how-to-improve) asks three questions: what are we trying to accomplish, how will we know a change is an improvement, and what change can we make that will result in improvement. It then tests each change through Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles, described as testing a change on a small scale, in the real setting, by planning it, trying it, observing the results and acting on what is learned.

The US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's [PDSA guidance](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool2b.html) adds practical limits: each cycle should cover a single step rather than the whole change, be as short as possible, sometimes as little as an hour, and involve only a small part of the organisation before being broadened.

For the distributor, the first test was modest: for one day, one sales administrator ran the credit check before entering orders from five regular customers. The question was whether it slowed order entry and whether it caught any holds earlier. It did both, slightly slower entry and two holds caught before picking, which was enough to justify a wider test.

## Contain the pilot

After a few small tests, run the changed process for a defined part of the business for a few weeks. Choose a slice that is representative but contained: one customer group, one branch, one product line or one shift. Make sure the pilot has:

- A clear boundary, so everyone knows which work follows the new process.
- A named owner who watches it daily.
- Two or three measures agreed in advance, such as order-to-dispatch time, holds after picking and errors.
- A simple way back to the old process for any case that goes wrong.

The old process continues for everything outside the boundary, so customers outside the pilot notice nothing.

## Protect the customer-facing edge

Changes inside the business can be tested with less risk than changes customers see. Where possible, sequence the redesign so that internal changes come first and customer-facing ones later, once the internal process is stable. The distributor moved the credit check and continuous picking before introducing the new order form, so that by the time customers used the form, the process behind it already worked.

When a customer-facing change does come, offer it to a willing group first and keep the old channel open for a period. Forced switches create avoidable friction.

## Plan the system changes around the process, not the reverse

Many redesigns need system changes: a new form, a changed status, an automation. Make those changes reversible and limited to the pilot where possible, for example a new order type used only by pilot customers. Avoid rebuilding the core configuration before the process has been proven. Our article on [implementing a new system without disrupting operations](/insights/how-to-implement-a-new-business-system-without-disrupting-operations/) covers timing and cutover when a larger system change is required.

## Spread in waves, and keep measuring

Once a pilot meets its measures, extend it in waves: the next customer group, the next branch, the next shift. Each wave is a chance to catch problems that did not appear in the pilot, such as a branch with different stock practices. Keep the same measures running throughout and compare each wave with the period before. If a wave performs worse, pause and investigate before going further.

## Retire the old process deliberately

Redesigns stall when the old way is never switched off. Staff fall back on it when busy, and the business runs two processes indefinitely. Set a date for retiring the old process once all waves are complete, remove its forms and shortcuts, update procedures, and tell customers and suppliers what has changed. If everything feels broken and you are unsure where to start, [what to fix first when everything feels broken](/insights/what-to-fix-first-when-everything-feels-broken/) helps choose the first process to redesign.

## Practical next actions

1. Map the current process with the people who run it, and mark where time and errors arise.
2. Break the redesign into separate changes that can be tested and introduced independently.
3. Run a small, short test of the first change with a handful of cases, and record what you learn.
4. Pilot the changed process in one contained slice of the business with an owner, agreed measures and a way back.
5. Extend in waves, internal changes before customer-facing ones, and set a date to retire the old process.
