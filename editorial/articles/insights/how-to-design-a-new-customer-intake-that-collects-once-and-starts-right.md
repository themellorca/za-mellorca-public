A new client signs with a Pretoria accounting practice on a Tuesday. By Thursday, they have been asked for their company details three times: once by the partner, once by the administrator, and once in an onboarding email. Their tax number is in the practice's system with a typo, nobody has noted that they need monthly rather than quarterly service, and the engagement letter went to a previous contact who left the company. The client has not yet received any service and already has a view of how the practice operates.

Intake is the stretch between "yes" and "ready to serve". It is easy to treat it as paperwork, and it is where many customer relationships quietly acquire their first problems. This article sets out how to design it so information is collected once, checked early, and passed on in a form the next team can use.

## Define what "ready to serve" means

Intake is finished when the business can deliver. That sounds obvious, but many businesses never write down the conditions. Begin by listing, for each product or service, exactly what must be true before work starts. Typical items include a confirmed legal entity and billing details, an authorised contact with a working phone number and email address, a signed agreement, a credit decision where relevant, delivery or site details, and any service-specific information such as meter numbers, system access or product configuration.

This list is your definition of done. It tells the customer what you need, tells staff what to check, and gives you a single point at which handover from sales to operations can be confirmed. Without it, each person applies their own idea of "complete" and the gaps appear later, usually in the form of a delayed first invoice or a missed first appointment. The same pattern is examined from the customer's side in [where customer information gets lost during onboarding](/insights/where-customer-information-gets-lost-during-onboarding/).

## Ask for each fact once, and ask the right person

Customers notice repetition more than most businesses realise, and it is a signal of disconnected systems. The causes and consequences are covered in [why customers are asked to repeat their details](/insights/why-customers-are-asked-to-repeat-their-details/). The fix is structural. Choose one place where intake information is captured, make that place the source for every later step, and let other tools read from it rather than ask again.

Questions should also go to the person who can answer them. Government guidance on [structuring forms](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/form-structure) recommends making a list of what you need and why before writing the form, starting with questions that tell people early if they are not eligible, and branching so people only answer what is relevant to them. For intake, that means asking a sole trader different questions to a company, and asking a finance contact for billing details rather than the person who requested the service. It also means keeping each screen or section to one topic, so people can find their way and recover from mistakes.

## Separate what is needed now from what can follow

A long front-loaded form lengthens the time to first value and increases abandonment. Divide intake into what is needed to begin and what can be gathered during the early weeks. A practice might need entity details, authorisation and engagement terms on day one, with the previous year's records requested in the first fortnight, and preferences for reporting formats at the first review.

Anything personal that you collect should also meet the limits in the [Protection of Personal Information Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/protection-personal-information-act), which requires that personal information be adequate, relevant and not excessive for the purpose. In intake, that means resisting the temptation to collect identity documents, home addresses or dates of birth unless the service genuinely requires them, and recording why you collect what you do.

## Check the data while the customer is still engaged

Errors cost least to fix at the moment they are made. Build a few simple checks into intake itself:

- **Validate format as it is entered**, for example the length of a tax number, the structure of an email address or the digits in a mobile number.
- **Read key details back to the customer**, such as legal name, billing contact and first service date, either on a confirmation screen or in a short confirmation message.
- **Check for an existing record** before creating a new one. Duplicates created at intake follow the customer around, a problem described in [how duplicate customer records are created](/insights/how-duplicate-customer-records-are-created/).
- **Verify the authority of the signatory** where an agreement is involved, so that the contract is not signed by someone who cannot bind the company.

## Make the handover a defined event

The point where sales hands over to delivery or finance is where intake most often breaks. Replace the informal email with a defined handover: a standard summary generated from the intake record, covering who the customer is, what was agreed, the dates that matter, and anything unusual that was promised. The person receiving it confirms that it is complete, or returns it with specific gaps. Until they accept it, the customer is not yet "live".

If your intake currently runs on a chain of emails and a checklist in someone's head, the progression from that state to something sturdier is described in [when client onboarding starts with a chain of emails](/patterns/when-client-onboarding-starts-with-a-chain-of-emails/). The wider design of what comes next is covered in [how to design a customer onboarding workflow that scales](/insights/how-to-design-a-customer-onboarding-workflow-that-scales/).

## Tell the customer what happens next

Customers are most anxious right after committing. A short, clear message after intake that says what has been received, what is still outstanding, who their contact is, and when they will hear from you next removes most of the follow-up calls. The Service Standard from the UK government makes a related point: it asks teams to help people succeed first time with minimum help, and to test all the parts of a service people interact with, including offline ones such as letters, which you can read at [make the service simple to use](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/service-standard/point-4-make-the-service-simple-to-use). Treat your welcome message and engagement letter as part of the product.

## Measure intake, then improve it

Track three measures: the time from signed agreement to first delivery, the number of customer contacts needed to complete intake, and the proportion of new customers who need corrections in their first month. Review them quarterly with the people who run intake. When a correction occurs, trace it to the question or step that allowed it and change that step.

## Practical next actions

1. Write a "ready to serve" checklist for your main service, listing what must be true before work starts.
2. Choose one place where intake data is captured, and stop requesting the same details in email or a second form.
3. Split your intake form into what is needed on day one and what can follow in the first fortnight.
4. Add format checks, a duplicate check and a read-back of key details before the record is saved.
5. Replace informal handover emails with a standard summary that the receiving team must accept before the customer goes live.
