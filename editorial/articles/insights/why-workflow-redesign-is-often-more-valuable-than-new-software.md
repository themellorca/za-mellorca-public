A Pietermaritzburg engineering contractor was losing money on variations, the changes clients request after a job is quoted. Variations were agreed on site, noted in site diaries, sometimes emailed, and often never invoiced. The directors bought a job-costing system with a variations module. A year later, unbilled variations were almost as high as before. The software worked. The workflow around it had not changed: site supervisors still agreed variations verbally, still did not capture them on the day, and still had no reason to.

When the company redesigned the workflow, so that no variation could be started without a client-signed instruction captured on a phone, priced from a standard rate list and sent automatically to the quantity surveyor, unbilled variations fell sharply within a quarter. The same software that had disappointed now did its job, because the workflow gave it something to work with.

## An old lesson that keeps being relearned

In 1990 Michael Hammer's Harvard Business Review article, [Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate](https://hbr.org/1990/07/reengineering-work-dont-automate-obliterate), argued that companies were using technology to speed up existing processes rather than rethinking them, and pointed to slow product development, error-prone order fulfilment and unanswered customer enquiries as the result. The language has aged; the point has not. Applying new tools to an old workflow tends to make the same work happen slightly faster, including the delays and errors built into it.

More recent economic research supports the same idea from a different angle. In their paper on the [productivity J-curve](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148), Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock and Chad Syverson argue that general-purpose technologies require significant complementary investments, including the co-invention of new processes, business models and skills, and that productivity gains depend on those investments. Technology alone is not the source of the improvement; the changed way of working is.

## Where the value in a workflow actually lies

Most operational problems in growing businesses come from a handful of workflow causes:

- **Information captured late or in the wrong form,** like the verbal site variations.
- **Unclear ownership at handoffs,** so work waits between people.
- **Steps that exist for historical reasons,** such as approvals that never reject anything.
- **Work done in batches** that could flow continuously.
- **Exceptions handled informally,** so every unusual case becomes a negotiation.

None of these is fixed by a new screen. Each is fixed by changing who does what, when, with what information and under what rule. Our article on [why automating a broken process makes the problem worse](/insights/why-automating-a-broken-process-makes-the-problem-worse/) shows what happens when software is laid over these causes untouched.

## Redesign often needs less technology than expected

When businesses redesign first, they frequently find that the software they already own can support the new workflow with configuration. The UK Government Digital Service's guidance on [defining a purchasing strategy](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/define-your-purchasing-strategy) advises using off-the-shelf products as they were intended, warns that even small modifications can remove most of their benefits, and suggests configuring settings rather than customising. A clear workflow design is what makes that possible, because you know exactly what the software needs to do and can see whether existing features already do it. Our article on [why software configuration should follow workflow design](/insights/why-software-configuration-should-follow-workflow-design/) covers this in more depth.

## When new software is the right answer

Redesign does not rule out new software. Sometimes the redesigned workflow needs a capability nothing in the business provides, such as mobile capture for field staff, a customer portal or a proper database for records currently in spreadsheets. The difference is that the purchase is now specified by the workflow rather than by a vendor's demo. You know which features matter, which do not, and how you will measure success. Our checklist on [what to ask before you buy another tool](/insights/before-you-buy-another-tool-ask-these-questions/) is useful at this point.

## Why businesses reach for software first

If redesign is often more valuable, why do businesses buy software first? Three reasons recur. Software is tangible and can be approved as a purchase, while redesign is diffuse work that takes management attention. Vendors actively sell software, and nobody sells "change how your site supervisors record variations". And software promises change without conflict, whereas redesign often means changing roles, rules and habits that some people prefer.

Recognising these pressures helps resist them. Spending on technology that does not change the underlying work is one of the main reasons, as our article on [why technology spend does not automatically create business value](/insights/why-technology-spend-does-not-automatically-create-business-value/) explains, that investment fails to show up in results.

## How to sequence redesign and software

A practical sequence for a smaller business:

- **Define the outcome.** What should be better, for whom, measured how?
- **Map the current workflow** and find where time, errors and cost arise.
- **Redesign on paper:** remove steps, move information capture earlier, clarify ownership, define exception rules.
- **Test the redesign** with existing tools, even if clumsy, to prove it works.
- **Only then specify technology,** starting with what you already own.

The contractor's redesign was first tested with a simple shared form and a WhatsApp photo of the signed instruction. Only once the workflow was proven did they configure the job-costing system to match it.

## How to tell whether redesign has paid off

Judge a redesign by the outcome you defined at the start, not by activity. For the contractor, that meant the share of variations invoiced within the same month, the value of unbilled variations at month-end and the number of client disputes about variations. Measure these for a few months before the change and for at least two quarters after it. If the numbers move, the redesign has earned its place, and any later software purchase can be judged against the same measures. If they do not, look again at the workflow before blaming the tools. Either way, the business learns more than it would from a new system installed on top of the old habits.

## Practical next actions

1. For your next proposed software purchase, write down the specific workflow problem it is meant to solve and how you will measure success.
2. Map the current workflow and identify whether the problem lies in late information, unclear ownership, unnecessary steps, batching or informal exceptions.
3. Redesign the workflow on paper and test it for a few weeks with the tools you already have.
4. Check whether existing software can support the redesigned workflow through configuration before buying anything new.
5. If new software is still needed, specify it from the redesigned workflow rather than from vendor demonstrations.
