Every Monday the operations manager of a Durban payroll bureau opens a spreadsheet of new clients and starts sending messages. Has the employee data file come in from the hardware wholesaler? Did anyone set up the tax reference numbers for the logistics firm? Is the first payroll for the dental group ready for parallel running? By Wednesday she has most of her answers, some of which are already out of date. She is good at this. She is also spending a day and a half a week acting as a human status report.

The problem is not her team's diligence. It is that onboarding progress lives in people's heads and inboxes rather than in a record anyone can read. Fixing that does not require expensive software. It requires deciding what "progress" means, recording it at the moment work happens, and making the exceptions visible without anyone having to ask.

## Why task lists do not show status

Most firms try to solve this with a checklist per client. The checklist helps the person doing the work, but it does not tell a manager how things are going. Basecamp's chapter on [showing progress](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/3.4-chapter-13) makes the point well: asking for status feels like nagging, and counting completed tasks misleads, because lists grow as people discover work they had not anticipated. A list with eight of ten items ticked can be nearly done, or it can be waiting on the one item that will take three weeks.

For onboarding, the most common hidden item is something only the client can supply: signed engagement letters, identity documents, bank details, historical data. A checklist marks these as "not done" in exactly the same way as work the team simply has not started. A manager cannot tell the difference without asking.

## Define stages that mean something

Replace the long checklist with a short set of stages that describe where the client actually is. The [Kanban Guide](https://kanbanguides.org/english/) describes this as defining the states that work items flow through from a clear start point to a clear finish point, with explicit policies about how items move between them. For the payroll bureau, the stages might be:

- **Signed:** engagement letter and mandate received.
- **Information requested:** data request sent to the client, with a due date.
- **Information complete:** all required files received and checked.
- **Configured:** company, employees and pay elements set up.
- **Parallel run:** first payroll run alongside the client's existing process.
- **Live:** first payroll paid from the new system and signed off.

Each stage needs an entry rule. "Information complete" should mean that a named person has checked the files against a list, not that someone thinks they arrived. Written entry rules stop clients sitting in a stage they have not really reached, which is one of the main reasons managers stop trusting a tracker and go back to asking.

## Separate waiting from working

The single most useful addition to an onboarding tracker is a clear distinction between "waiting on us" and "waiting on the client". It changes the conversation from "why is this slow?" to "who needs to act?". Record the reason for any wait in a short list of options, such as awaiting documents, awaiting client decision, awaiting third party or internal capacity, plus the date the wait started.

That one field lets you answer questions that otherwise need a round of messages. How many clients are stuck on documents? Which of them have been waiting more than ten days? Which waits are ours to fix? Our article on [where customer information gets lost during onboarding](/insights/where-customer-information-gets-lost-during-onboarding/) explains why the document stage is so often where time disappears.

## Agree how long each stage should take

Visibility only helps if people know what normal looks like. The Kanban Guide recommends a service level expectation: a forecast of how long an item should take from start to finish, expressed with a probability, such as 85 percent of items finishing in eight days or less, and based on past cycle times. Applied to onboarding, the bureau might find that 85 percent of clients go from signed to live within 30 days, and that the information stage accounts for most of the variation.

Once that expectation exists, an item that has been in a stage for longer than usual is a signal, not a mystery. The tracker can flag it automatically. Nobody needs to ask whether the hardware wholesaler is late, because the record shows it has been waiting on employee data for twelve days against a normal five.

## Limit how many onboardings run at once

When a business is keen to grow, every new signature gets started immediately. Ten clients in configuration at once means each one moves slowly and each one generates follow-up questions. The Kanban Guide treats controlling work in progress as a core practice for exactly this reason: fewer items in flight finish faster and are easier to see. A simple rule, such as no more than four clients in configuration per consultant, often shortens the whole onboarding period while making the tracker easier to read.

## Record updates where the work happens

A tracker that depends on people remembering to update it will drift. Wherever possible, let the work itself move the record. When the engagement letter is signed in your e-signature tool, the stage changes to Signed. When the data request email is sent from the CRM, the due date is set. When the client uploads files through a portal, the record notes that documents have arrived and assigns the check. Our guide on [what should trigger after a customer says yes](/insights/what-should-trigger-after-a-customer-says-yes/) covers which of these events are worth automating first.

Where an update must be manual, make it a single click or a single field at the point the person finishes the step. If updating the tracker takes longer than sending a WhatsApp message, people will send the message instead.

## Measure completion, not just activity

Tracking progress client by client is half the job. The other half is seeing how onboarding performs overall. The UK government's guidance on [measuring completion rate](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/measuring-success/measuring-completion-rate) recommends counting how many started transactions reach a defined end, and treating those that are abandoned or fail as part of the picture. For onboarding, that means tracking how many signed clients go live, how many stall, and how many cancel before their first billable month. A business with a fast average onboarding time and a quietly rising cancellation rate has not improved.

## Give clients a view too

Chasing runs in both directions. Clients ask the account manager where things stand, and the account manager asks the operations team. A short, automatic weekly summary to each new client, generated from the same stages, removes much of that traffic. It should say what has been done, what is outstanding, and what the client needs to supply, with dates. It also makes the "waiting on the client" items visible to the person who can actually resolve them.

## Use the tracker in a short review, not a long meeting

With stages, wait reasons and time expectations in place, the weekly onboarding meeting can shrink to fifteen minutes spent only on items that are over their expected time or blocked. Everything else is visible on the board. The broader principle, explained in our article on [why chasing people for updates is a systems failure](/insights/why-chasing-people-for-updates-is-a-systems-failure/), is that a well-designed record answers routine questions so that people only talk about the exceptions. For the full onboarding workflow behind the tracker, see [how to design a customer onboarding workflow that scales](/insights/how-to-design-a-customer-onboarding-workflow-that-scales/).

## Practical next actions

1. Replace the long onboarding checklist with five to seven stages, each with a written entry rule.
2. Add a wait reason and wait start date to every onboarding record, separating client-side waits from internal ones.
3. Use the last six to twelve months of onboardings to work out how long each stage normally takes, and flag items that exceed it.
4. Set a limit on how many clients each person configures at once.
5. Connect at least one stage change to the event that causes it, such as a signed document or a completed upload.
6. Send each new client an automatic weekly progress summary drawn from the same record.
