She wakes up with a fever on the Thursday before month-end. She is the only person who knows how to run payroll, and salaries are due on Monday. So she logs in from bed, wrapped in a blanket, and works through the afternoon. She is not being heroic. She simply knows that if she does not do it, nobody will, and forty people will not be paid.

This piece looks at the person who keeps everything running and therefore cannot get sick, and what a business can do so that they can. It is reasoned from general guidance on workplace support and statutory leave, not from a study of South African firms, and it is not legal advice.

## How one person becomes essential

In small businesses, critical tasks often settle on whoever first learned to do them. The person who set up the accounting system runs it. The person who knows the suppliers places the orders. The person who understands the server restarts it. Over years, their knowledge deepens and nobody else's does, because there is never a quiet moment to teach someone else.

The dependence is usually invisible until the person is absent. Then it becomes obvious all at once: nobody knows the password, nobody knows the steps, and nobody knows which of the person's habits are essential and which are just habits. The Moment on [the employee who dreads being the only one who knows](/moments/the-employee-who-dreads-being-the-only-one-who-knows/) describes the anxiety this creates even on ordinary days.

Consider a hypothetical logistics firm with thirty-five staff. One operations administrator manages the fleet tracking system, the fuel card accounts and the weekly driver schedule. When she has flu, the schedule is not issued, two drivers turn up for the same route, and a fuel card is blocked. She takes calls from her sickbed to sort it out.

## Why sick leave exists

South Africa's [Basic Conditions of Employment Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/basic-conditions-employment-act) sets baseline conditions of employment, including paid sick leave. The point of sick leave is that an ill person stops working and recovers. A sick leave entitlement that cannot be used in practice, because the business would stall, is a right on paper only.

The World Health Organization's [fact sheet on mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) lists understaffing and excessive workloads among the psychosocial risks to mental health. Being the single point of failure carries both: there is no one to share the load, and the load does not stop when the person is unwell. The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standard on support adds that staff should know what support is available and how to access it. For a key person, the most important support is a colleague who can step in.

## The human tax: dignity and wellbeing

The wellbeing cost is direct. Working while ill slows recovery, and can spread illness to colleagues if the person comes in. Repeated over years, never being able to fully switch off can wear down health in ways that are hard to trace back to work.

The dignity cost is about being treated as a function, not a person. Being indispensable can feel like being valued, but it also means being unable to be human: to be ill, to have a family emergency or to take a real holiday. The business's continuity depends on the person never having an ordinary bad week.

## Making it possible to be ill

The remedy is cover. For each critical task held by one person, there should be at least one other person who has done it, with written steps they can follow and the access they need. That does not mean the backup must be as fast or as expert. It means the business can survive a week without the key person, and the key person can know that.

The guide on [knowing whether your business depends too much on one person](/insights/how-to-know-whether-your-business-depends-too-much-on-one-person/) helps find the risks, and the guide on [moving operational knowledge out of people's heads](/insights/how-to-move-operational-knowledge-out-of-peoples-heads/) sets out how to capture what they know. The Moment on [the owner facing illness with no one who knows the systems](/moments/the-owner-facing-illness-with-no-one-who-knows-the-systems/) describes the same problem at the top of the business.

## Practical next actions

1. **List the single points.** Write down every task that only one person can do, such as payroll, supplier payments, system restarts and scheduling. Mark those that would cause harm within a week if not done.
2. **Name a backup for each critical task.** Choose one other person for each, and agree with both people when the training will happen.
3. **Write the steps as they are done.** Ask the key person to write down the steps the next time they do each critical task, with screenshots where helpful. Keep it in a shared, secure place.
4. **Have the backup do it for real.** Let the backup run the task once while the key person watches, then once alone with the key person available. Fix the written steps where they fell short.
5. **Sort out access.** Make sure the backup has the logins, permissions and authorisations needed, granted properly through the system rather than shared passwords.

## The business outcome

Absences no longer become crises. Critical tasks continue when the key person is ill, on leave or leaves the business. The risk of a single absence causing payroll failures, missed orders or system outages falls sharply.

## The human dividend

The dividend is presence and predictability. The key person can be properly ill and recover, take leave and be present with family, knowing the business will cope. They return to work without a backlog of emergencies, and their value is in their expertise, not in their inability to be absent.

## The trade-offs

- **Training takes time.** Teaching a backup takes hours from the busiest people, which is why it rarely happens without a deliberate decision.
- **Documentation needs keeping current.** Written steps go out of date when systems change, and need a review routine.
- **Access must be controlled.** Giving a second person access to payroll or payments needs proper permissions and checks.
- **The backup will be slower.** That is acceptable. The goal is continuity, not equal speed.

## Why it matters

Every person needs to be able to be ill. A business that cannot function without one person has made that person's health part of its operating plan, without asking them. Building cover is a practical act of care: it gives the people who hold everything together permission to put themselves first when they need to.
