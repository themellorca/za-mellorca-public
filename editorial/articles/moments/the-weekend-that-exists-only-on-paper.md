On paper, the owner does not work on Sundays. The shop is open from nine to one, the rota is pinned up in the back, and two experienced staff are on duty. The owner told the family that Sunday is theirs.

At 9.40 the phone rings: a customer wants a refund larger than the till allows. At 10.15 a message: the card machine is down, can they take EFT, and to which account? At 11.30 the courier is at the back door with a delivery that is short, and someone needs to decide whether to sign for it. By lunchtime the owner has been at work for most of the morning, from the edge of a braai.

The scene is illustrative, not a specific business. But the shape will be familiar to many owners of shops, restaurants, workshops and delivery businesses: a weekend that exists on the rota and in the owner's intentions, but not in practice.

## A rest period the owner does not have

South Africa's Basic Conditions of Employment Act requires employers to give employees a weekly rest period of at least 36 consecutive hours which, unless otherwise agreed, must include Sunday. It is a floor for employees. Owners are not employees of their own business and have no equivalent protection. Their day off is protected only by how the business is organised.

The wider evidence on working time gives some reason to take this seriously. WHO and ILO estimated that working 55 or more hours a week is associated with an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and a 17% higher risk of dying from ischaemic heart disease, compared with working 35 to 40 hours. These are population-level estimates about sustained long hours, not a claim that a few weekend calls cause illness. They are a reminder that weeks without real rest are not a neutral habit.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index analysis of anonymised Microsoft 365 activity found that nearly 20% of active users in its data checked email before noon on weekends. That is a global, vendor-specific figure for office software users, not a measure of South African owners, many of whom run their businesses through phone calls and WhatsApp. It does suggest that the weekend boundary is porous well beyond any one business.

## Why the weekend leaks

Consider the illustrative shop more closely: a home and garden store in Mbombela with about 12 staff, open six and a half days a week. The two weekend staff are capable and trusted, but they have the same authority at the weekend as on a Tuesday, when the owner is in the building.

- **Decision rights do not change with the rota.** Refund limits, discount limits and supplier sign-offs are set for weekdays, when the owner is present. At the weekend they become calls.
- **Systems only know one approver.** If the till, accounting software or banking app recognises only the owner for certain actions, nobody else can act, however willing they are.
- **Exceptions have no rulebook.** A short delivery or a broken card machine is handled by asking because there is no written "what to do if".
- **Nobody is formally in charge.** When two people are on duty but neither is the named lead, both defer to the owner.

These are versions of a familiar dependency. Mellorca has looked at it in [key-person dependency is a systems problem](/insights/key-person-dependency-is-a-systems-problem/) and at the cost in [key-person operational dependency](/impact/key-person-operational-dependency/). The holiday version, where the owner packs a laptop just in case, is described in [why holidays still need a laptop in the suitcase](/moments/why-holidays-still-need-a-laptop-in-the-suitcase/). The weekend version is smaller, but it happens every week.

## The human tax: time, autonomy and energy

The time cost is the scattered hours of a nominal day off. The autonomy cost is less visible. A day off is valuable partly because it belongs to the person: they can plan it, change it, spend it on whatever matters to them. A day that might be interrupted at any moment is harder to plan and easier to give up on. Some owners stop making weekend plans at all.

Energy is the third cost. A week with no real break means the next week starts tired. It would be wrong to claim that every owner feels this. Some like being involved, find the calls reassuring, or simply enjoy their business. Mellorca does not prescribe how anyone should spend a weekend. The point is that the choice is often being made by the way authority is set up rather than by the owner.

There is also a quieter cost to the staff on duty. Phoning the owner on a Sunday is not comfortable for them either, and having to wait for an answer while a customer stands at the counter is worse.

## What could change

1. **Name a weekend lead.** One person on each weekend shift is formally in charge, and everyone knows it. That alone removes many calls.
2. **Give the lead weekend decision rights.** Set explicit limits for refunds, discounts, accepting short deliveries and similar decisions. Write them on one page.
3. **Make the systems match.** Business software commonly supports approval limits and substitute approvers. Microsoft's Business Central documentation, for example, describes setting amount limits per user and naming a substitute approver for when the usual approver is unavailable. The same idea applies to till overrides and banking roles, within the controls the bank allows.
4. **Write the exception list.** The five things that most often prompt a weekend call, each with a short "if this, do that", turn calls into actions.
5. **Agree what still justifies a call.** Safety, a security incident, or something above the lead's limits. Everything else waits for Monday, with a note.
6. **Review on Monday.** Ten minutes reviewing weekend decisions keeps control with the owner without requiring them to be present.

In the illustrative garden store, a named lead with a refund limit, a one-page exception list and a Monday review might reduce a morning of calls to one or two genuine exceptions a month.

## Trade-offs

- **Delegation carries risk.** Wider authority means more room for error or fraud. Limits, records and the Monday review exist to manage that.
- **Responsibility may need recognition.** A weekend lead takes on more than before. That may justify extra pay or a different role, which has a cost.
- **Decisions will differ.** The lead will sometimes decide differently from the owner. Accepting reasonable differences is part of the arrangement.
- **Some owners want to be called.** The aim is to make involvement a choice, not to forbid it.

## Why it matters

The business reasons are faster service at weekends and less dependence on one person. The human reason is that a day off should be a day the owner controls. When authority, systems and exceptions are set up for the weekend as it actually runs, the rota's promise becomes real.

That is the dividend: a weekend that exists off paper too, used for family, rest, sport, faith, study or nothing at all, as the owner chooses.
