The branch manager is responsible for customer satisfaction scores. She can see the problem clearly: deliveries are late because the dispatch schedule is set by head office and does not match local traffic. She cannot change the schedule. She cannot approve overtime for the drivers. She cannot hire a temporary packer for the busy month. Each month the scores come in, and each month she is asked to explain them.

This piece looks at the specific strain of being accountable for an outcome without the authority to influence it. It is reasoned from general research on role clarity and job control, not from a study of South African managers.

## How the gap opens

Accountability is easy to delegate. A target can be set in a meeting, written into a performance review or announced in an email. Authority is harder to delegate, because it means the owner or senior team giving up some control: over money, over people, over how things are done. So in many businesses, accountability travels down the organisation faster than authority does.

The gap is often unintentional. An owner who delegates a result may assume the manager will ask for whatever they need. The manager may assume that asking is a sign of weakness, or may have asked before and been refused. Either way, the person carrying the target lacks the levers to meet it.

Consider a hypothetical building supplies business with three branches. Each branch manager is responsible for branch profit. Pricing is set centrally, stock is ordered centrally and staffing levels are approved by the owner. The manager controls little beyond the daily roster and how customers are greeted. When profit falls, the manager is asked why.

## What the research says

The UK Health and Safety Executive's [Management Standard on role](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/role.htm) says employees should understand their role and responsibilities, that the organisation should make sure the different requirements it places on people are compatible, and that systems should exist for people to raise concerns about their role. Being accountable for something you cannot control is a clear case of incompatible requirements.

The [standard on control](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/control.htm) describes a workplace where people have a say in how their work is done and are encouraged to use their skills and initiative. A manager whose levers are held elsewhere has a title that implies control and a reality that lacks it.

## The human tax: dignity and energy

The energy cost is the most noticeable. Being answerable for something you cannot change produces a particular kind of tiredness. The person spends effort lobbying, explaining, apologising and finding workarounds, all to compensate for decisions made elsewhere. They are always on the back foot.

The dignity cost comes from being judged on results that reflect other people's choices. Monthly reviews feel like trials. Over time, the person may start to doubt their own competence, even though the problem is structural. Some will stop trying to improve things and focus instead on documenting why it was not their fault, which is a sensible defence and a sad use of a capable person.

The Moment on [the manager who cannot act without the founder](/moments/the-manager-who-cant-act-without-the-founder/) describes the version of this pattern in founder-led businesses. The Moment on [why clear ownership gives people room to decide](/moments/why-clear-ownership-gives-people-room-to-decide/) describes the healthier condition.

## Matching the two

The fix is not necessarily to give managers unlimited authority. It is to make sure that, for each thing a person is answerable for, they have either the authority to act on it or a fast, reliable route to someone who does. That means writing down what each role can decide, what it can spend, and whom it can direct, and checking that against what it is held accountable for.

The Moment on [why decision rights belong in the system](/moments/why-decision-rights-belong-in-the-system-not-in-someones-head/) explains why written limits matter, and the guide on [making ownership clear at every stage](/insights/how-to-make-ownership-clear-at-every-stage/) sets out a practical method.

Where authority cannot be handed over, because the decision genuinely belongs with the owner or a central function, the next best thing is speed and information. A manager who knows that a request will be answered within a day, and who can see the figures behind central decisions, can plan around them. What exhausts people is not the existence of limits but the combination of limits, silence and blame.

## Practical next actions

1. **List what each role answers for.** For each manager or team lead, write down the targets and outcomes they are held accountable for in reviews.
2. **List what each role can decide.** Next to each target, write down the decisions the person can make alone: spending, hiring, scheduling, pricing, process changes.
3. **Find the mismatches.** Mark every target where the person has no lever. For each one, either grant a specific authority, set up a fast escalation route, or remove the target from their review.
4. **Write spending and decision limits.** Agree amounts and types of decision each role can make without approval, and put them where everyone can see them.
5. **Review results with context.** In performance discussions, separate what the person controlled from what they did not, and discuss each honestly.

## The business outcome

Problems get fixed closer to where they happen and faster. Owners spend less time on decisions that a capable manager could make. Performance reviews become more useful, because they measure what people actually control.

## The human dividend

The dividend is autonomy. The person who is answerable for a result has the means to influence it. Their energy goes into solving problems, not explaining them, and their sense of competence rests on what they actually do.

## The trade-offs

- **Authority brings variation.** Managers will make different choices from the owner, and some will be mistakes. That is the price of delegation.
- **Controls still matter.** Spending limits and checks protect the business and should remain, matched to the role.
- **Owners may feel exposed.** Letting go of decisions is uncomfortable, especially in a business the owner built.
- **Some outcomes are uncontrollable.** Markets, load-shedding and supplier failures affect results regardless of authority, and reviews should account for them.

## Why it matters

Responsibility without power is a heavy thing to carry. Matching the two is not only a management technique. It is a statement that the business trusts the person it holds to account, and wants their effort spent on making things better, not on explaining why they could not.
