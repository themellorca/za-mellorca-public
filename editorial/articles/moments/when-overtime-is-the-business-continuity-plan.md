A colleague is off sick, on leave or has resigned. Nobody opens a continuity document, because there isn't one. Instead the team lead asks around, someone says they'll stay late to catch up, and the work gets done. The following week it happens again. Over a few months, overtime has quietly become the business continuity plan.

This piece looks at what it means when staying late is the standard answer to absence, and what it costs the people who provide it. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on working hours and fatigue, not a finding about any particular South African employer.

## How goodwill becomes a system

Few businesses decide to rely on overtime. It grows out of reasonable moments. The first time, a colleague covers a shift because the customer matters and the absence was unexpected. The cover is appreciated, the work is done and the problem goes away. The second time, the same people are the obvious ones to ask.

By the fifth time, nobody remembers a choice being made. The pattern now has the characteristics of a plan: it is relied on, it has regular participants and it fills a real gap. It lacks the features of a plan that make it fair or safe. It has no written procedure, no limit on how often it can be invoked and no agreed way of giving time back.

Consider a hypothetical customer service office in a medium-sized insurer's branch. Two of the six staff know how to process a certain kind of claim. When one is absent, the other works late to clear the queue. When both are absent, a team lead does it on a Saturday. Everyone agrees this is unsustainable, yet nobody has the time to document the process because everyone is covering for someone.

## What the research says about long hours

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance on [fatigue](https://www.hse.gov.uk/humanfactors/topics/fatigue.htm) explains that long working hours and irregular patterns are among the main causes of tiredness at work, and that tiredness reduces alertness and judgement. The point has an uncomfortable consequence here. Cover work is usually done in the extra hours of a person who has already worked a full day, so it is done by someone less sharp than usual, on a task that is not quite their own.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work lists excessive workloads among the working conditions that pose a risk to mental health. A permanent overtime culture is a workload that does not ease. Recovery takes time, and the evenings that would have supplied it are the ones given away.

In South Africa, the [Basic Conditions of Employment Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/basic-conditions-employment-act) contains provisions on ordinary hours of work, overtime and rest periods for the employees it covers. Which rules apply depends on the role, any collective agreement and any sectoral determination, so a business that relies on regular overtime should confirm the current position for each job instead of assuming goodwill sits outside the rules.

## What a real continuity plan would contain

Most continuity plans are described in terms of disasters: fire, flood, cyber attack. The most frequent threat to a small team is more ordinary, a person who isn't there on Tuesday. A plan for that has a few plain parts:

- **A list of what only one person can do.** For every critical task, who else could do it today, and what would they need?
- **Written steps for the top tasks.** Not a manual, but enough that a competent colleague could follow it once.
- **Access that does not depend on one login.** Systems, shared mailboxes and approvals that can be reached by a named deputy.
- **A rule on what slips.** Agreeing in advance which work waits, which is done by someone else and which is done by nobody, so that no one has to absorb everything by default.

The accounts of [the owner facing illness with no one who knows the systems](/moments/the-owner-facing-illness-with-no-one-who-knows-the-systems/) and [the sleep lost over a system only one person understands](/moments/the-sleep-lost-over-a-system-only-one-person-understands/) show how this goes wrong at the top of a business. The same dynamics play out further down, with smaller stakes and more people.

## The human tax: dignity, time and energy

The first cost is dignity. The person who stays late, again, to cover a colleague is being relied on as a resource, not planned for as a colleague. If the extra hours go unpaid, untracked or only thanked in passing, reliability becomes a quiet expectation. Declining then feels like breaking a promise nobody made.

The second cost is time. Evenings spent catching up are evenings not spent on family, study, rest or anything else. These hours are rarely tallied, which is why they are so easy to spend. The note on [why "I'll do it tonight" became the default plan](/moments/why-ill-do-it-tonight-became-the-default-plan/) describes how the habit takes hold.

The third cost is energy. A pattern of covering is tiring in a way that a single hard week is not, because there is no end in sight and no recovery built in. People who cover for others often become the ones who need cover next, which is how a continuity gap turns into a second absence.

## What could realistically change

The first step is to make the current cost visible. Count the extra hours worked to cover absence over the last quarter, by person. Most businesses discover that the total is larger than anyone thought and that it is concentrated in a few people. That list usually makes the case for itself.

The second step is to cross-train deliberately. Pair each critical task with a second person, and let them do it for real at least once a quarter so that the knowledge stays alive. [Catching up after absence: the cost of poor handover](/impact/catching-up-after-absence-poor-handover-cost/) sets out why a clean handover is cheaper than a clean-up.

## Practical next actions

1. **Tally the cover hours.** For the last three months, list each time someone stayed late or worked extra to cover an absence, who it was and how long it took. Share the totals with the team.
2. **Name a deputy for each critical task.** Write the task, its owner and a second person who can do it. Start with the five tasks that stop the business if they are not done.
3. **Write one-page procedures.** Ask each task owner to document the steps in plain language, then have the deputy follow them once. Fix what doesn't work.
4. **Decide what extra hours earn.** Agree in advance whether cover work is paid, taken back as time off, or both, and check the arrangement against the Basic Conditions of Employment Act and any sectoral determination that applies.
5. **Agree what can wait.** For a typical absence, list which tasks are done by the deputy, which are postponed and which are dropped, so the team does not default to doing everything.

## The business outcome

A written plan for absence protects service levels, shortens the time to recover from a resignation and reduces the errors that come from tired, rushed cover work. It also reveals which parts of the operation are fragile, and that knowledge is useful well beyond absence. A business that can survive a colleague's week off can usually survive a lot else.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time and presence. When cover is planned, people can leave at a normal hour, knowing the work is held by someone else, and can be properly present at home or wherever they choose to be. The ability to be absent without guilt is a benefit for the person who is away as well, since they return to a situation that has been handled and not left to pile up.

## The trade-offs

- **Cross-training takes time.** A busy team may find it hard to invest hours in documenting and rehearsing work that already seems to function.
- **Some overtime is voluntary and welcome.** Certain people want the extra pay, and the arrangement should allow for that choice within legal limits.
- **Small teams have limited depth.** A team of three cannot keep a deputy for every task and may need an outside partner or shared cover.
- **Documents go stale.** Procedures need an owner and a review date, or they quietly stop being true.

## Why it matters

Overtime as a continuity plan works until the day it doesn't, and by then the people who made it work are tired. Replacing it with a plan that is written, shared and rehearsed is not a luxury for large organisations. It is the difference between a business that depends on its staff's evenings and one that respects them.
