Picture a new general manager, three months into the role. The title is on the email signature and the organogram. The founder announced the appointment warmly and meant it. And yet, most days, the general manager cannot finish anything that matters without the founder.

A supplier needs paying, but only the founder can release payments on the bank profile. A staff member needs access to the accounting system, but the founder holds the only administrator account. A long-standing client calls with a problem and asks for the founder by name. The general manager prepares, recommends and waits. The founder, who hired a general manager precisely to stop being needed for everything, is still needed for everything.

This piece looks at that situation from the manager's side. It is not about bad faith on anyone's part. It is about the gap between appointing someone to run things and giving them the means to do it.

## Title without tools

When a business appoints its first senior manager, attention naturally goes to the person: their experience, their fit, the announcement to staff. Less attention goes to what the role needs in order to work. Authority in a modern business is not only a matter of who is in charge. It is also held in very practical places:

- **The bank profile**, which decides who can release payments and up to what value.
- **Administrator accounts** for email, accounting, payroll and other systems.
- **Approval settings** in business software, which decide whose sign-off a purchase or discount needs.
- **Client and supplier relationships**, which follow whoever the other side has been taught to call.
- **Unwritten knowledge** about pricing, exceptions and history that lives in the founder's head.

If none of these move, the appointment is real on paper but not in practice. Microsoft's own documentation for Microsoft 365 makes one of these points plainly: the person who purchased the subscription is automatically a Global Administrator. In many small businesses, that person is the founder, and nobody has changed anything since.

## An illustrative appointment

Consider an illustrative example: a Bloemfontein agricultural supplies business with thirty staff, whose founder appoints an experienced general manager from outside. This is a hypothetical scenario, not a real company. The founder plans to step back to three days a week within a year.

Six months later, the founder is working more than before. Each supplier payment run needs the founder's bank authorisation. New staff wait for system access until the founder has time. Purchase orders above a modest amount route to the founder because the approval setting was never changed. The general manager has learned to say "let me check with the founder", and staff have learned to skip the general manager and ask the founder directly.

Nobody decided this. It is simply what happens when a role is created on the organogram but not in the systems.

## The human tax: autonomy and time

For the **manager**, the tax is autonomy. They have the accountability of the role but not its means. Eurostat's statistics on [job autonomy](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Job_autonomy_and_pressure_at_work_-_statistics), based on the 2019 EU Labour Force Survey, found that managers were the most autonomous occupational group, with 78.6% reporting some or a large influence over the content and order of their tasks. Those are European figures for managers in general, but they describe what the role is normally understood to involve. A manager who cannot act is in an unusual and uncomfortable position.

It is reasonable to infer that this can affect confidence and credibility over time, as staff notice where decisions are really made. We cannot know how any particular manager experiences it, and some may be content with a slow handover. The concern is the case where the role was meant to be real and has not become so.

For the **founder**, the tax is time. The hire was meant to return time. Instead, the founder now has a new person to brief and the same tasks to complete. Mellorca has explored the founder's side of this in [when the founder's identity is fused with every task](/moments/when-the-founders-identity-is-fused-with-every-task/).

## Why the handover stalls

There are good reasons the handover is slow. Bank authority and system access carry real risk. The founder may reasonably want to see the general manager in action before handing over payments. Some clients genuinely need reassurance before they accept a new contact.

But "later" can become indefinite when there is no plan. Mellorca's insight on why [key-person dependency is a systems problem](/insights/key-person-dependency-is-a-systems-problem/) makes the point that dependency is usually built into the way things are set up, not into the people. The same applies here: the founder remains the bottleneck because the systems say so.

## What could realistically change

1. **Write a transfer plan with dates.** List each authority, from bank to systems to clients, and agree when and how it moves. A staged plan is fine; an open-ended one tends to stall.
2. **Use limited roles rather than all or nothing.** Microsoft recommends assigning the least permissive role that does the job, such as a user or helpdesk administrator rather than a global administrator. A general manager can be given the access the role needs without handing over every key.
3. **Set approval limits for the role.** Business software can usually do this directly. Microsoft's Business Central documentation, for example, describes [approval users](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/business-central/across-how-to-set-up-approval-users) with purchase amount limits and substitute approvers. The general manager approves up to an agreed value; above that, the founder.
4. **Add the manager to the bank mandate.** With appropriate limits and dual authorisation for large amounts, this is a governance decision as much as a convenience.
5. **Introduce clients in person.** A joint call or visit transfers a relationship far more effectively than an email.
6. **Redirect the questions.** When staff come to the founder with something the general manager can decide, the founder sends them back. This is uncomfortable at first and the single most effective signal that the role is real.

## What changes first, and the business outcome

The first change is that work stops waiting. Payments go out on time, new staff get access on day one, purchase orders within the limit are approved the same day.

The business becomes less dependent on one person, which Mellorca discusses in [key-person operational dependency](/impact/key-person-operational-dependency/). Decisions move faster, the general manager's credibility grows, and the founder's plan to step back becomes achievable rather than aspirational.

## The human dividend

The dividends are **autonomy** and **optionality**. Autonomy for the manager, who can do the job they were hired for and be judged on the results. Optionality for the founder, who regains real choices: to step back, to focus on the work they most enjoy, to take extended leave, or eventually to sell a business that can run without them. None of these needs to be chosen. What matters is that they become possible.

## The trade-offs

- **Transferred authority carries risk.** The manager may make decisions the founder would not. Limits, dual authorisation and audit trails contain this but do not remove it.
- **The founder loses some visibility.** Not being involved in every payment and approval means seeing less of the detail. A regular summary needs to replace that view.
- **Some clients will resist.** Relationships built over years do not transfer instantly, and a few clients may prefer to leave.
- **The wrong hire becomes more costly.** Giving real authority to the wrong person does more damage than withholding it. That is a reason to stage the transfer, not to avoid it.
- **Founders may miss the work.** Being needed can be a source of meaning. Stepping back is a choice, not an obligation.

## Why it matters

Appointing a manager is a promise in two directions: to the manager, that they will be able to manage, and to the founder, that they will no longer have to do everything. Both promises depend less on the appointment itself than on the quiet, practical work of moving authority into the places where it actually lives. Mellorca's pattern on [why one approval can hold up an entire process](/patterns/why-does-one-approval-hold-up-an-entire-process/) shows how much depends on that move.
