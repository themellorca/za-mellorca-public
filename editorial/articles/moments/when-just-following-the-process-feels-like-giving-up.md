There is a particular tone people use when they say "I just follow the process." Sometimes it is pride in doing things properly. Sometimes it is flat, almost weary, and it means something else: I used to try, and I have stopped. The words are the same. The person behind them is in a very different place.

This piece looks at the second version, the moment when compliance turns into a kind of resignation, and at what a business can do about it without throwing away rules that exist for good reasons. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on job control and role design, not a finding from any particular South African workplace.

## How the shrug is learned

Nobody arrives at a job planning to switch off. The shrug is learned in small steps. A buyer notices that a regular supplier's small top-up orders need the same three signatures as a large new contract. She suggests a lighter route for repeat orders under a set amount. She is thanked and told the process is the process. A month later she notices a duplicate step in the stock receiving form and mentions it. Nothing changes. The third time she sees something, she does not mention it.

Consider a hypothetical wholesale business with twenty staff. Every credit note, however small, needs approval from the financial manager, who is often out. Counter staff can see that a customer has been overcharged by a few rand, can see the cause, and could fix it in a minute. Instead they log a request, tell the customer it will be sorted, and wait. After a year of this, they no longer look for the cause. They log the request and move on. The process has not only slowed the fix. It has taught people that noticing is pointless.

Three conditions tend to produce the shrug:

- **No visible reason for the rule.** When people do not know why a step exists, they cannot tell a sensible control from an accident of history, so every rule feels arbitrary.
- **No route to question it.** A suggestion that disappears into a manager's inbox teaches the same lesson as a refusal.
- **No room for small judgement.** When even trivial exceptions need escalation, the job stops asking for thought.

## What the research says about control

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standards for work-related stress include [a standard on control](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/control.htm). It describes a workplace where employees have a say in the way they do their work, and where they are encouraged to use their skills and initiative. The companion [standard on role](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/role.htm) adds that systems should exist to let employees raise concerns about their role and responsibilities.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work lists low job control, including over job design and workload, among the psychosocial risks to mental health. It also names the under-use of skills. Neither source says that processes are harmful. The point is narrower: a job in which a person has no say and no route to raise concerns is a recognised source of strain, and approval processes are one of the most common places where that happens.

## The human tax: dignity and autonomy

The cost to dignity is quiet but real. Being told, in effect, that your view of your own work does not matter is a small humiliation, and small humiliations add up. The person begins to see themselves as an operator of someone else's system, not as someone trusted to think about it.

The cost to autonomy is more practical. Autonomy is not doing whatever you like. It is having enough say in how your work is done that your judgement counts for something. When every judgement is pre-empted by a rule, the person loses the experience of making decisions, and with it some of the confidence to make them when it matters. The Moment on [good judgement buried under process](/moments/why-good-judgement-gets-buried-under-process/) describes the professional version of this, and [the employee who needs permission for every small fix](/moments/the-employee-who-needs-permission-for-every-small-fix/) shows how it feels day to day.

For the business the cost is easy to miss, because a resigned employee still does the work. What disappears is the stream of small observations that would have improved it. The Moment on [why teams stop suggesting improvements](/moments/why-teams-stop-suggesting-improvements/) traces how that silence spreads from one person to a whole team.

## Separating rules that protect from rules that remain

Some steps exist for reasons that cannot bend. Tax records, payment controls, safety checks and anything that protects customers or staff from fraud or harm must hold, whatever the inconvenience. Other steps exist because someone once made a mistake, or because the old system could not do something the new one can, or because nobody ever asked whether they were still needed.

The honest move is to sort them, in the open, with the people who use the process. A rule that is explained becomes easier to accept. A rule that cannot be explained is a candidate for removal. And a rule that is right for large or unusual cases may need a lighter path for small or routine ones. The guide on [automating approvals without losing control](/insights/how-to-automate-approvals-without-losing-control/) sets out how thresholds and tiers can keep the control where it matters and remove it where it does not.

## Practical next actions

1. **List your approval steps.** For one process, such as credit notes or purchase orders, write down every approval step and the reason it exists. Mark any step nobody can explain.
2. **Ask the people who run it.** Spend half an hour with the staff who handle the process and ask where it wastes time, where it frustrates customers and what they would change. Write their answers down.
3. **Set a threshold for small exceptions.** Agree an amount or a type of case below which a trained staff member can decide alone, with the decision recorded so it can be reviewed later.
4. **Create a visible route for suggestions.** Use a shared list or form where anyone can propose a change to a process. Review it monthly and record a response to every entry, including "no, and here is why."
5. **Explain the rules that stay.** For each control you keep, add one plain sentence to the procedure saying why it exists. People follow a rule more willingly when they understand what it protects.

## The business outcome

Customer problems get fixed at the counter, not days later. Managers spend less time approving trivial cases and more time on decisions that need them. The process itself improves, because the people closest to it are once again telling you where it fails.

## The human dividend

The dividend is autonomy. Following the process becomes something the person understands and can question, not something done to them. They have a small area where their judgement is trusted, and a safe way to say when a rule has stopped making sense. That restores the sense that thinking about the work is part of the work.

## The trade-offs

- **Some controls cannot bend.** Rules required for tax, audit, safety or fraud prevention must hold, and the exception route should say so clearly.
- **Discretion brings variation.** Two people will sometimes decide the same small case differently. A record of decisions makes this visible and fixable.
- **Listening costs time.** Reviewing suggestions and answering every one takes management effort that a busy firm may find hard to protect.
- **Not everyone wants more decisions.** Some staff prefer clear rules, and that preference deserves respect too.

## Why it matters

A business shapes people as well as products. A process that never listens teaches capable people to stop offering what they know. A process that explains itself, sets sensible limits and invites challenge teaches the opposite: that following the rules and using your head are not in conflict. The difference shows up in the tone of voice when someone says they follow the process.
