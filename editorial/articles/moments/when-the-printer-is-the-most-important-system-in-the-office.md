In some offices, the most important system is not the accounting package or the email server. It is the printer in the corner. Invoices are printed to be signed. Leave forms are printed to be approved. Supplier statements are printed, ticked with a pen and scanned back. When the printer jams or runs out of toner, approvals stop, payments wait and someone spends an hour on the phone to the technician.

This piece looks at why print-sign-scan steps survive in otherwise digital offices, what they cost the people who do them, and how to retire them carefully. It is a hypothesis drawn from South African tax and electronic-transactions frameworks and vendor documentation. It is not legal advice; some documents do still require paper, and owners should check their own obligations.

## Why paper steps survive

Most print-sign-scan steps were introduced for a good reason that has since faded. A manager wanted to see the physical invoice before paying it. An auditor once asked for signed approvals. A form had to be signed in ink when the process was designed. The reason changed, but the habit stayed.

Paper steps also survive because they feel safe. A signature on a page looks like control. In fact, a scanned signature is easy to copy, and a pile of signed printouts is hard to search. The control is often more apparent than real.

## An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a freight-forwarding agency in Cape Town with fifteen staff. Every supplier invoice is printed, initialled by the operations manager, signed by the owner, scanned and attached in the accounting system. Leave forms and expense claims follow the same path. The administrator spends a good part of each day at the printer and scanner, and the stack of papers waiting for the owner's signature grows whenever he travels.

When the printer fails for two days, invoice approvals stop entirely. Suppliers are paid late, and two call to ask why. The administrator, who has done nothing wrong, spends the week apologising.

## The human tax: time and energy

The time cost is easy to see once counted: walking to the printer, waiting for jobs, collecting signatures, scanning, renaming files and attaching them. The energy cost is the background frustration of a process that depends on a temperamental machine and on finding the right person at their desk. Our story [the evening spent chasing signatures](/moments/the-evening-spent-chasing-signatures/) describes the end of that chain, and [the half hour lost to finding the right file](/moments/the-half-hour-lost-to-finding-the-right-file/) covers the search for scanned documents afterwards.

There is also an infrastructure cost. Microsoft's documentation on [Universal Print](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/universal-print/fundamentals/universal-print-whatis) describes the burden of maintaining print servers and drivers and handling support tickets from users who cannot find the right printer. Whatever product a business uses, keeping printing reliable takes effort, and that effort grows when printing sits at the centre of every approval.

## Retiring paper carefully

Retiring paper is mostly a matter of asking, for each paper step, what it is really for, and whether an electronic step can serve the same purpose.

South African law broadly supports electronic records and transactions. The [Electronic Communications and Transactions Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/electronic-communications-and-transactions-act) provides the framework for electronic communications, transactions and signatures. SARS explains on its [record-keeping page](https://www.sars.gov.za/client-segments/record-keeping/) that records can be kept in electronic form as prescribed, and that records generally need to be kept for five years. That does not mean every document can go paperless: some agreements and filings have specific requirements, and a business should check with its accountant or attorney before changing processes that carry legal weight.

For most internal approvals, though, the path is simpler. An invoice can be approved in the accounting system or an approval tool, with a record of who approved and when. Leave can be requested and approved electronically. Supplier statements can be reconciled on screen. The record is often better than the paper version, because it is searchable and time-stamped. The story about [why audit trails protect people, not just companies](/moments/why-audit-trails-protect-people-not-just-companies/) explains why such records matter.

## Practical next actions

1. **Follow the printouts.** For one week, keep a tally of everything printed and why.
2. **Ask what each step is for.** For every print-sign-scan step, write down the control it was meant to provide.
3. **Check legal requirements.** Confirm with your accountant or attorney which documents genuinely require paper or wet-ink signatures.
4. **Move internal approvals first.** Replace printed approvals for invoices, leave and expenses with electronic approvals that keep a record.
5. **Store records properly.** Keep electronic records in an organised, backed-up location that meets your retention obligations.
6. **Keep a printer, not a dependency.** Retain printing for what needs it, but make sure no routine approval stops when the printer does.

## The business outcome

Electronic approvals move faster, especially when managers travel or work remotely. Suppliers are paid on time more often, and records are easier to find during audits. Printing costs fall, and the printer becomes a convenience rather than a single point of failure for the office.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time, and a quieter kind of day. The administrator who no longer spends hours at the printer and scanner can turn to work that uses her judgement. The owner can approve an invoice from an airport lounge instead of returning to a pile of papers. And nobody spends a week apologising for a jammed machine.

## The trade-offs

- **Some paper remains.** Certain documents still need physical signatures or originals, and the plan should respect that.
- **Electronic signing has costs.** Tools, subscriptions and set-up time must be weighed against the time saved.
- **Preferences differ.** Some staff and customers prefer paper, and a sudden switch can feel like a loss of control.
- **Security still matters.** Electronic approvals need proper access controls, or they can be easier to misuse than paper.

## Why it matters

A printer at the centre of every process is a sign of habits that outlived their reasons. Retiring those habits carefully gives people back time, makes approvals more reliable and removes a small daily frustration that nobody chose. It is an unglamorous change that people notice quickly.
