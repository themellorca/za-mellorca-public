Many businesses try to make work more meaningful by adding things: a values workshop, a recognition programme, a team day. These can help. But for a bookkeeper who spends Thursday afternoon re-checking a report that nobody reads, no amount of addition fixes the afternoon. The more direct route to meaningful work is often subtraction.

This piece argues that removing meaningless tasks is a precondition for meaning, and suggests a practical way to find them. It is a hypothesis grounded in workplace health guidance and general management practice, not a finding about any specific organisation.

## What makes a task meaningless

Meaningless does not mean easy or boring. Plenty of valuable work is repetitive. A task feels meaningless when the person doing it can see that it changes nothing: the report is filed and never opened, the approval is always granted, the figure is copied from one system to another only because the systems do not connect. The person knows it, the manager often knows it, and the task continues because stopping it requires someone to decide.

An illustrative example, not a real case: a finance administrator at a Cape Town property management firm. Each month she prints the bank statement, ticks each rental receipt against a spreadsheet, scans the ticked statement and files the scan. The firm's accounting software already matches the receipts automatically. The printing and ticking began years earlier, before the software, and nobody has asked whether it is still needed. It takes most of a day each month, and she quietly resents it, not because it is hard, but because she knows it is pointless.

## Why subtraction is harder than addition

- **Nobody owns the removal.** Tasks are added by people solving a problem. Removing them needs someone with authority to say the problem has gone.
- **Fear of the one time it mattered.** A check that has caught nothing in three years still feels risky to drop.
- **Busyness looks like value.** In some cultures, a full day is evidence of commitment, so cutting tasks can feel like admitting the role was overstaffed.
- **The person doing it is rarely asked.** The administrator is the one who knows the check is redundant, but she may not feel entitled to say so.

## The human tax: dignity

The cost of meaningless work is primarily one of dignity. Being asked to spend skilled hours on something you know to be pointless sends a message about how your time is valued. The World Health Organization's guidance on [mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) names under-use of skills and lack of control over job design among the risks to wellbeing. Meaningless tasks often involve both: the person's ability is unused, and they have no say in whether the task survives.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standard on [control](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/control.htm) describes a working environment where people have a say in how they do their work and are encouraged to use their skills and initiative. Inviting people to name the tasks they think are pointless, and acting on what they say, is a practical expression of that standard.

## A simple way to find meaningless work

A subtraction review can be short. For each recurring task in a role, ask four questions: who uses the output, what would happen if it stopped, is the same thing done somewhere else already, and when was it last needed. Tasks with no clear user, no consequence on stopping, or a duplicate elsewhere are candidates for removal. Some tasks will survive the review because they protect the business; that is a useful result too, because the person now knows why the task matters.

Only after removal does automation become worth considering. Automating a pointless task makes it cheaper, not meaningful. Where a necessary task is repetitive, tools such as [Power Automate cloud flows](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-automate/overview-cloud), which can run on a schedule or when an event occurs, may carry it. But the first question is always whether the task should exist.

## Practical next actions

1. **Ask the person doing the work.** In a one-to-one, ask each team member to name one recurring task they believe nobody would miss. Write the answers down.
2. **Run the four questions.** For each named task, confirm who uses the output, what would happen if it stopped, whether it is duplicated and when it last mattered.
3. **Stop one task for a trial month.** Pause the strongest candidate, tell anyone who might be affected, and see whether anyone notices.
4. **Record the decision.** Note what was stopped, why, and who agreed, so the task does not quietly return when staff change.
5. **Return the time deliberately.** Agree with the person how the freed time will be used, whether for development, a neglected priority or simply a more manageable week.

## The business outcome

Removing meaningless work cuts cost without cutting output. It also improves the quality of the remaining work, because attention is no longer split across tasks people resent. Managers learn which controls genuinely protect the business, which makes those controls easier to defend and enforce.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time and optionality. A finance administrator who regains a day a month has room to choose: learning a new part of the system, taking on analysis she has wanted to try, or leaving on time during month-end. Meaning does not automatically fill the space. What changes is that the space exists, and the person has a say in what goes into it.

## The trade-offs

- **Some removals will be wrong.** A dropped check may turn out to matter, so trial removals and keep a way to restore them.
- **Freed time can be quietly reabsorbed.** Without an agreement, new low-value tasks may fill the gap.
- **Roles may need redesign.** If much of a role turns out to be redundant, the conversation becomes about the role itself, which needs care and honesty.
- **Speaking up takes trust.** People will only name pointless tasks if they believe it will not count against them.

## Why it matters

Meaning at work is hard to manufacture but easy to obstruct. Each pointless task tells someone that their time is cheap. Removing those tasks, with the people who do them, is a concrete way of showing the opposite, and it usually costs less than the programmes designed to make up for them.
