A finance clerk joins a business keen to understand how the numbers fit together. In her first month she asks good questions: why this supplier is paid on the 25th, why two codes exist for the same expense. By her second year, she has stopped asking. Her days are a loop of capturing invoices, chasing approvals and matching statements, and every question now feels like a delay. She is efficient, accurate and no longer curious.

This piece looks at how routine administration can crowd out the thinking that makes people grow, why the cost is easy to overlook, and what a business can do to release time for learning without letting the books slip. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on learning and work design, not a finding about any particular South African employer.

## What curiosity does at work

Psychologists describe [curiosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curiosity) as a drive to seek out new information and experience. At work, it looks modest: noticing that a figure does not look right, wondering whether a step is needed, asking how another department handles the same task. These small acts are how people learn a business rather than just a job, and how many process improvements begin.

A business that wants better systems, fewer errors and employees who can take on more responsibility depends on this kind of attention. Yet curiosity needs two things that busywork consumes: spare time and spare mental energy.

## How busywork takes both

The idea of [cognitive load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load), developed in educational psychology, holds that working memory is limited. When a task demands constant attention to detail, little capacity remains for anything else. Administrative work in finance is a good example: capturing figures correctly requires focus, and the volume of small tasks keeps the load high all day.

Time is the second casualty. A day made of many small, urgent items leaves no uninterrupted stretch for reading, experimenting or asking a colleague how something works. Learning gets postponed to "when things quieten down", which in a growing business rarely happens.

The result is a loop. Repetitive work consumes the time that might have been used to improve it, so the work stays repetitive. The piece on [how repetitive admin work accumulates across a team](/insights/how-repetitive-admin-work-accumulates-across-a-team/) describes how this builds up, one small task at a time, until nobody remembers that it was once a choice.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Durban wholesale distributor with a four-person finance team. Each month, supplier statements arrive as PDFs and spreadsheets in different layouts, and the team reconciles them by hand against the ledger. The work takes about a week of every month. It is not difficult, but it has to be right.

A junior bookkeeper, curious about why one supplier's balance never matched, spent an evening tracing the problem to a pricing change that had never been recorded. It saved the company a few thousand Rand. Her manager thanked her, then quietly asked her not to spend time on such things during month-end. The instruction was reasonable: month-end had a deadline. But she learned something from it. Curiosity is for her own time, not working hours.

Over the next year she asked fewer questions, and when a better-paid job came up at a competitor, she took it. The distributor lost a person who had shown she could find money, and replaced her with someone who would need the same year to learn what she already knew.

## The human tax: optionality

The cost to the individual is optionality, the ability to grow into different or bigger work. Someone whose days are consumed by repetitive tasks has little chance to learn the adjacent skills, such as analysis, supplier negotiation or systems work, that would open other doors. They also have little evidence to point to when seeking a promotion, because curiosity left no visible trace.

There is a quieter cost: a gradual narrowing of how a person sees themselves. People who are not asked to think begin to assume they are not meant to. The related article on [the skilled professional reduced to copy and paste](/moments/the-skilled-professional-reduced-to-copy-and-paste/) looks at how this feels from the inside, and [the employee who never has time to learn](/moments/the-employee-who-never-has-time-to-learn/) covers the time dimension in detail.

## The cost of time that never appears

For the business, the time lost is invisible. Nobody records the improvement that was not made or the question that was not asked. But the pattern can be seen in the cost of people doing work below their ability. The impact note on [high-value employees doing low-value admin](/impact/high-value-employees-doing-low-value-admin/) sets out why this is expensive even when each task is small.

## What could realistically change

The answer is not to tell people to be more curious. It is to remove enough busywork that curiosity has somewhere to go.

1. **Find the repeatable tasks.** List what the team does every week and every month. Mark the tasks that follow the same steps each time. These are candidates for templates, rules or automation, and they are the first to hand back time.
2. **Protect a small block of learning time.** Two hours a fortnight, put in the diary and respected, is more effective than a vague promise of "when it's quiet". Use it for tracing a recurring error, shadowing another department, or reading about how a process might work.
3. **Treat questions as output.** When someone traces a problem to its source, record what they found and share it. A question that leads to a fix is work, not a distraction.
4. **Pair routine with variety.** Where some admin cannot be removed, rotate it, so no one person carries all of it, and give each person one task a quarter that stretches them.
5. **Keep the deadline honest.** If month-end really has no room, say so, and schedule the curious work for the week after, rather than leaving it to evenings. The note on [why purpose gets lost in administrative noise](/moments/why-purpose-gets-lost-in-administrative-noise/) shows how clear purpose helps people tolerate necessary routine.

## The business outcome

A finance team with room to think finds errors earlier, improves its own processes and develops people who can move into more senior work. Retention improves, because people who are growing have less reason to leave. The business also gains a better view of its own operations, since the people closest to the data are the ones asking about it.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time and optionality together. Time to look at something properly rather than getting through it, and the freedom to turn that curiosity into a wider set of skills. For one person it might be learning enough about the supplier ledger to question its structure. For another it could be the confidence to apply for a role in operations. The dividend is the room to find out, not a promise of promotion.

## The trade-offs

- **Short-term output.** Time spent on learning is time not spent on tasks, which is a real cost for a small team in a busy month.
- **Uneven results.** Some curiosity leads nowhere, and not every question produces a saving.
- **Automation takes effort.** Removing routine work needs an upfront investment, and badly chosen tools can add new admin.
- **Staff may leave.** People who grow may outgrow the role. Many businesses accept this as the price of attracting capable people in the first place.

## Why it matters

When busywork takes all the time and attention, people stop asking questions, and the business stops learning from the people who see its detail most clearly. Giving back even a small amount of room restores the habit of noticing, and the habit pays for itself in errors caught, processes improved and people who stay because they are still growing.
