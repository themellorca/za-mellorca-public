The technician has diagnosed the fault in ten minutes. The pump seal needs replacing. He thinks there is one in the van, but the stock system is in the office, and the price needs confirming before the customer will agree. He phones the office. The line is busy. He sends a message. He waits. The customer stands nearby, watching, wondering why the expert in front of them cannot answer a simple question.

This piece looks at field workers who spend part of every day waiting for the office to confirm, and what it would take to let them decide. It is reasoned from general research on job control and support, not from a study of South African field service businesses.

## Why field staff wait

Field work is done far from the information it depends on. Stock levels, prices, customer history, warranty status and approval limits usually live in office systems. When the technician needs one of them, they have to ask someone. That someone is often busy, on another call, or handling three other field workers with similar questions.

Consider a hypothetical air-conditioning service company with eight technicians and two office staff. Each technician might call the office several times a day to confirm a part, a price, a customer's account status or permission for extra work. Each call is short. Together they add up to a steady pattern of waiting on site and constant interruptions in the office.

The pattern on [field work happening outside the system](/patterns/when-field-work-happens-outside-the-system/) describes the information gap from the business's side, and the pattern on [the job card typed up three days later](/patterns/the-job-card-that-is-typed-up-three-days-later/) shows what happens to the record afterward.

## What the wait does to the worker

There is a particular discomfort in standing idle in front of a customer. The technician is the visible face of the business and the person the customer trusts with the problem. Being unable to answer a basic question undermines that trust and the technician's own sense of competence. They know the fix. They cannot act on it.

The Moment on [why waiting for information feels like working](/moments/why-waiting-for-information-feels-like-working/) describes how waiting fills time without adding value. The Moment on [why approval chains make capable people wait](/moments/why-approval-chains-make-capable-people-wait/) looks at the same frustration inside offices. In the field it is sharper, because the customer is watching.

## What the research says

The UK Health and Safety Executive's [Management Standard on control](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/control.htm) describes a workplace where employees have a say in how they do their work and are encouraged to use their skills and initiative. A technician who must wait for permission on every routine decision has little control over their own pace and few chances to use their judgement.

The [standard on support](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/support.htm) says employees should receive adequate information and know how to get the resources they need to do their job. In field work, the most important resource is often information: what is in stock, what it costs and what the customer is entitled to.

## The human tax: dignity and time

The time is lost twice: the technician waits, and the office is interrupted. The dignity cost is in being treated as unable to decide what a skilled person is clearly able to decide. A technician who has diagnosed a fault and knows the right part is being asked to wait for someone who has never seen the equipment to say yes.

## Moving the decision to where the work is

Two changes do most of the work. The first is information: give field staff mobile access to the data they ask about most, such as stock in their van and the warehouse, standard prices, customer account status and job history. The second is authority: agree in advance what a technician may decide alone, such as standard repairs below an agreed amount, common parts from van stock and warranty work that meets clear conditions. Anything above the limit still goes to the office.

Together these turn most calls into decisions made on site. The office is left with the cases that genuinely need it, and has time to handle them well.

## Practical next actions

1. **Log the calls for two weeks.** Ask the office to record every call or message from field staff: what was asked and how long the answer took.
2. **Group the questions.** Sort the log by type, such as stock, price, account status and approval. The top three types will usually cover most of the calls.
3. **Put the top answers in their hands.** Give technicians mobile access to the information behind the most common questions, starting with van stock and standard prices.
4. **Agree written decision limits.** Set clear amounts and conditions below which technicians can approve work on site, with every decision recorded in the job record.
5. **Review decisions monthly.** Check a sample of on-site decisions for accuracy. Adjust the limits up or down based on what you find, and tell the team.

## The business outcome

Jobs finish faster and more are completed on the first visit. Office staff are interrupted less and can focus on scheduling and exceptions. Customers see a technician who can answer questions and act, which supports trust and repeat business.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time and autonomy. Technicians spend their day working, not waiting, and are trusted to make the decisions their skill qualifies them to make. That trust is part of what makes field work satisfying: solving a problem from start to finish, on the spot, for someone who needed it solved.

## The trade-offs

- **Mobile tools cost money.** Devices, data and software subscriptions add cost that must be weighed against time saved.
- **Signal is not everywhere.** In areas with poor coverage, real-time access may fail. Tools that work offline and sync later help.
- **On-site decisions can be wrong.** Clear limits and regular review keep mistakes small and correctable.
- **Some approvals must stay central.** Large costs, safety-critical work and unusual cases still need the office.

## Why it matters

A skilled field worker waiting for permission is a small picture of a larger choice: whether a business trusts its people with the information and authority to do their job. Moving routine decisions to the place where the work happens returns time to everyone, and tells the person on site that their judgement counts.
