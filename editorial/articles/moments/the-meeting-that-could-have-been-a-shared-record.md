It is ten past nine on a Monday. Eight people sit around a table, or in a grid of small video squares. One by one, each of them says what they did last week, what they are doing this week, and what is stuck. The others listen, or half-listen, waiting for their turn. Occasionally something important comes up. Mostly it does not.

An hour later the meeting ends. Eight hours of working time have gone into it. Most of what was said could have been read in a few minutes, if it had been written down somewhere everyone could see.

This piece is about that kind of meeting: not meetings in general, many of which are valuable, but the specific meeting that exists because the work itself has no shared record. When the only place to find out where things stand is in each other's heads, people have to gather to say it out loud.

## What the evidence suggests about meetings and interruptions

Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index analysis of anonymised Microsoft 365 activity describes what it calls the infinite workday. It estimates that the average worker in its data is interrupted about every two minutes during core hours by a meeting, email or chat. It also found that 57% of meetings were ad hoc calls without a calendar invitation. These are global figures from one vendor's product data, not a measurement of South African businesses, but they describe a working day that is already crowded before a recurring status meeting is added.

An earlier Work Trend Index survey asked people how meetings were going. Among its findings, 55% said next steps were unclear after meetings, and 57% said it was hard to catch up if they joined late. In other words, even when people meet, the outcome often does not end up anywhere reliable.

The other side of the picture comes from MIT Sloan Management Review, which reported research on 76 large companies that introduced one or more meeting-free days a week. Employees reported improvements in autonomy, cooperation, engagement, satisfaction and productivity, and less stress and micromanagement. These were large multinational firms, so the findings cannot simply be transferred to a 25-person business in Pretoria. They do show that reducing meetings does not automatically damage coordination, and can improve how people experience their work.

## Why the status meeting exists

Consider an illustrative case: a Cape Town engineering services firm of about 25 people, running a dozen client projects at a time. Each project manager keeps their own spreadsheet. Client emails sit in personal inboxes. Decisions are made on calls and remembered rather than recorded. The owner has no single place to see which projects are on track.

So every Monday, everyone meets. The meeting is the system. It is the only point in the week when the business's status is assembled in one place, and it is assembled verbally, by people reciting what they remember. When the meeting ends, the assembled picture disappears again.

The mechanism usually has a few parts:

- **Status lives in personal tools.** Spreadsheets, notebooks and inboxes hold progress, but only their owners can read them.
- **No agreed place for decisions.** When a decision is made on a call, nobody records it, so it has to be repeated next week.
- **Blockers are invisible until spoken.** A task that has been waiting on a supplier for six days is known only to the person waiting.
- **Managers cannot see without asking.** As described in [when a status update requires a staff member to investigate](/patterns/when-a-status-update-requires-a-staff-member-to-investigate/), finding out where something stands becomes a task in its own right.

Mellorca has argued in [why chasing people for updates is a systems failure](/insights/why-chasing-people-for-updates-is-a-systems-failure/) that the constant need to ask is a symptom of a missing record, not of disorganised staff. The weekly status meeting is the same symptom, scheduled.

## The human tax: time and attention

Time is the obvious cost. An hour a week for eight people is a full working day lost every week, before anyone counts the preparation, the travel between offices or the minutes spent reconnecting with the work afterwards.

Attention is the less obvious cost, and it may matter more. When status lives only in people's heads, each person has to carry it all week so that they can recite it on Monday. They hold in mind what they did, what is late and what they need from others. That carrying is a small, constant load. It is also fragile: if someone is ill or on leave, their part of the picture goes missing.

The meeting itself can also consume attention in an uneven way. People wait for their turn, listen to updates that do not concern them, and may feel some pressure to sound busy. Some find that uncomfortable; others find it reassuring. Mellorca cannot say which applies to any particular team, and should not pretend to.

## What could change

The aim is not to abolish meetings. It is to stop using a meeting as a substitute for a record.

1. **Give work one shared home.** A simple job or project board, or a shared tracker that everyone updates, means status can be read rather than recited. It does not need to be elaborate; it needs to be the agreed place.
2. **Record decisions where the work lives.** A line noting what was decided, by whom and when removes the need to re-establish it next week.
3. **Make blockers visible as they happen.** If a task is waiting, mark it as waiting and say on what. The person who can unblock it can see it on Tuesday rather than the following Monday.
4. **Change what the meeting is for.** Once status is readable, the meeting can be shorter and spent on the items that genuinely need discussion: problems, trade-offs and decisions.

In the illustrative engineering firm, moving project status from personal spreadsheets to one shared board, and agreeing that it is updated by Friday afternoon, might turn the Monday hour into twenty minutes on the three projects that need attention. The rest of the time goes back to the people who were sitting in the room.

The wider point about scattered project information is covered in [projects managed through email and spreadsheets](/impact/pain-projects-managed-through-email-spreadsheets/).

## Trade-offs

Status meetings do more than transmit status, and that deserves to be taken seriously.

- **Contact and belonging.** For some teams, Monday is the only time everyone sees each other. Removing it can leave people more isolated, particularly in hybrid teams. The presence question is explored in [when screens sit between colleagues who sit together](/moments/when-screens-sit-between-colleagues-who-sit-together/).
- **Early warning.** A tone of voice or a hesitation can reveal a problem that a status field never would.
- **A shared record needs upkeep.** If people do not update it, the business has lost the meeting and gained nothing. The record shifts some work from speaking to writing; it does not remove it.
- **Visibility can feel like watching.** A board that shows everyone's progress can be experienced as monitoring, especially if managers use it to criticise rather than to help.
- **Quieter voices.** Some people find it easier to raise concerns in a meeting than in writing; for others it is the reverse.

## Why it matters

The business case for fewer status meetings is familiar: more working time, faster responses to blockers, and less dependence on people remembering. The human case is quieter. When the state of the work lives somewhere everyone can see, people no longer have to carry it all week in order to say it aloud on Monday. They get back an hour, and they get back some of the attention that the carrying consumed.

The meetings that remain can then be about something. People gather because there is a decision to make or a problem to solve together, not because the business has nowhere else to keep track of itself. That is the dividend: not fewer conversations, but conversations that are worth the time of the people having them.
