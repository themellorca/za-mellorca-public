The manager has a new client project to assign. She looks around the team and gives it to the person who always delivers. That person is already carrying four projects, two of them behind schedule, but nobody can see that. Across the room, a colleague has had a quiet fortnight. Nobody can see that either. The decision feels sensible. It is also how workloads become unfair without anyone intending it.

This piece looks at why fair allocation depends on visible workloads, and what simple visibility makes possible. It is reasoned from general research on workload and organisational interventions, not from a study of South African teams.

## How invisible workloads drift

When nobody can see the whole picture, work is allocated by impression. Managers give tasks to people who seem available, who say yes easily, or who have done similar work well before. Each choice is reasonable in the moment. Over weeks and months, the pattern concentrates work on a few people: the reliable, the willing and the ones who do not complain.

Invisible work makes it worse. Some tasks never appear in any list, such as helping colleagues, fixing data, answering questions and covering for absences. The person doing them looks less productive on paper while working harder in reality. The Moment on [night-shift exceptions landing on the same few people](/moments/when-night-shift-exceptions-keep-landing-on-the-same-few-people/) shows the same drift in shift work.

Consider a hypothetical marketing agency with ten staff. Work arrives by email, phone and conversation, and each person keeps their own to-do list. The managing director allocates new work based on who she spoke to last and who she trusts most. Two designers consistently work late; two others leave on time. Nobody is acting unfairly. Nobody can see enough to act fairly either.

## What the research says

The UK Health and Safety Executive's [Management Standard on demands](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/demands.htm) says the organisation should provide adequate and achievable demands within agreed hours, and that employees' concerns about their work should be addressed. HSE's page on [the causes of work-related stress](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/causes.htm) lists demands as one of six main areas, and notes that talking with workers helps employers prevent stress.

The World Health Organization's [Guidelines on mental health at work](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240053052) include organisational interventions as one of their main areas, meaning changes to working conditions rather than only to individuals. Making workloads visible so they can be managed is one practical form of that.

## The human tax: dignity and energy

The energy cost falls on the overloaded: longer hours, constant pressure and no slack for the unexpected. The dignity cost falls on everyone. The overloaded feel that their willingness is being exploited. The underloaded may feel overlooked or distrusted. And anyone who raises concerns has no evidence beyond their own account, which can be dismissed as complaining.

There is a quieter cost too. When workloads are invisible, people start to manage how busy they look instead of how much they do. Some stay late so that effort is seen. Others avoid volunteering for anything, because a yes today becomes a permanent assignment. Both responses are rational, and both make the team worse at sharing work. Trust between colleagues erodes when each suspects the other of carrying less.

## What visibility makes possible

Visibility does not need complex software. A shared board listing each active piece of work, who owns it, its size and its due date can be enough. What matters is that everyone, including the manager, sees the same picture. That makes three things possible:

- **Fairer allocation.** New work goes to people with capacity, not only to people who seem reliable.
- **Early warning.** Overload is visible before it becomes missed deadlines or burnout.
- **Evidence for saying no.** A team member can point to their load instead of having to argue for it.

The pattern on [the difference between having data and having visibility](/patterns/the-difference-between-having-data-and-having-visibility/) explains why a list in someone's inbox is not the same as a shared view. The Moment on [why fair rosters need transparent systems](/moments/why-fair-rosters-need-transparent-systems/) makes the same argument for shift allocation, and [what changes when people can see their own numbers](/moments/what-changes-when-people-can-see-their-own-numbers/) describes the effect on the individual.

## Practical next actions

1. **Make one shared list.** Create a single board or sheet with every active piece of work, its owner, a rough size and its due date. Ask everyone to add their current work.
2. **Include the invisible work.** Add recurring support tasks, such as helping colleagues, data fixes and covering for others, with a rough weekly time for each.
3. **Look before you allocate.** Before assigning new work, check the board and choose based on capacity and skill, not only on reliability.
4. **Review weekly as a team.** Spend ten minutes each week looking at the board together. Rebalance where someone is overloaded and ask what can wait.
5. **Act on what you see.** If the same people are consistently overloaded, address it directly: redistribute, hire, drop low-value work or adjust deadlines.

## The business outcome

Work is delivered more evenly and more predictably. The most capable people are less likely to burn out or leave. Managers make better allocation decisions and can plan capacity before taking on new work.

## The human dividend

The dividend is predictability. People can see their own load and their colleagues', trust that new work will be shared fairly, and raise concerns with evidence. The willing are no longer punished for their willingness, and the quiet are no longer overlooked.

## The trade-offs

- **Tracking takes effort.** Keeping a board current needs a few minutes from everyone, every week.
- **Visibility can feel like surveillance.** Present it as a tool for fairness, use it to protect people, and never use it to count minutes.
- **Some work is hard to size.** Rough estimates are enough. Precision is not the point.
- **A view needs action.** If managers see overload and do nothing, visibility only makes the unfairness more obvious.

## Why it matters

Fairness at work is hard to achieve by good intentions alone. When managers cannot see who is carrying what, even fair-minded people allocate unfairly. A shared view of workloads turns fairness from a hope into a practice, and gives everyone the same picture to discuss.
