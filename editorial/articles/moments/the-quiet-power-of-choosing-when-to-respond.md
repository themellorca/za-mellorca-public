There is a small decision hiding in every message that arrives out of hours: reply now, or reply tomorrow. Many people feel that choice was taken from them long ago. The phone buzzes, the sender can see you have read it, and an unspoken rule says that a quick answer is a good answer.

This piece is about getting that choice back. Not by ignoring colleagues or customers, but by making it normal and safe to respond at a time that suits the work and the person. It is a modest idea, and it may be one of the most practical forms of autonomy available to an ordinary employee or owner.

## Why the choice matters

When people say they cannot switch off, they often describe a feeling rather than a workload. They are not necessarily working long hours; they are on call in their heads. Anything might arrive, and answering late might be seen as unreliable.

The World Health Organization, in its guidance on teleworking and worker health, points to the importance of limits around working time and the ability to disconnect. The relevant point here is not any single statistic but the principle: the boundaries of work need to be set by something other than the next notification.

South African law sets some boundaries as well. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act regulates ordinary hours of work, overtime and daily and weekly rest periods for covered employees. A law firm's overview of the idea of a [right to disconnect in South Africa](https://cklaw.co.za/work-life-harmony-exploring-the-possibility-of-right-to-disconnect-in-south-africa/) notes that there is no standalone statute of that name, and that these existing protections are the starting point. In practice, whether a message sent at nine at night counts as work is a question for the employment contract, policy and the manager's expectations, which is exactly why a clear team agreement helps.

## Reading is not the same as responding

Two things are often confused. **Reading** a message is receiving information. **Responding** is doing work. A person who sees a request at 8 pm and thinks about it for ten seconds has already given something. If they are then expected to answer immediately, the weekend or the evening is no longer theirs.

Separating the two requires tools and norms to work together.

## An illustrative scenario

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: the owner of a ten-person bookkeeping practice in Pretoria, who often does her best thinking late in the evening and sends emails as she goes.

Her staff see those emails arrive at nine or ten at night. Nobody has told them to respond, but nobody has told them not to. A couple of them reply the same evening, and the owner, noticing this, quietly begins to count it as commitment. The others, aware of it, start to check their phones before bed.

In this scenario the owner does not realise she has changed the working hours of her entire team. She just wanted to clear her own head.

The fix is small. She uses the scheduled send feature in her email tool so that evening messages go out at eight the next morning. She tells the team that nothing sent outside office hours requires a response until the next working day, and that anything truly urgent will be a phone call. Within a few weeks the evening replies stop, and so does the sense that they are expected. Mellorca's piece on [the evening email that sets tomorrow's mood](/moments/the-evening-email-that-sets-tomorrows-mood/) looks at the same dynamic from the receiving end.

## The human tax: autonomy

The tax here is autonomy: the loss of a say over when your own attention is spent. It does not require anyone to be unreasonable. A culture where quick replies are quietly rewarded creates the pressure without a single instruction being given.

It is not the same for everyone. Some people prefer to clear messages in the evening so their mornings are free, and for them the ability to reply at night is a form of control. The principle is choice. The point is that each person decides, not that nobody works in the evening.

## The dividend: autonomy and presence

The first part of the dividend is autonomy, the ability to decide when to respond. The second is presence. A person who knows that nothing requires an answer tonight can be at dinner, at a school concert or in a conversation without part of their attention on the phone. ## What could realistically change

This is mainly a matter of agreement, supported by a few settings.

- **Agree what urgent means.** Choose a channel for genuine emergencies, usually a phone call, and keep all other channels as asynchronous. Without that, everything is urgent and nothing is.
- **Use scheduled sending.** Outlook lets you schedule an email to be delivered later, so the sender can work when it suits them without setting the recipient's clock. Microsoft documents the feature.
- **Use [quiet time](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/quiet-time-in-microsoft-teams-for-mobile-devices-174c4d2d-c7c1-4228-80a7-031c14f9bcf2) settings.** Microsoft Teams on mobile can silence notifications outside chosen hours, which gives each person a way to enforce their own boundary.
- **State response expectations.** A short line in the team handbook, such as same working day for ordinary messages, removes the guesswork.
- **Model it from the top.** If the owner or manager replies at midnight and expects the same, the written rule means little. If they schedule and rest, others follow.
- **Have an out-of-hours route for real emergencies.** A named person on a clear rota, ideally with agreed compensation under the contract, is better than everyone being half on call.

## The first consequence, and the business outcome

The first consequence is quieter evenings and weekends, and fewer snap replies. The business outcome is more considered answers, since most people think better after rest, and less reactive work. Customers generally prefer a right answer tomorrow to a hurried one tonight, provided they know when to expect it.

## The trade-offs

- **Some customers expect instant responses.** Slower replies can cost goodwill in competitive or time-sensitive work, so expectations need to be set outwardly too.
- **Real emergencies do happen.** A rule that nothing is urgent after hours fails the day a payment run breaks. The emergency route has to exist and be used sparingly.
- **Norms drift.** Without regular reinforcement, the habit of replying at night creeps back.
- **Time zones and shifts complicate it.** Teams that cover different hours or regions cannot share one definition of after hours.

## Why it matters

Choosing when to respond is not dramatic. It will not transform a business. But it returns a small, daily piece of control to people who may have given it up without noticing, and that tends to matter more than its size suggests.

Mellorca's interpretation is that this is more a question of agreement than of software. The settings help, but the real change is a team deciding together that reading is not responding, and that urgent has a meaning everyone understands.
