A new person joins the finance and admin team, keen to learn. Within a month they can post a supplier invoice, capture a payroll change and reconcile the petty cash float. What they cannot do is explain why any of it is done that way. They have been taught a sequence of clicks, copy-and-paste steps and quiet adjustments, each of which exists to get around some limitation of the system. They have learned the workarounds. The craft underneath, understanding what the numbers mean and why the controls are there, was never part of the lesson.

This piece looks at why apprentices in administrative roles so often learn the workaround first, what that does to their growth and dignity, and how an office can make the real work teachable. It is a hypothesis about a common pattern, not a verdict on any particular team.

## Why the workaround is what gets handed on

Experienced staff teach what they do. If what they do every day involves a spreadsheet that corrects an export from the accounting system, then the apprentice is shown the spreadsheet. The correction is part of the job, so it is part of the training. Nobody decides to teach a bad habit; they teach the process as it exists.

Several things make this worse in finance and admin work.

- **The workaround is faster to teach than the reason.** Showing someone where to click takes ten minutes. Explaining why a particular ledger code or approval step exists takes an afternoon, and the afternoon is rarely available.
- **The reasons have been forgotten.** Many workarounds outlive the problem that created them. The senior person may no longer know whether the extra step is still needed, only that skipping it once caused trouble.
- **Mistakes are expensive.** When errors affect payments, tax submissions or salaries, supervisors understandably prefer a narrow, safe routine to open-ended exploration.

Mellorca's work on [why repeated workarounds are a warning sign](/insights/why-repeated-workarounds-are-a-warning-sign/) and on [why teams keep building manual workarounds](/patterns/why-teams-keep-building-manual-workarounds/) covers how these shortcuts accumulate. The point here is what they do to the person who inherits them.

## What learning research says about apprenticeship

Apprenticeship, in its traditional sense, combines practice with gradual access to the reasoning of an experienced practitioner. General reference material describes it as a system of training in which a learner works under a skilled person while building toward full competence in a trade or profession. The learner is meant to see the whole job, not only the repetitive parts.

Research on [situated learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situated_learning) and [cognitive apprenticeship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_apprenticeship) goes further. It argues that people learn best when expertise is made visible: when the expert explains their thinking, the learner tries tasks with support, and the support is gradually withdrawn. The learner needs to see why decisions are made, not only what is done. An office that hands over a procedure without its reasoning delivers the first part of an apprenticeship and leaves out the part that builds judgement.

Reference material on workarounds describes them as methods used to overcome a problem or limitation in a system, often temporary in intent but lasting in practice. A newcomer cannot tell which steps are essential and which are patches. To them, everything looks equally arbitrary. No survey of South African finance teams is cited here; this is an inference from general learning research.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Bloemfontein firm of building contractors with an office of five. The bookkeeper has been there eleven years. A twenty-two-year-old junior, recently qualified with a business administration certificate, joins to learn the accounts function.

In the first week the bookkeeper shows the junior how to capture supplier invoices. Because the accounting package does not handle retention amounts on contracts properly, the bookkeeper keeps a separate spreadsheet and adjusts the totals by hand before month end. The junior is told, "Always do the retention sheet first." Nobody explains what retention is, why the package cannot handle it, or what the adjusted figures feed into.

Six months later the junior is efficient. They can run the month-end routine in half the time it took in the first month. A client queries a retention balance, and the junior cannot answer. They know the steps but not the subject. When the bookkeeper takes leave, the junior follows the routine exactly and still gets it wrong, because the sheet contained an adjustment that existed only in the bookkeeper's memory.

The junior has not failed. They have been trained for a job that is really a collection of exceptions.

## The human tax: optionality and dignity

The first cost is optionality. Someone who knows only the workarounds of one office has skills that do not travel. They cannot easily move to another employer, take on a wider role or be promoted, because what they know is specific to a system that is itself an accident of history. Their future options narrow without anyone intending it.

The second is dignity. A capable young person who senses that they are doing the job without understanding it often feels quietly ashamed. They may hide gaps, avoid questions that reveal them and conclude that they are not clever enough, when the real problem is that no one has shown them the subject. The Mellorca piece on [the skilled professional reduced to copy and paste](/moments/the-skilled-professional-reduced-to-copy-and-paste/) describes the same loss of standing among experienced people, and [why documented processes help people grow into roles](/moments/why-documented-processes-help-people-grow-into-roles/) shows what the alternative can look like.

## What could realistically change

The goal is not a formal academy. It is to make the reasoning visible alongside the routine.

1. **Mark each workaround as such.** When teaching a step, say plainly whether it is a requirement or a patch for a limitation. Even this small honesty changes how the learner understands the job.
2. **Teach the output before the input.** Show the apprentice the report, return or statement that the work feeds, and trace backwards. Knowing what the final figure is for gives every earlier step a purpose.
3. **Write down why, not only how.** A procedure note with a single sentence of reason beside each step is far more useful than a bare checklist. See [which business processes need documentation first](/insights/what-business-processes-need-documentation-first/) for where to start.
4. **Rotate through the whole cycle.** Let the apprentice watch, then assist with, a full month-end, a VAT submission and a year-end preparation, not only the daily capture.
5. **Retire patches when you can.** Each workaround removed is one less thing to explain. The article on [why the person closest to the problem cannot fix it](/moments/why-the-person-closest-to-the-problem-cant-fix-it/) is a reminder that apprentices often see the flaw first and have no way to raise it.

## The business outcome

New staff become genuinely independent sooner, because they understand the work and can handle the unexpected case. Errors fall, cover during leave becomes safer, and the office stops depending on a single person's memory of why things are done a certain way. Some workarounds get retired, because someone finally asked why they exist.

## The human dividend

The dividend is optionality: a learner who leaves with real understanding of their field can choose where to take it. It is also quieter respect, the experience of being treated as someone who can understand the reasons, not only follow the steps.

## The trade-offs

- **Teaching the reasoning is slower at first.** The apprentice will be productive later, and a busy office has to accept a temporary dip.
- **Senior staff may not know the reasons.** Asking why can expose that nobody remembers, which is uncomfortable but useful.
- **Some steps really are only patches.** Not every routine has a deep principle behind it, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of dishonesty.
- **Not every learner wants the full craft.** Some will be content to stay in a narrow role, which is a legitimate choice.

## Why it matters

An apprentice who learns only workarounds has been taught the symptoms of a system, not the substance of a profession. Making the reasons visible costs a little time and gives a young person something they can carry for the rest of their working life.
