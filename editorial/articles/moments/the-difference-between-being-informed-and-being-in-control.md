A branch manager starts each morning with a set of reports. Sales against target. Stock below reorder level. Staff hours. Overdue customer accounts. A dashboard on the office screen updates through the day, and a weekly pack arrives on Monday. By any reasonable measure, this manager is well informed.

And yet, looking more closely, there is very little they can do with most of it. Stock reorders are placed by head office. Pricing is set centrally. Staffing levels are fixed by a budget agreed months ago. The overdue accounts belong to the credit controller. The manager sees nearly everything and controls nearly none of it.

This is a quieter problem than having too little information, and in some ways a harder one to notice. Being informed feels like being in control. The two are not the same.

## Information without levers

Control, in the practical sense, means being able to change what happens next. It needs three things together: knowing what is going on, having the authority to act, and having a way to act that will make a difference in time. Many reporting arrangements deliver only the first.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standards on work-related stress describe control as how much say a person has in the way they do their work. The standard is about influence over one's own work, not about how much one is told. A person can receive a great deal of information about a situation they have little power to shape.

When that happens, information can become a kind of burden. The manager now knows about the problem, may be asked about it, and may feel responsible for it, without the means to resolve it. Some people will handle this calmly, passing it on and moving on. Others may find it wearing. It would be wrong to assume one response. But the arrangement creates a recognisable gap between awareness and agency.

## How the gap opens

Few businesses design this gap on purpose. It tends to emerge from the way reporting grows:

- **Reports are built for availability, not for decisions.** Data is shown because it can be shown, rather than because someone can act on it. Mellorca has written about this in [why more dashboards do not automatically create better decisions](/insights/why-more-dashboards-do-not-automatically-create-better-decisions/).
- **The person who sees is not the person who decides.** Reports are circulated widely, but decision rights stay where they were.
- **Figures arrive after the moment has passed.** A weekly pack describes a week that cannot be changed. Mellorca has covered this in [when the weekly report is already out of date](/patterns/when-the-weekly-report-is-already-out-of-date/).
- **Nothing says what counts as a problem.** Without agreed thresholds, every number needs interpretation, and interpretation takes time and confidence.

The UK Government's AQuA Book, its guidance on producing quality analysis, starts from a role it calls the commissioner: the person who requests analysis and sets out their requirements. That is a useful discipline for any business. Reporting that begins from the question "what will this person decide with it?" looks very different from reporting that begins with "what data do we have?"

## The human tax

The tax in this situation is autonomy. The manager has been given the experience of oversight without its substance. They are expected to know, and often to explain, but not to decide. Over time this can shape how people see their own role: less as someone who runs a part of the business and more as someone who relays news about it. That shift is rarely announced. It happens gradually, as each new report adds a little more to watch and nothing more to do.

## An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative case: a building-materials retailer with branches in Mbombela and two nearby towns, and about sixty staff in total. Each branch manager receives the same daily report pack: around a dozen pages covering the whole business, with their branch's figures somewhere inside it.

In this scenario, a branch manager notices that a fast-moving product has been out of stock for three days. Replenishment is controlled by a central buyer, who receives the same report but is looking at the whole business. The branch manager emails the buyer, then phones, then explains to customers. The information reached the right person on day one. Nothing changed until day four, because the person who noticed had no lever and the person with the lever had too much to notice.

## What could realistically change

1. **Start reports from decisions.** For each role, list the decisions that person actually makes. Show them what they need for those decisions first, and the rest on request.
2. **Attach a threshold to each important figure.** Agree what level counts as a problem. Reporting tools can then alert the right person when it is crossed. Microsoft's documentation for Power BI describes data alerts that notify a user when a figure on a dashboard tile moves beyond a limit they set, and notes that alerts only work on refreshed data. The point is not the tool but the habit: a number with a threshold asks for action; a number without one asks for interpretation.
3. **Pair visibility with a bounded action.** If a branch manager will see stock-outs, give them a defined way to act on them, such as raising an urgent order within limits, or a guaranteed response from the buyer within a set time.
4. **Route the alert to the person with the lever.** Where the viewer cannot act, make sure the person who can is told directly, not just copied on a report.
5. **Remove reports that nobody acts on.** Fewer, sharper reports leave more attention for the ones that matter.

## What changes first, and the human dividend

The first change is that some numbers stop being merely interesting and start triggering something. A stock-out produces an order, not an email chain. The business outcome is faster response and less time spent reading reports that change nothing. Mellorca has written about the cost of the opposite in [why management finds out too late](/patterns/why-management-finds-out-too-late/).

The human dividend is autonomy and predictability. The person who sees a problem either has a way to act on it, or knows exactly who will and by when. That is a different working experience from carrying awareness without agency. It also tends to reduce a particular kind of mental load: the unresolved knowledge of problems one can see but not touch.

## The trade-offs

- **Narrower reports can hide context.** A manager who sees only their own decisions may miss patterns elsewhere in the business. Access to the wider picture should remain available.
- **Thresholds can become targets.** Once a number has a line, people may manage to the line rather than to the outcome.
- **Alerts can multiply.** Too many alerts produce the same overload as too many reports, just faster. Mellorca's Moments piece on [figures nobody can agree on](/moments/when-nobody-can-say-which-number-is-true/) shows how fragile trust in numbers already is.
- **Giving levers means giving accountability.** A manager who can act will also be asked why they did not. Not everyone wants that, and it should be agreed rather than assumed.

## Why it matters

Businesses often respond to a feeling of being out of control by producing more information. Sometimes that helps. Often it simply increases the number of people who know about problems they cannot solve. Mellorca's interpretation is that the more useful question is not "who should see this?" but "who can do something about this, and do they have the means?" Answering it returns something specific to people at work: not just knowledge of what is happening, but a real say in what happens next.
