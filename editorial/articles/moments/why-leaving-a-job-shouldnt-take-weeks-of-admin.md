An employee resigns, works her notice and says goodbye on a Friday. She expects her final pay at the next pay run. It arrives, but without her leave pay, which someone forgot to calculate. She needs her tax certificate and a certificate of service for her new employer, and asks twice before they arrive. If she needs to claim unemployment benefits between jobs, she also needs the employer's paperwork. Meanwhile, she still has access to the old company's email on her phone, which nobody has noticed. Leaving took a day. Finishing leaving takes weeks.

This piece looks at why exit administration so often drags, what it costs the person leaving, and how to make exits as orderly as onboarding. It is a hypothesis drawn from South African tax and employment frameworks and vendor documentation. It is not legal advice; employers should confirm their obligations for each case.

## Why exits drag

Exits drag because nobody owns them end to end. Payroll calculates the final salary. Someone else is meant to calculate leave. The manager is supposed to collect the laptop. IT is supposed to remove access. The owner signs the certificate of service when reminded. Each step is small, and each waits for someone to notice it.

There is also less motivation. A new hire is the future of the business, so onboarding gets attention. A leaver is the past, so exit tasks slip down the list. The person on the other end, though, is still waiting for money and documents that matter to them.

## What leavers are waiting for

Several of the items a leaver needs carry formal obligations. The SARS page on [pay-as-you-earn](https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/pay-as-you-earn/) states that employers must issue IRP5 or IT3(a) certificates to employees, and that when an employee resigns, retires or dies the certificate must be delivered within fourteen days. The [Basic Conditions of Employment Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/basic-conditions-employment-act) sets out the basic conditions of employment, including matters related to termination, such as notice and payments due when employment ends, for the employees it covers. Many leavers also need their employer's declaration to the Unemployment Insurance Fund if they claim benefits.

These are not favours. They are things the leaver is owed, often needed for a new job, a tax return or income between jobs.

## An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a chain of three furniture stores in the Vaal with forty staff. Resignations are handled by each store manager, who emails head office. Head office processes final pay but often learns of leave balances late. Tax certificates are generated in a batch at year-end unless someone asks. System access is removed when the IT contractor next visits.

A former sales assistant waits five weeks for her leave pay and three for her tax certificate, and phones head office repeatedly. The payroll clerk, who is handling all of this alongside regular payroll, dreads those calls. Nobody is acting in bad faith. There is simply no exit process.

## The human tax: time

For the leaver, the tax is time spent chasing, and the anxiety of not knowing when money will arrive. For someone moving between jobs, a delayed final payment or missing document can affect rent, debit orders and the start of the new role. It also leaves a sour final impression of the employer.

For remaining staff, the tax is interruptions and awkward calls. The payroll clerk in our example has much in common with [the payroll clerk who never closes the month cleanly](/moments/the-payroll-clerk-who-never-closes-the-month-cleanly/). For managers, unfinished exits linger as open items that never quite close.

## Making exits orderly

The fix is to treat exits as a defined process with an owner, a checklist and a timeline, just as onboarding should be. A simple checklist covers final pay and leave pay, the tax certificate within the required period, the certificate of service, any unemployment insurance declaration, the return of equipment, removal of system access and a handover of work.

Technology can help with the access side, which is often the most neglected. Microsoft's documentation on [lifecycle workflows](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/id-governance/what-are-lifecycle-workflows) describes automating joiner, mover and leaver processes, so that people who leave have their access removed in a timely way, with a history of what was done. A small business may not need such a tool, but it needs the same outcome: access removed on the last day, and a record of it.

Handover matters too, so that removing access does not lose knowledge. The story [why a good handover note is an act of care for colleagues](/moments/why-a-good-handover-note-is-an-act-of-care-for-colleagues/) describes what a useful handover contains, and [what a reliable handover gives back on a Friday afternoon](/moments/what-a-reliable-handover-gives-back-on-a-friday-afternoon/) shows the benefit.

## Practical next actions

1. **Name an exit owner.** Make one person responsible for every exit from resignation to final documents.
2. **Write a checklist.** List every exit task, with who does it and by when, including pay, leave, certificates, equipment and access.
3. **Calculate leave at resignation.** Confirm the leave balance when notice is given, not after the last day.
4. **Issue documents promptly.** Prepare the tax certificate and certificate of service during the notice period so they are ready on time.
5. **Remove access on the last day.** Disable accounts and collect devices on the final day, after the handover is complete.
6. **Tell the leaver the timeline.** Give the departing employee a written note of what they will receive and when.

## The business outcome

An orderly exit process reduces calls from former employees, closes payroll items cleanly and lowers security risk from forgotten accounts. It also protects the employer's reputation. Former staff talk to future candidates, and a respectful exit is part of being a good place to work.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time, and a clean ending. The person leaving receives what they are owed on a known schedule, without chasing, and can give their attention to the next chapter. The payroll clerk processes an exit in days and moves on. Leaving becomes an ordinary event, not a drawn-out frustration for everyone involved.

## The trade-offs

- **Some amounts take time.** Final payments can depend on returned property or unresolved disputes, and should be explained rather than delayed silently.
- **Fast access removal needs a handover first.** Cutting access too early can lose work that should have been handed over.
- **Checklists need upkeep.** As systems and rules change, the exit checklist must be reviewed.
- **Tools may cost money.** Automated access removal may require licences a small business does not have.

## Why it matters

How a business treats people on the way out says a good deal about how it treats people generally. An exit process that delivers pay, documents and closure on time respects the work the person did and lets them move on cleanly. It is a modest piece of organisation with a lasting effect on trust.
