The biggest client is on the phone again, and the owner already knows what it will be. A change to the scope, a request for a faster turnaround, a gentle mention that another supplier has offered a lower price. The owner says yes, as the owner has said yes to the last eight requests. The client accounts for most of the business's income, so saying anything else is not really an option.

This piece looks at what happens to an owner-managed business when one client becomes too large to lose: why it happens so naturally, how it quietly removes the owner's ability to choose, and what a business can do to widen its footing without walking away from the client that keeps it alive. It is a hypothesis drawn from general business and risk thinking, not a finding about any particular South African employer.

## How it happens without anyone deciding

Nobody sets out to depend on a single customer. It usually starts well. A large client places a big order, the business hires to meet it, buys equipment, perhaps takes on bigger premises. Revenue jumps. The next year the same client asks for more, and it makes sense to give them priority. Other customers, who are smaller and less demanding, get less attention because there is only so much time.

The finance world calls the underlying idea [concentration risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration_risk): the exposure that comes from relying heavily on a single counterparty, sector or source. Banks and investors measure it formally. A small business seldom does, but it feels the same thing in a simpler form. If this one customer left, how many months could we last?

## What dependence does to decisions

The financial risk is obvious. The behavioural risk is less so. A business that earns most of its income from one client loses bargaining power, and the owner knows it. Prices stay where they are, because asking for an increase might be taken badly. Payment terms drift from thirty days to sixty, because the client's finance department is slow and the owner does not want to complain. Requests that fall outside the contract are met anyway, because refusing is too risky.

Over time, the owner stops evaluating whether a request is reasonable and starts evaluating whether it is survivable. The business becomes, in effect, an extension of the client's operations, and the owner a manager of the client's priorities rather than their own.

The article on [the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake](/moments/the-cash-flow-week-that-keeps-the-owner-awake/) shows how a single late payment from a large customer can turn into a week of sleepless arithmetic. When the late payer is also the biggest customer, the stress is multiplied.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Pretoria packaging company with nineteen employees. Seven years ago, it won a contract to supply a national retail group. Today that contract represents roughly three quarters of turnover. The retailer pays in sixty days, sometimes seventy-five. The owner finances the gap with an overdraft that costs more every time the interest rate moves.

When the retailer asks for a design change that requires new tooling, the owner pays for it rather than negotiate. When the retailer's buyer hints that a competitor has quoted lower, the owner trims the margin without being asked. Each concession is small. Together, they leave the company with thin margins on its biggest account and little cash to invest in finding others.

The owner would like to approach new customers, but the plant is working at near capacity for the retailer, and there is no time to chase new business. The retailer's volume is the very thing blocking diversification.

## The human tax: optionality and financial resilience

The first cost is optionality, the freedom to say no, to raise a price, to take a holiday, or to choose which work to chase. An owner whose income hangs on one relationship cannot afford to let that relationship cool. The second is financial resilience. A single lost contract, a restructuring at the client or a change of buyer could take a business from comfortable to critical in weeks.

The owner carries this personally, often without telling staff. Employees see a busy workshop and assume security, while the owner knows how thin it is. The piece on [why knowing your margins changes what you can choose](/moments/why-knowing-your-margins-changes-what-you-can-choose/) explains how understanding the real profit on each client is the first step to regaining choice.

## What could realistically change

Reducing dependence does not mean ending the relationship. It means building a business that could survive a hard conversation.

1. **Measure the concentration.** Work out what share of revenue and of gross profit each client represents. The two figures often differ: a large client at a low margin may matter less to profit than its size suggests.
2. **Know the runway.** Calculate how many months of fixed costs the business could cover if the largest client stopped buying tomorrow. This figure turns a vague worry into something to plan around.
3. **Tighten the terms.** Review payment terms, price escalation and scope change rules in the contract. Where possible, add a clause that adjusts price for agreed cost increases, and a process for charging for out-of-scope work. Raise these at renewal, when both parties expect to talk.
4. **Set a diversification target.** Aim for a modest and specific goal, such as no client above forty per cent of revenue within two years, and assign time each week to chase it. Small, steady steps work better than a sudden change.
5. **Protect capacity.** Decide in advance how much capacity can be committed to any single client, and keep some in reserve for new work. The note on [the owner who can finally say no to work](/moments/the-owner-who-can-finally-say-no-to-work/) shows what the freedom to decline gives back.

The underlying risk is also operational. If only the owner holds the relationship, the client's loyalty is to a person, not to the business. The impact note on [key-person operational dependency](/impact/key-person-operational-dependency/) covers why that deserves attention too.

## The business outcome

A business with a wider client base can negotiate from a steadier position. It can raise prices at the right moment, refuse unprofitable requests and make long-term investments without fearing that one phone call will undo them. Lenders and buyers also tend to value diversified revenue more highly, which matters if the owner ever wants to borrow, sell or step back.

## The human dividend

The dividend is optionality and financial breathing room. For an owner, it is the ability to answer a client's demand with "let me think about that" rather than "of course". It is the room to take a week off without checking the phone, to hire for growth rather than for one account, and to sleep on the night before a contract review. It is not freedom from risk, but the replacement of a single point of failure with several smaller exposures.

## The trade-offs

- **Short-term income.** Time spent chasing new clients is time not spent serving the big one, and early new accounts are often smaller and less profitable.
- **Relationship risk.** Pushing back on terms may strain a relationship that the business depends on. The approach needs care and good timing.
- **Capacity limits.** A small business cannot always serve many clients well, and spreading too thin can harm quality.
- **Some concentration is rational.** A large, stable client on fair terms may be a good foundation. The aim is to see the risk clearly, not to eliminate it.

## Why it matters

A big client is a gift until it becomes the only thing standing between the business and a hard month. Measuring the dependence, improving the terms and gradually widening the base turns a hostage situation back into an ordinary commercial relationship, in which the owner can say yes because they want to rather than because they have to. Reading [business continuity planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_continuity_planning) is a helpful start for thinking through what would happen if the largest account disappeared.
