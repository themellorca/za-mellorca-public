Ask a nurse why they chose the profession and the answers are rarely about forms. They talk about caring for people, being useful in a hard hour, or the satisfaction of noticing something early and acting on it. Ask the same nurse what fills their shift, and a different picture emerges: charts, handover notes, medication records, referral forms, stock sheets and the same patient details typed into more than one place.

The headline of this piece, "half paperwork", is a perception that many clinicians would recognise. It is not a measurement, and the research below suggests the real figure varies a good deal. The point of the article is not to settle the number but to look at what happens when documentation grows large enough to change how the work feels, and what a clinic or hospital could do about it. It is a hypothesis drawn from published reviews, not a finding about any South African facility.

## What the research reports

A [systematic review of interaction time with electronic health records](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8387128/) found that nurses spent between 18% and 57% of their working day on this activity across the studies it included. When the authors pooled the time-motion studies, which they regarded as less prone to bias, the average came to roughly 22% of a shift. The wide spread matters. Depending on the ward, the system, the method of measurement and how documentation is defined, a nurse's share of paperwork can be a fifth of the shift or well over a half.

A [time-motion study of nurses' time allocation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6371290/) looked at how nursing hours were actually divided and reported that documentation took up a substantial share, about a quarter in that setting, alongside direct care and other tasks. And a [systematic review of the effect of health information technology on nurses' time](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32159770/) reported that most of the systems studied increased documentation time, though some tools, such as barcode medication administration, reduced time for the tasks they supported.

Three cautions follow. These studies come mostly from other countries and health systems. Methods differ, so numbers cannot be compared too tightly. And none of them says documentation is wasteful in itself. The honest summary is that recording takes a large and variable share of nursing time, and that how it is designed makes a difference.

## Why records grow

Nobody sets out to bury nurses in paper. Documentation accumulates for understandable reasons.

- **Safety and continuity.** A clear record lets the next shift know what happened, and it protects patients when staff change.
- **Legal and funder requirements.** Medical schemes, regulators and accreditation bodies each ask for evidence in their own format.
- **Layers added, none removed.** A new form arrives after an incident, but the old one is not retired, so the same facts are recorded twice.
- **Systems that do not talk.** Admissions, pharmacy, billing and the ward chart each need the same details keyed in separately.

The result is a nurse who is also a part-time data clerk, a theme also visible in [the teacher who became a data-entry clerk](/moments/the-teacher-who-became-a-data-entry-clerk/) and [the skilled professional reduced to copy and paste](/moments/the-skilled-professional-reduced-to-copy-and-paste/).

## Illustrating the drift

Consider an illustrative private day clinic in Cape Town with a handful of nursing staff. At each patient visit the nurse records observations on a paper chart, enters the same findings into the practice system for billing, and writes a summary for the referring doctor. Each step is defensible. Together they mean that on a busy afternoon the nurse is typing while the patient waits, and the unhurried conversation that would have surfaced a worry is the first thing to shrink.

The nurse does not experience this as a time-management problem. They experience it as a slow redefinition of the job, from caring for people to producing a record that they cared for them.

## The human tax: dignity and presence

Two costs stand out. The first is dignity. Skilled people want their expertise used, and a trained clinician spending long hours on re-keying and form-filling can feel that their judgement is being treated as optional. The second is presence. Time at a keyboard is time away from the patient, and patients notice. Fewer questions are asked, fewer worries are spoken, and the nurse leaves a shift with the sense of having processed people instead of looked after them.

Not every nurse will frame it this way, and some value the discipline of good records. The aim is not to dismiss documentation. It is to ask which of it supports care and which merely repeats itself.

## What could realistically change

The most reliable gains come from removing repetition before adding technology. A story such as [why purpose gets lost in administrative noise](/moments/why-purpose-gets-lost-in-administrative-noise/) makes the broader case, and [the dignity of work that adds something new](/moments/the-dignity-of-work-that-adds-something-new/) describes what is gained when routine recording stops competing with skilled work.

In practice this means mapping where each piece of information is written, deleting the duplicates, capturing data once at the point of care, and letting other systems read it instead of asking for it again. Where software is part of the answer, it should be judged by whether it removes entries, since the review above shows that new systems often add time.

## Practical next actions

1. **Measure before changing.** Ask two or three nurses to note, for a week, how many minutes they spend on each form or system per patient. Use real times in place of impressions.
2. **Trace one patient visit.** Follow a single visit from arrival to discharge and list every place the same detail is written down. Mark each repeat.
3. **Retire one form.** Pick the least-used or most duplicated form and agree, with the clinical lead, to stop using it. Check that no regulator or funder requires it first.
4. **Move recording to the point of care.** Where possible, let nurses capture information while with the patient, on one device or one chart, instead of afterwards from memory.
5. **Ask the nurses.** Hold a short session on what paperwork they would drop first and what they would never drop, and treat the answers as design input.
6. **Review every quarter.** Re-measure the minutes and check that new requirements are replacing old ones and not stacking on top.

## The business outcome

A facility that cuts duplicated recording gets more patient-facing time from the nurses it already employs, which supports throughput without adding headcount. It reduces transcription errors, since each fact is entered once, and it makes the work more attractive in a profession where retention is a constant concern. Staff who feel their skills are used tend to stay, and recruiting a replacement nurse is slow and costly.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time and presence. Minutes returned from the keyboard can go to listening, to checking on a worried patient, to teaching a junior colleague or simply to finishing the shift on time. For the nurse, it is the experience of doing the work they trained for. How each person uses the time is their own judgement; the point is that the judgement is theirs to exercise.

## The trade-offs

- **Records protect people.** Cutting documentation too far can weaken handovers, medico-legal protection and continuity of care.
- **Some forms are not optional.** Regulators, medical schemes and accreditation bodies set requirements that a facility cannot waive.
- **Change has a cost.** New forms and systems need training and a bedding-in period during which time may get worse before it improves.
- **Later readers lose detail.** A shorter record gives the next clinician less to work with, so what is removed should be chosen with them.

## Why it matters

People enter caring professions for reasons that have little to do with administration, and a health service depends on them keeping those reasons. If a nurse's shift is dominated by recording care, the business is spending its most valuable clinical skill on clerical work. Giving some of that time back is good for the patient in front of them and for the person who chose to stand there.
