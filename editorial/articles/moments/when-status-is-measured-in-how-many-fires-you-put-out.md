Most businesses have a person everyone calls when something breaks. The delivery that went to the wrong branch, the payroll file that will not upload at four on the last working day, the client whose statement does not match their own records: this person drops everything, solves it, and is thanked in the corridor. Over a year, that thanks adds up to a reputation. Without anyone deciding it, rescuing has become the way status is earned.

This piece looks at what happens to people and to a business when status is measured in fires extinguished. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on work pressure, not a finding about any specific South African firm, and the scenarios are illustrative.

## Why rescuing is rewarded and preventing is not

A rescue is visible. It has a start, a villain (the broken thing), a hero and an end. Everyone in the building knows it happened. Prevention is the opposite: when a problem is stopped before it exists, nothing happens, and nobody can point to the nothing. The employee who quietly fixed the root cause of last quarter's invoice errors has no story to tell, because the errors did not occur.

Managers are not being foolish when they reward what they can see. A busy owner notices the person who stayed until nine to get the stock count right. They rarely notice the person whose tidy supplier records meant that count was never in doubt. The incentive that results is quiet but strong: it pays to be near the emergency.

## What it does to the person at the centre

Being the designated rescuer feels good at first. It carries trust, importance and a sense of being needed. The costs arrive more slowly.

- **Energy.** Emergencies arrive on the business's schedule, not the employee's. Evenings and weekends become standby time, whether or not anything happens.
- **Dignity.** A person valued mainly for rescuing is valued for the failures of the system, not for their judgement. If the system improved, their standing would fall, which is a strange position to be in.
- **Career shape.** Rescuers are hard to promote because the business cannot spare them from the fires, and hard to develop because there is never time for anything else.

The Job Demands-Resources model, a widely used framework in occupational research and summarised in [this overview of the Job Demands-Resources model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_demands-resources_model), describes work as a balance between demands that drain and resources that restore. A role built around unpredictable emergencies is heavy on the first and, if there is no time to recover or plan, light on the second.

## What it does to everyone else

The effect spreads. Colleagues learn that raising a quiet warning gets less attention than waiting for the problem to become loud. A team member who spots a small mismatch in a delivery note may stay silent, because flagging it would make no one a hero. The business gradually loses its early-warning system, and the fires grow more frequent. This is why the pattern feeds itself: the more crises there are, the more status rescuing carries, and the less anyone invests in preventing the next one.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards for work-related stress treat demands, control and support as the conditions that decide whether pressure becomes harmful. Its page on [demands in the management standards](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/demands.htm) notes that employees should be able to cope with the demands of their jobs and that systems should be in place to respond to individual concerns. A culture that prizes firefighting is, in effect, one where demand is set by whatever breaks next.

## An illustrative case

Picture, as a hypothetical example, a Gqeberha parts distributor with thirty staff. The operations coordinator is known as the person who can sort anything out. In one week she re-routes a truck after a wrong dispatch, rebuilds a customer's missing order from emails, and talks a supplier into an urgent re-delivery. The owner praises her at the Friday meeting. Nobody asks why three orders in one week had the wrong address, because the address field is free text and the capture screen allows it.

The coordinator's reputation rises. The address field stays the same. Next week there are three more.

## Changing what the business notices

None of this means rescuers should be discouraged. Responding well under pressure is a real skill. The aim is to make sure the business also notices, names and rewards the work that removes the need for the rescue, and to treat each emergency as information about the system rather than only as a test of the person. The note on [what a week without firefighting would feel like](/moments/what-a-week-without-firefighting-would-feel-like/) describes the experience on the other side. For the practical question of which problems truly need a drop-everything response, the guide to [separating urgent systems problems from merely annoying ones](/insights/how-to-separate-urgent-systems-problems-from-merely-annoying-ones/) is a useful filter.

## Practical next actions

1. **Keep a simple fire log for one month.** Record each emergency, who handled it, how long it took, and what caused it. Do not assign blame. The aim is to see patterns, such as the same field, supplier or report appearing again and again.
2. **Ask a root-cause question after every rescue.** A five-minute conversation: what let this happen, and what small change would stop it recurring? Write down the answer and an owner.
3. **Praise prevention out loud.** In the weekly meeting, name one thing that did not go wrong because someone fixed its cause. Be specific about what they changed.
4. **Protect planned time for the person who rescues.** Block a few hours each week that are not available for emergencies, and use them for the root-cause fixes the log has identified.
5. **Put the cheapest fix first.** Many repeating fires come from a free-text field, a missing approval step or an unclear owner. Use the thinking in [why recurring maintenance is cheaper than emergency repair](/insights/why-recurring-systems-maintenance-is-cheaper-than-emergency-repair/) to compare the cost of a fix against the cost of the next rescue.
6. **Review how you recognise people.** Look at the last six months of thank-yous and promotions. If most went to rescues, adjust what you measure and mention.

## The business outcome

A business that rewards prevention sees fewer emergencies, calmer weeks and more predictable delivery. It also keeps its best problem-solvers, because they are no longer spending their energy on repeat failures. Predictability is the main gain: a plan for Thursday that is still the plan on Thursday afternoon.

## The human dividend

For the person at the centre, the dividend is predictability and the return of dignity. Their standing rests on what they build and improve, not on how often the system lets them down. What they do with the recovered evenings is theirs to decide. For some it is rest, for others study, a side project or simply a normal dinner. The sleep and attention lost in the other direction are explored in [the sleep lost over a system only one person understands](/moments/the-sleep-lost-over-a-system-only-one-person-understands/).

## The trade-offs

- **Prevention is slower to show results.** The first months of root-cause work may feel like less output, because the benefit is fires that never start.
- **Some emergencies are genuine.** Weather, supplier failure and load-shedding cannot be designed away, and a capable responder remains valuable.
- **Changing recognition can feel like criticism.** A long-standing rescuer may hear a new emphasis as a verdict on past work, so the shift needs careful framing.
- **Logging takes effort.** A fire log costs a few minutes per incident, and it is only worth keeping if someone acts on it.

## Why it matters

What a business praises is what it gets more of. If the loudest praise goes to the rescue, the business will keep producing things to be rescued from. Moving some of that recognition to the quiet work of making problems smaller costs nothing, and it gives the most capable people a future beyond the next emergency.
