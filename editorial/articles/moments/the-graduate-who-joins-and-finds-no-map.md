The first day of a first job carries a particular mix of pride and nerves. The graduate has a degree, a new outfit and a strong wish to prove they were the right choice. They arrive to find that the person who was going to train them is on leave for two weeks, or off sick, or has just resigned. Someone shows them a desk and a login. After that, they are mostly on their own.

This piece looks at what happens to graduates who join small businesses with no written map of the job, especially when the one person who holds the knowledge is absent, and what a useful map looks like. It is a hypothesis informed by workplace-stress standards and the South African skills framework, not a survey of graduate experiences.

## Why there is no map

In a small firm, the job a graduate is hired into usually exists as a set of habits in someone else's head. The bookkeeper knows which clients send statements late. The site supervisor knows which supplier delivers early. Nobody wrote it down because nobody needed to; the people doing the work already knew.

That works until a newcomer arrives at the same moment that the knowledgeable person is away. Then the gap is suddenly very visible, and it lands on the person with the least power to close it.

## An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a small accounting practice in Polokwane hires a graduate as a trainee. The senior bookkeeper who handles the payroll clients is meant to train her. In her second week, he is booked off sick for a month.

The graduate is given access to the payroll software and a list of clients. She does not know which clients are paid weekly, which have special deductions, or where the previous month's working papers are filed. She asks the partner, who is in meetings most days and answers in brief messages. She is anxious about making mistakes on people's wages, so she checks everything twice and still gets one wrong. By the time the senior bookkeeper returns, she has concluded that she is not good at the job. In fact, she was never given the job to learn.

## The human tax: confidence

The tax is confidence, and it is particularly costly at the start of a career. A graduate who struggles in a well-documented role can see that the problem is skill and work on it. A graduate who struggles in an undocumented role cannot tell whether the problem is them or the setup, and often blames themselves.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's standard on [role](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/role.htm) expects organisations to give employees information so they understand their role and responsibilities, and to make requirements clear. Its standard on [support](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/support.htm) expects that employees know what support is available, how to access the resources they need, and that they receive regular, constructive feedback. A graduate with no map and an absent mentor has none of these, through nobody's malice.

## What a role map contains

A role map is not a policy manual. It is a short, practical description of the job that lets a capable newcomer find their way. For most roles it fits in a few pages and links to the detail:

- **The rhythm of the work.** What happens daily, weekly, monthly and annually, with dates.
- **The main tasks, step by step.** Brief instructions for the five to ten tasks that make up most of the role, with screenshots where useful.
- **The exceptions.** Known quirks: the client who needs a different format, the supplier who must be phoned rather than emailed.
- **Where things live.** Folder locations, system names and logins to request.
- **Who to ask.** A first and second contact for each area, so the absence of one person is not a dead end.
- **What good looks like.** How the newcomer will know they are doing well, and when they will get feedback.

The map is best written by the person who does the work, reviewed by a manager, and then improved by the newcomer as they find gaps. That last step turns the graduate from a passive recipient into a contributor in their first month. The broader case is made in [why documented processes help people grow into roles](/moments/why-documented-processes-help-people-grow-into-roles/).

## Linking it to formal learning

Many South African graduates enter work through internships or learnerships. The [Skills Development Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/skills-development-act) provides the framework for workplace skills development and learnerships linked to recognised qualifications. A role map makes such programmes more effective, because structured learning needs a clear picture of the work it is preparing people for, and workplace mentors cannot always be present.

## Practical next actions

1. **Write the map before the start date.** Ask the person who does the work to draft a short role map before any new hire arrives.
2. **Name two contacts per area.** Make sure no part of the role has a single person as its only source of knowledge.
3. **Plan for absence.** Assume the main mentor may be away in the first month, and decide in advance who covers.
4. **Schedule feedback.** Book short weekly check-ins for the first three months, so the graduate hears how they are doing.
5. **Ask the newcomer to improve it.** Make updating the map part of the graduate's first-month work.
6. **Review after each hire.** Use what each new starter struggled with to strengthen the map for the next one.

## The business outcome

Graduates who have a map become productive sooner and make fewer costly mistakes. The business becomes less dependent on any one experienced person, which also helps when that person is on leave or moves on. Firms that are known for training people well find it easier to recruit good graduates.

## The human dividend

The dividend is predictability: knowing what the job is, where the steps are and who to ask. For a graduate, that predictability is the ground on which confidence grows. Instead of spending a first month afraid of invisible rules, they spend it learning the work and discovering that they can do it.

## The trade-offs

- **It takes senior time.** Writing a role map competes with client work, and the benefit appears later.
- **Maps go stale.** Without regular updates, a map can mislead as much as it helps.
- **Steps are not understanding.** Written instructions can be followed without grasping why; conversation with experienced people still matters.
- **Some knowledge resists writing.** Judgement about clients and situations is learned alongside people, and the map should say so.

## Why it matters

First jobs shape how people see their own ability for years. A graduate who is given a map and a fair chance learns that they can master new work. One who is left to guess learns something much less useful. A few pages, written before the start date, can decide which lesson is learned.
