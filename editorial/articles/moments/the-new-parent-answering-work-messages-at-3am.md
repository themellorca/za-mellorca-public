The baby wakes at three. One parent feeds, rocks and watches the clock. The phone is on the bedside table because it is also the torch, the white-noise machine and the timer for the next feed. A work message lights the screen. It is not an emergency, but nobody has ever said it is not. Replying takes a minute. Not replying leaves a small worry that lasts longer than that.

This piece looks at the new parent who ends up answering work at night, and at how an employer can make the expectation explicit. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on fatigue and working conditions, not a finding about any particular South African workplace.

## How the night message becomes a habit

Nobody sets out to make a new parent work at three in the morning. The pattern forms from small, reasonable steps. A colleague sends a message at night because that is when they happen to be working. The parent happens to be awake and replies. The reply is quick and helpful, and the colleague notes that they are reliable. Next time the message arrives at the same hour, with a little more expectation.

The parent does not feel able to push back. Returning from leave, they may worry about being seen as less committed. They may be the main earner, or a new member of the team, or the only person who understands a particular system. The night reply becomes a small proof of dedication, offered at the price of sleep.

Three conditions make it more likely:

- **No stated reply window.** Without a rule that messages after a certain hour can wait, the safest assumption is that they cannot.
- **Visible activity.** Status indicators, read receipts and "last seen" times show that someone is awake, which invites a message.
- **Single points of knowledge.** If only one person can answer a question, that person is the one who gets messaged, whatever the hour.

## What the research says about broken sleep and work

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance on fatigue explains that tiredness reduces alertness and judgement, and that long or irregular hours and disturbed sleep contribute to it. New parents are, almost by definition, coping with disturbed sleep. Adding work to the night does not only cost them rest. It makes their decisions the next day less reliable, and in some jobs, such as driving, operating machinery or handling money, the consequences can be serious. Fatigue is a risk to safety as well as to comfort.

The World Health Organization's [fact sheet on mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) lists excessive workloads, inflexible or long hours and low control as working conditions that can harm wellbeing. A parent who cannot choose when a message will arrive, and who feels unable to ignore it, has exactly that low control, during a period when the rest of life has also become less predictable.

The Health and Safety Executive's guidance on [work-related stress](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/) points to demands, control and support as the factors that shape how work affects people. A new parent has demands from two directions, little control over when either arrives and, if the team has not discussed it, little support. South African employment law gives new parents leave and related benefits, and it is worth checking the current rules for the role. Those rules deal with time away, though, and not with the quieter pressures in the working hours around it, which is where an employer's own norms have to do the work.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Cape Town logistics firm with an operations coordinator who has returned from leave after the birth of her first child. The firm coordinates deliveries across time zones, and the team uses a group chat that never really sleeps.

Messages from a colleague in another time zone arrive between one and four in the morning, local time. She is awake with the baby anyway, so she answers. Within a month the team lead has begun to rely on this. When she is slow to reply once, he asks whether everything is all right. She starts to keep her phone within reach for the whole night, including the hours when the baby sleeps, and her own sleep fragments further. Her work is good. Her afternoons are a struggle.

## The human tax: presence, energy and wellbeing

The first cost is presence. The night hours with a newborn are demanding and also unrepeatable, and a parent who is half-focused on a screen is not fully there for the child, or fully asleep when the chance comes. Neither the work nor the family gets their whole attention.

The second cost is energy. Recovery from broken sleep depends on whatever consolidated rest the parent can get. Each night-time reply, and the worry that waits behind it, reduces that rest. The effect builds over weeks.

The third is wellbeing and safety. Chronic tiredness affects mood, patience and health, and it affects concentration at work and on the road. A team that asks for night-time replies from a new parent is borrowing against a person who has little to lend.

## What could realistically change

Most of the remedy is a matter of saying aloud what is currently unsaid. The team can agree a quiet window, for example no expectation of replies between nine at night and seven in the morning, with a separate, clear route for genuine emergencies. Messages can be scheduled to send at working hours. Knowledge held by one person can be written down so that someone else can answer. The piece on [the quiet cost of always being reachable](/moments/the-quiet-cost-of-always-being-reachable/) describes how the habit spreads across a team, and [when flexible work comes with unwritten after-hours rules](/moments/when-flexible-work-comes-with-unwritten-after-hours-rules/) explains why flexibility can turn into availability.

## Practical next actions

1. **Ask the new parent what would help.** Before assuming, ask whether they want a quiet window, flexible hours or a different arrangement, and respect the answer even if it differs from yours.
2. **Agree and publish a quiet window.** State the hours in which no reply is expected, and say that this applies to everyone, not only to people with young children.
3. **Define what counts as urgent.** Write down a short list of situations that justify a call at night, and the number to use. Everything else waits until morning.
4. **Schedule your own messages.** Use the delay-send feature in your email or chat tool so that messages written late arrive at the start of the working day.
5. **Write down the single-person knowledge.** Ask the parent to note the five questions only they can answer, and train a colleague to handle them, so that coverage does not depend on anyone being awake. The note on [when parental leave means handing over an undocumented job](/moments/when-parental-leave-means-handing-over-an-undocumented-job/) covers the handover that makes this easier.

## The business outcome

A team with explicit quiet hours loses little. Genuine emergencies still get through on the agreed route, and routine questions are answered a few hours later, in most cases without harm. In exchange, the business keeps an experienced colleague who is rested enough to do her job well, and shows other staff, present and future, that having a child does not mean an always-on phone. That is a retention argument as well as a decent one.

## The human dividend

The dividend is presence. A parent who knows that the phone can wait can be with the baby, or asleep, without a second set of obligations running in the background. For another person the same boundary might mean quiet for study, rest after a shift or time with a partner. The point is that the hours belong to the person, and they decide how to use them.

## The trade-offs

- **Boundaries can slow responses.** Some matters really do need a quick answer, and the emergency route has to be reliable enough for the team to trust it.
- **Colleagues cover more.** Moving work away from the parent moves it to others, so the load needs to be shared fairly and not simply pushed to the nearest person.
- **Some parents choose odd hours.** A person who prefers to work while the baby sleeps may value flexibility more than a quiet window, so the arrangement should be theirs to shape.
- **Tiny teams have no backup.** A business of three may have nobody to take an urgent matter, and will need clear priorities and a plan for the first months.

## Why it matters

The first months of a child's life are short and cannot be repeated. A workplace that treats them as ordinary working hours takes something from the parent that it can never give back. A workplace that protects the night, with a stated rule and a working emergency route, loses very little and shows that it understands what the person is carrying.
