On a new employee's third day, the colleague who was meant to show them around is off sick. The employee has a login, a desk and a folder of policies. What they do not have is an answer to the question they actually need answered: if the weekly report is due on Friday, does that mean Friday at noon or Friday at five, and does it go to the manager, or to the manager's assistant who then chases it anyway?

Every workplace has a layer of rules like that. They are not secret. They are simply unwritten, and they sit in a handful of heads. This piece looks at what it is like to arrive without them, and at what a business can do to make them findable. It is a hypothesis drawn from general workplace guidance, not a finding about any particular South African employer, and the example is illustrative.

## What counts as an unwritten rule

Formal documents cover the contract, the leave policy, the disciplinary code and the system manuals. The unwritten layer covers what makes work move in practice:

- **Who to ask.** Which person actually resolves a stock query, regardless of what the organogram says.
- **What is really due.** The difference between an official deadline and the time after which somebody starts to worry.
- **What is skippable.** Which steps in the procedure everyone completes, and which everyone quietly bypasses.
- **How to word things.** Which customers prefer a call to an email, and which manager wants only the summary.
- **What not to touch.** The spreadsheet that looks redundant but feeds a month-end report.

None of these rules is dramatic. Together they decide whether a new person feels competent or foolish in their first month.

## Why newcomers pay for the gap

People who have been in a team for years often cannot see these rules, because they stopped noticing them long ago. The rules feel like common sense. So the newcomer's questions can sound like a lack of ability, and the newcomer, wary of seeming slow, asks fewer of them. They guess, sometimes wrongly, and the wrong guesses are remembered.

HSE's management standard on role describes an organisation that provides information so that employees understand their role and responsibilities, and that makes sure the requirements placed on them are clear. Its standard on support describes an organisation that gives employees the information and support they need from colleagues and line management. When the rules are unwritten, both standards depend on one person's availability. If that person is away, there is no fallback.

The effect on access is easy to miss. People who arrive already connected to the team's social circle, through a former colleague, a shared school or simply being outgoing, pick up the rules faster. People who are shy, who work remotely, who are older or younger than the rest, or whose first language is not the language of the office, have less chance to overhear. The formal system looks equal. The informal one is not.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Bloemfontein logistics firm with a thirty-person office. A new dispatch coordinator starts on a Monday. The team lead who trained everyone for the past six years is attending a family funeral that week. The coordinator is given the procedures folder and told to ask anyone if stuck.

By Wednesday she has learned a few things from the folder and a good many more from mistakes. The procedures say that a delivery note must be signed on receipt, but nobody told her that drivers from one regular supplier never carry them and that a photograph on the shared phone counts. She books a courier on the system when the customer's account manager would have phoned the usual depot instead. She sends a status report to the whole team when only the lead was meant to see it. None of it is serious. All of it is noticed.

By Friday she has concluded that she is not good at the job, which is not true. She has simply been asked to do a job whose rules are stored in somebody who is not there.

## Making the rules findable

The fix is not a longer manual. A long manual becomes one more thing nobody reads. A better approach is to capture the small number of unwritten rules that cause the most trouble when they are missed, and to put them somewhere a newcomer can look without having to ask.

A practical way to start is to ask recent joiners. Within their first year, people remember exactly which things surprised them. Their answers are a more reliable guide than a manager's own recollection, because the manager no longer sees the gap. A short page titled "How things really work here" can hold the answers to who to ask, what is really due, what is skippable and what not to touch. It does not need to be polished. It needs an owner and a date.

A second part is a named backup. Whenever a team has a person who holds most of the knowledge, a second person should be briefed on what to say in their absence. HSE's guidance on control talks about giving people a say in how they do their work. A newcomer with a written orientation page and a named alternative has more control over the early weeks than one who must wait for a particular person to return.

It is also worth stating one boundary clearly. Some unwritten rules should not be written down, because they are unfair. If the real rule is that women are expected to organise the tea or that younger staff stay late, then writing it up is not the answer; changing it is. The exercise of listing the rules will make these visible, and the manager can decide which to keep, which to formalise and which to retire.

The written terms of employment still matter. South Africa's [Basic Conditions of Employment Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/basic-conditions-employment-act) sets out the particulars that an employer must give an employee in writing at the start of employment. Those particulars cover pay, hours and leave; they do not cover how the office works day to day, which is why a separate, simple orientation page is useful on top of them.

## The trade-offs

This takes time from the very people who are busiest. Some conventions are better learned by working alongside someone, and a written list cannot replace a conversation. A page of rules can go stale quickly if nobody owns it. And a team that writes everything down may start to treat the page as the whole truth, when the real work still needs judgement.

## Practical next actions

1. Ask the three most recent joiners what surprised them in their first month, and what they had to guess. Write down every answer without commenting.
2. Turn the answers into one page titled "How things really work here", covering who to ask, what is really due, what can be skipped and what not to touch.
3. Name a backup for each person who holds a lot of informal knowledge, and brief the backup on the top five things a newcomer would ask.
4. Add a thirty-minute check-in at the end of the first week with a different question each time: what is still unclear, and what would you like written down?
5. Review the page every quarter. Remove rules that are no longer true, and decide deliberately about any that are unfair.
