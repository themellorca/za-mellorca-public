At the end of a working day, people tend to divide what they did into two kinds, even if they never say so. There is the work that moved something along, and the work that made something. Moving a figure from one screen to another, forwarding a document, retyping an address: these are necessary, and they leave no trace of the person who did them. Spotting that a supplier has been charged twice, working out why a month's costs jumped, or finding a cleaner way to run a monthly process: these leave something behind that was not there before.

The difference rarely appears in a job description. Both kinds of work might be listed under "accounts" or "administration". But the second kind tends to be what people mean when they say a job feels worth doing, and its absence is often what they mean when they say it feels as though anyone could do it.

## Moving things and making things

Finance and administrative roles contain a great deal of transfer work. Information arrives in one form and has to be put into another: a PDF becomes a ledger entry, an email becomes a calendar booking, a bank line becomes a matched payment. The work needs care, and getting it wrong has consequences. But in the end the output is the same information in a different place.

Work that adds something new is different in kind. It changes what the business knows or how it works. A reconciliation turns up a pattern. A clerk notices that a particular customer always pays eleven days late, and suggests changing the reminder. A new starter writes down the steps nobody had recorded. In each case the person has contributed their judgement, and the business has something it did not have before.

Mellorca has written about adjacent territory in [the skilled professional reduced to copy and paste](/moments/the-skilled-professional-reduced-to-copy-and-paste/) and [the employee whose expertise is trapped in data entry](/moments/the-employee-whose-expertise-is-trapped-in-data-entry/). Those pieces describe skill going unused. This one is about a narrower question: whether the work leaves a mark that the person can point to.

## Why this is a question of dignity

Dignity at work is a large word and should be used carefully. In this context it means something modest: the sense that one's contribution matters, that it is recognised, and that one is treated as a person who can think and not only as a pair of hands.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on mental health at work lists under-use of skills among the psychosocial risks, alongside lack of control over job design, unclear roles and limited career development. The UK Health and Safety Executive's standard on role asks that people understand their role and responsibilities, and that demands are consistent and clearly stated. Its standard on control describes people being encouraged to apply their abilities and initiative, supported in building new capabilities, and given a say in how work is done. None of these sources talks about adding something new as such. Mellorca's inference is that a role made entirely of transfer work leaves little room for abilities and initiative to show, and that this may be part of what the research on under-used skills describes.

The word "inference" matters here. The research establishes that under-use of skills and low control are working conditions that can put wellbeing at risk. It does not establish that any particular finance office suffers from them, nor that the cure is a particular piece of software.

## An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative case, not a real one: an agricultural supply company in the Free State, with a finance team of three. The assistant accountant spends most of each month capturing supplier invoices, matching delivery notes and preparing payment batches. She is accurate and trusted. She also has ideas about the business that nobody asks for: she can tell which suppliers are habitually late with credit notes, and which branches order in ways that cost the company money on freight. These observations are real knowledge. But they are formed in the gaps between capture tasks and live in her head. There is no place in the month where she is expected to produce them, and so they stay private. When she looks back on a month, what she can point to is a number of invoices processed.

Suppose the capture and matching are made lighter, by a system that reads invoices into a draft entry for her to confirm, matches delivery notes to orders and flags the mismatches. Her work does not disappear. It changes. She reviews the flagged items, which require judgement. And, if the business chooses to ask, she can spend part of the month looking at supplier patterns and branch ordering, and produce something: a short note to the finance manager with two recommendations. That note is work that adds something new.

## What could realistically change

1. **Name the two kinds of work.** For each role, list what is transfer and what is contribution. Many offices find that the second list is shorter than anyone would like.
2. **Reduce the transfer work that a system can do reliably.** Microsoft's documentation describes cloud flows that run on events or schedules, and notes that predictable tasks can be automated once. The same documentation describes approvals that route a request to a named person and record the decision. These are descriptions of what tools can do, not promises of results.
3. **Create a place for contribution.** Freed time does not turn into insight unless there is somewhere for it to go. A monthly twenty-minute slot where the team brings one observation to the finance manager is often enough to start.
4. **Ask, and act on some of it.** Contribution that is ignored teaches people to stop offering. Mellorca's piece on [when your best work is invisible because it's repair work](/moments/when-your-best-work-is-invisible-because-its-repair-work/) shows how easily good work goes unnoticed.
5. **Recognise it in how the role is described.** If a role is only measured by volume processed, then volume is what people will optimise. Mellorca's impact material on [high-value employees doing low-value admin](/impact/high-value-employees-doing-low-value-admin/) sets out the cost to the business.

## What changes first, and the business outcome

The first change is that part of each month moves from entering and matching to reviewing and questioning. The employee has more reason to look closely at the data, because there is something to do with what they see.

The business outcome could be better supplier terms, earlier notice of cost drift, or small process improvements made by the people closest to the work. These are plausible gains that depend on the business actually using what the employee produces. Mellorca does not claim that every finance team has such insights waiting to be released.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time, in the specific sense of time returned for the part of the job that uses a person's judgement, and with it optionality: the possibility of growing the role instead of leaving it to find one. A person who has started to produce analysis and recommendations has a different story to tell about their own work, inside the company and outside it. That is a real kind of choice, even if it is modest.

It is also worth saying what the dividend is not. It is not a requirement that everyone want a bigger role. Some people prefer steady, well-defined work, and take pride in doing it accurately. Dignity does not belong only to those who analyse. It belongs equally to the person who is trusted to do careful work and is not blamed for the failures of the system around them.

## The trade-offs

Moving routine to systems can reduce the work that some people find restful or reliable. A task that fills a quiet hour and demands little can be a welcome rhythm in a stressful week.

Asking for contribution adds expectation. An employee who was paid to process invoices may not have agreed to produce recommendations, and a new expectation without a new conversation about pay and workload can feel like more work for the same reward.

Contribution can be unevenly recognised. If one person's insights are noticed and another's are not, the result can be resentment rather than dignity. Fairness in who is asked and credited matters.

The systems also need care. Automated capture can make mistakes that nobody sees if everyone assumes the system is right. Someone has to own checking, and that checking is itself transfer work of a kind.

## Why it matters

When people describe a good working life, they often describe leaving something behind: a problem solved, a process improved, a thing that exists because they were there. Mellorca's reading, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dignity of work lies partly in this, and that many finance and administrative roles contain more of it than their daily routine allows to show. Where systems can carry the transfer work, the useful question is not only how many hours were saved. It is what the person now has room to add.
