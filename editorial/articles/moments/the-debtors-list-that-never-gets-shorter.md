Every month, the debtors' report comes out, and every month the bottom of it looks the same. Ninety days. A hundred and twenty days. Over a year. Some of the names belong to customers who still buy. Some belong to businesses that may no longer exist. A few belong to people the owner knows personally, which makes them the hardest of all. The owner reads the list, feels a familiar weight, and closes the file.

This piece is about that stubborn tail of old debt: why it stops moving, what it costs, and how a simple routine can work through it. It is a hypothesis drawn from accounting-software documentation and South African court guidance, not a measurement of how much small businesses are owed.

## Why the list stops moving

Fresh overdue invoices usually get chased. Old ones often do not, for reasons that are human more than financial:

- **The dispute was never resolved.** The customer queried part of the invoice, nobody followed up, and the whole amount sits in limbo.
- **The relationship is awkward.** The debtor is a long-standing customer, a friend or a family connection, and chasing feels like a betrayal.
- **Nobody owns it.** The person who raised the invoice has left, or the bookkeeper assumes the owner is handling it.
- **The next step is unclear.** After a few reminders, the owner does not know what to do, so does nothing.

Each month without action makes the next step harder, because memories fade, contacts change and the debt starts to feel like a permanent fixture.

## An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a plumbing business in Pietermaritzburg with nine staff, doing a mix of household call-outs and work for small property managers. Its debtors' report shows about thirty accounts over ninety days. Some are small household jobs where the customer moved house. Two are property managers who dispute call-out fees. One is a body corporate whose treasurer changed and whose new treasurer has never seen the invoices.

The owner spends a few anxious evenings a year scrolling through the list and promising himself he will deal with it. He never has a clear afternoon to do so, and each time it feels bigger.

## The human tax: financial resilience and energy

The financial tax is straightforward. Money that is owed but not collected is money the business has to find elsewhere, from an overdraft, a delayed supplier payment or the owner's own pocket. The story of [when late invoices become late salaries](/moments/when-late-invoices-become-late-salaries/) follows that chain to payroll, and [the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake](/moments/the-cash-flow-week-that-keeps-the-owner-awake/) describes how it feels.

The energy tax is less obvious. An unresolved list sits in the back of the mind. It turns every look at the accounts into a reminder of something undone, and it makes the owner less willing to look at the accounts at all. That avoidance is how small problems become large ones.

## A routine instead of dread

The way out is to replace occasional bursts of guilt with a steady, boring routine. Accounting software helps. Microsoft's Business Central documentation on [collecting outstanding balances](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/business-central/receivables-collect-outstanding-balances) describes sending customer statements, issuing reminders according to reminder terms with levels that set when each reminder goes out, applying finance charges where agreed, and automating the creation and sending of reminders. Most small-business accounting packages offer similar features.

Software handles the routine accounts. The stale tail needs decisions. A practical approach is to sort every old account into one of four groups, and to act on each group in turn:

- **Collectable and undisputed:** send a statement and a firm, polite reminder, then phone.
- **Disputed:** resolve the dispute, even if that means crediting part of the amount, so the rest can be collected.
- **Unresponsive:** move to a formal letter of demand and, if needed, legal steps.
- **Uncollectable:** write off, with your accountant's advice, so the list reflects reality.

For smaller claims, the South African [Small Claims Court](https://www.justice.gov.za/scc/scc.htm) offers a route without a lawyer. The Department of Justice explains that claims up to R30 000 can be brought there, and that the process starts with trying to settle, followed by a letter of demand giving the other party fourteen days. Check with the clerk of your local Magistrate's Court about eligibility, because the rules about who may bring a claim matter for businesses.

## Practical next actions

1. **Book an hour a week.** Put a fixed, recurring hour in the diary for the debtors' list, and keep it.
2. **Sort the old accounts.** Place every account over ninety days into one of the four groups above.
3. **Resolve one dispute per week.** Pick the oldest dispute and settle it, even partially, rather than leaving it open.
4. **Send letters of demand.** For unresponsive debtors, send a formal letter of demand by a method you can prove.
5. **Write off what is gone.** Ask your accountant to help you write off uncollectable debts so your figures are honest.
6. **Automate the new.** Switch on automatic statements and reminders so new invoices do not join the stale tail.

## The business outcome

Working through old debt improves cash, sometimes noticeably, and gives a more honest picture of what the business is owed. Resolving disputes improves relationships with customers who were annoyed but silent. And a clean list makes it easier to spot genuinely new problems early. The related story [the dignity of not having to chase](/moments/the-dignity-of-not-having-to-chase/) considers the human value of systems that collect without constant pursuit.

## The human dividend

The dividend is financial breathing room, and with it some peace of mind. The owner opens the monthly report and sees a shorter list, with each remaining account in a known state. The sinking feeling becomes an ordinary task with a time slot. That change is quiet, but it gives back attention that had been leaking away for months.

## The trade-offs

- **Relationships can suffer.** Firm collection may cost a customer or two, and some owners will decide that a relationship is worth more than the debt.
- **Legal routes take effort.** Letters of demand and court processes require time, paperwork and patience.
- **Some money is gone.** Writing off debt is painful, but pretending otherwise distorts decisions.
- **Automation can feel cold.** Long-standing customers may prefer a personal call to an automated reminder.

## Why it matters

The debtors' list is a record of work already done and not yet paid for. Leaving it to grow is a way of working for free and carrying the worry as well. A weekly hour and a clear set of next steps turn that list from a source of dread into a task that ends.
