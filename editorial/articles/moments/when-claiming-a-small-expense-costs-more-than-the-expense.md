A taxi fare of ninety rand, a parking ticket, a box of printer cartridges bought on the way to the office. Small purchases like these are made every week in almost every business. Getting the money back for them is where the trouble starts. The claim form has to be found, the slip photographed or taped, the cost centre looked up, the manager chased, and finance asked why the amount does not match the receipt.

This piece looks at why the effort of claiming a small expense often exceeds the expense, who pays that cost, and how a business can lower it without loosening control. It is a hypothesis drawn from general guidance on administration and tax record keeping, not a measurement of any particular South African employer.

## Where the time actually goes

The money in a small claim is rarely the problem. The time is. A single claim passes through several hands, and each hand adds a step that made sense on its own.

- **The claimant** collects the slip, finds the form, works out the category and writes a description that finance will accept.
- **The approver** interrupts their own work to check a claim they cannot easily verify, usually by trusting the claimant.
- **Finance** checks the slip against the policy, queries anything unclear, and keys the figures into the accounting package.
- **Everyone** waits. The claimant waits for repayment, often into the following month, while the approver and finance carry the claim in their heads.

A claim of a few hundred rand can consume more than an hour of combined effort. The business pays that hour in salary. The claimant pays it in attention and sometimes in cash, because they have funded the purchase from their own account until the money returns.

## Why the process drifts toward heaviness

Expense processes grow heavy for sound reasons. One bad claim leads to a new field. A query from an auditor leads to a new approval. A change in tax rules leads to a new category. Each addition protects the business from a real risk, and none is ever removed.

The tax side adds its own weight. South African businesses registered for VAT work under rules set out by the South African Revenue Service, which publishes guidance on the [obligations of a VAT vendor](https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/value-added-tax/), including the registration thresholds and the standard rate of fifteen per cent. Whether an input claim can be made on a given purchase depends on the documents that support it, so finance has good reason to insist on proper slips. The sensible response is not to relax that requirement but to make the capture of the slip easy at the moment of purchase. A business should confirm the current documentary requirements with SARS or a tax practitioner before it redesigns the process.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Cape Town consultancy of twenty-two people. A junior consultant buys a day parking ticket and a sandwich for a client meeting, a total of about two hundred rand. The claim form is a spreadsheet kept in a shared folder. She photographs the slips, but the file name rules are unclear, so the finance clerk asks her to resend them. Her manager approves on a Friday afternoon, after the month-end cut-off, so the money arrives six weeks later.

She does not complain. She simply stops claiming small amounts. The consultancy saves two hundred rand and loses the visibility of what its client work costs. Meanwhile the junior learns, quietly, that the business expects her to subsidise it.

## The human tax: time and energy

The World Health Organization's [fact sheet on mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) counts heavy workloads and low control among the conditions that can harm wellbeing. A pile of small administrative tasks that cannot be finished in one sitting is a mild version of both.

The cost to people is mostly time and energy. Time goes on the claim itself and on the follow-up. Energy goes on remembering. A claim that cannot be made on the spot becomes a small unfinished task, which the note on [the attention spent remembering what systems should remember](/moments/the-attention-spent-remembering-what-systems-should-remember/) describes as a drain that adds up across many such tasks.

There is a fairness cost too. People on lower salaries feel a delayed repayment more sharply than people on higher ones, and people who travel often carry more of the float. When claims are slow, the burden falls unevenly, and nobody has chosen that outcome.

Approvers pay as well. A manager who signs thirty small claims a month is spending attention on low-value decisions. The article on [the lunch break eaten by approval requests](/moments/the-lunch-break-eaten-by-approval-requests/) shows how such interruptions spread into the rest of the day.

## What could realistically change

The aim is to keep control proportionate to risk. Three ideas recur in practice.

First, capture at the source. If the slip is photographed and attached to the claim at the moment of purchase, finance no longer chases it. Many accounting and expense tools allow this from a phone.

Second, tier the approval. A small claim within a stated limit, in a stated category, by a person with a clear role can be approved by a simple rule or by a periodic review, while larger or unusual claims go to a manager. Microsoft's documentation on [approvals in Power Automate](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-automate/get-started-approvals) shows the kind of routed sign-off that software can handle, with the request, the decision and the record kept together. The same idea can be run on paper or email if the business has no suitable tool.

Third, pay on a fixed rhythm. A weekly or fortnightly repayment run, announced in advance, is easier to trust than a variable wait. For businesses that handle many small purchases, a company card for routine buys removes the claim altogether, though it brings its own controls and reconciliation. The article on [the evening spent chasing signatures](/moments/the-evening-spent-chasing-signatures/) and the piece on [when ordering supplies takes five emails](/moments/when-ordering-supplies-takes-five-emails/) describe the same pattern in neighbouring processes.

## Practical next actions

1. **Time ten recent claims.** For each, note the minutes the claimant, approver and finance spent, and the days between purchase and repayment. Compare the total with the value of the claims.
2. **Set a small-claim threshold.** Decide an amount and a list of categories under which claims are paid with a light check, and review a sample each month instead of every item.
3. **Make capture instant.** Choose one way to photograph and attach a slip at the point of purchase, and write the file naming rule on a single page.
4. **Announce a repayment rhythm.** Publish the day claims are paid, and keep to it. Predictable repayment matters more to most claimants than speed.
5. **Confirm the tax documents.** Ask your tax practitioner or check SARS guidance to confirm which documents must be kept for each claim type, so that the lighter process still meets the requirement.

## The business outcome

A lighter process lowers the hidden salary cost of administration, shortens the month-end rush and improves the quality of expense data. Finance spends less time chasing and more time reviewing the claims that matter. Staff are more likely to claim what they are owed, which means the business sees its true costs. Control improves when it is concentrated where the risk is, instead of spread thinly across every rand.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time. A claim that takes two minutes at the point of purchase, and repays on a known day, gives back the evenings and attention that the old process absorbed. Nobody needs to remember anything, and nobody needs to fund the business from their own pocket for six weeks. How people use the recovered time is for them to decide.

## The trade-offs

- **Lighter checks carry risk.** A higher threshold lets a few errors or abuses through, so the business should sample and audit instead of ignoring.
- **Tools cost money.** An expense application has a licence fee and a setup effort, which is hard to justify for a very small team.
- **Company cards need discipline.** They remove claims but invite reconciliation work and the risk of personal use.
- **Tax rules change.** A process built around today's documentary requirements needs review when the rules move.

## Why it matters

An expense claim is a small test of whether a business trusts its people and values their time. When claiming costs more than the expense, people notice, and they adjust their behaviour in ways the business does not intend. A proportionate process says that the time of the claimant, the approver and finance is worth protecting, and that control can be kept without being heavy.
