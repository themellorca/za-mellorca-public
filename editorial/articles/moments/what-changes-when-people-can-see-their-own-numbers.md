In many businesses, the numbers that describe a team's work are owned by somebody else. The finance person compiles them. The manager reads them. They appear in a monthly pack, discussed in a meeting the team is not in, and the verdict is passed down: sales were low, returns were high, the turnaround time slipped. By the time the people who could have changed the outcome hear about it, it is history.

This piece looks at what changes when that arrangement flips and the people doing the work can see their own figures, in good time, in a form they trust. It is a hypothesis about a common pattern, not a claim that visibility fixes everything.

## The gap between doing and knowing

A team packing orders knows roughly how it is going. It can feel when the day is slow. What it cannot feel is whether it is a tenth below the usual rate, or whether the returns this week are twice last week's. Those facts are in a system somewhere, but they are not in front of the people who could do something about them.

Descriptions of the business dashboard make the intent clear: it is a way of presenting key information in one place so that those looking at it can understand status at a glance. A key performance indicator, similarly, is a measure chosen to show how well an objective is being achieved. Neither concept says the viewer must be a senior manager. Where the viewer is the person whose work the measure describes, the loop between action and result becomes short enough to learn from.

The article on [the difference between being informed and being in control](/moments/the-difference-between-being-informed-and-being-in-control/) draws the useful distinction: being told how things went is not the same as being able to influence them. Giving people their own figures is a step from the first towards the second.

## Why timing and ownership matter

Self-determination theory, a well-known framework in psychology, proposes that people do best when three needs are met: competence, relatedness and autonomy. Seeing the results of one's own work supports competence, because feedback shows whether what you do is working. Having a say in how to respond supports autonomy. A figure delivered a month late, through someone else, offers neither.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance on control reaches a similar practical point from a different direction. It treats having a say in how work is done as a key factor in wellbeing, and it lists encouraging people to use their own judgement and giving them influence over their work as part of good practice. Information is a precondition: it is hard to exercise judgement about something you cannot see.

Neither source tests this in small South African firms, and this article does not claim they do. The argument is that the principle is well supported and the application is a matter of practical design.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a regional distributor of pet food in Gqeberha, with a warehouse team of twelve and a supervisor. Each month the finance manager produces a report with pick accuracy, dispatch times and returns. The supervisor sees it ten days after month-end. The pickers never see it.

In March the report shows that returns for mis-picked items doubled. The supervisor calls a meeting and the team says that the problem started after a shelf layout change in the second week. If the team had seen the weekly figures, someone would have spotted the jump early. Instead, three weeks of avoidable errors piled up and a few customers were lost.

The distributor puts a simple weekly view on a screen in the warehouse and shares it with the team: picks per day, mis-picks, returns, orders dispatched on time. Nothing fancy, no individual names, the same definitions finance uses. Within a month the team starts proposing small changes, such as moving fast-moving lines closer to the packing bench, and checks whether the numbers move.

## The human tax: autonomy

The tax of the old arrangement is mainly autonomy. People are accountable for results they cannot see, which is a position of responsibility without information. They cannot adjust early, so they are judged on outcomes that were fixed before the verdict arrived. Reports travel up and decisions travel down, and the people in between often feel they are being managed through a rear-view mirror.

It is also a quiet cost in trust. If management has the figures and the team does not, the team can reasonably wonder what is being done with them. The [cost of managers chasing status updates](/impact/managers-chasing-status-updates-visibility-cost/) is the same problem seen from the other side: the manager lacks visibility too, so asks people to report what a shared figure could simply show.

## What could realistically change

Giving people their numbers is not a software purchase. It is a set of decisions about what to show, to whom and how often.

1. **Choose a small set of figures.** Three to five measures that the team can influence directly. Measures the team cannot affect create frustration rather than action.
2. **Agree the definitions.** What counts as a late dispatch? What counts as a return? Disagreement about meaning is one of the commonest causes of distrust, as in [when nobody can say which number is true](/moments/when-nobody-can-say-which-number-is-true/). Write the definitions beside the figures.
3. **Source them once.** The figures should come from the same place finance and management use, not a parallel spreadsheet. Guidance on [deciding what should be on an operations dashboard](/insights/how-to-decide-what-should-be-on-an-operations-dashboard/) covers how to choose what belongs and how to keep it honest.
4. **Refresh them at the pace of the work.** A daily operation needs daily figures. A monthly figure for a daily job is a history lesson.
5. **Use them for conversation, not blame.** Review the figures with the team, ask what they think explains a change, and let them propose responses. Using the numbers to discipline individuals will teach people to hide them.

## The business outcome

Problems are caught earlier and by the people best placed to fix them. Managers spend less time compiling and chasing, and more time on the issues the team cannot solve alone. Disputes over whose numbers are right decrease because there is one set. Forecasting becomes steadier because small deviations are corrected before they accumulate.

## The human dividend

The dividend is autonomy and predictability. Autonomy because people can act on what they see without waiting for permission or a verdict. Predictability because there are fewer surprises: when the figures are in front of everyone, nobody learns at month-end that a quarter went badly. The result is a more settled working week, with less anxiety about what the next report will say.

## The trade-offs

- **Visibility can feel like surveillance.** Showing team-level figures is different from tracking individuals. The latter needs care, consultation and, where personal information is involved, POPIA-compliant handling.
- **Some figures should stay restricted.** Salaries, margins and customer-specific terms are not for every screen.
- **Dashboards decay.** A view that is not maintained slowly drifts from the truth, and a wrong figure on a wall is worse than none.
- **More visibility prompts more questions.** Managers must be ready to answer them rather than treat them as challenges.

## Why it matters

People are better at changing their work when they can see its effects. When the numbers belong to the team as well as to management, the team can steer instead of waiting to be told how the journey went.
