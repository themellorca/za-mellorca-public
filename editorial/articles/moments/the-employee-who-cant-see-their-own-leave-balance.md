An employee wants to book a week away in December. Before she does, she wants to know whether she has the days. She emails HR. A reply comes two days later with a number. A colleague, asked the same question, was given a different number last month. She books the week anyway and hopes that the figure holds.

This piece looks at a small but common situation: an employee who cannot see their own leave balance, and the way that missing information shapes planning, trust and the relationship with the employer. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on fairness and transparency at work, not a finding about any particular South African employer.

## Why the balance is so often hidden

In many small and mid-sized businesses, leave is tracked in a spreadsheet kept by one person, or on paper forms filed after approval. The balance is calculated on request. There is nothing malicious about this. The system grew up when the business was ten people and the person who kept the spreadsheet knew everyone.

As the business grows, the arrangement stops scaling. Leave accrues at different rates depending on length of service, contract type and agreed carry-over. Sick leave and family responsibility leave follow their own rules. South African employers also work within the Basic Conditions of Employment Act, which sets minimum leave entitlements, and many add their own policies on top. A balance that was simple to work out for ten people becomes a calculation with several moving parts, and every answer depends on a person with a spreadsheet being available.

## What it costs the person asking

The immediate cost is planning. Without a reliable balance, employees cannot book travel, plan family events or schedule medical procedures with confidence. They may hold back from taking leave they are entitled to, out of caution, or take it and later discover a discrepancy.

The deeper cost is to trust. Research on [organisational justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organizational_justice) separates fairness into several kinds. One concerns outcomes, whether people receive what they are due. Another concerns procedures, whether the way decisions are made is consistent and open. A third concerns information, whether people are told what they need to know. A hidden leave balance touches all three. The employee cannot check that the outcome is right, cannot see how it was worked out, and has to ask for information that, in principle, is theirs.

The idea of [procedural justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_justice) adds that people judge fairness not only by results but by whether the process feels consistent and respectful. Having to ask permission to know your own entitlement fails that test, even when the number turns out to be correct.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Port Elizabeth logistics company with sixty employees and a part-time HR administrator. Leave is recorded on a shared spreadsheet that only she and the finance manager can open. Employees submit paper requests, which she logs once approved.

In November, three drivers each discover that their balances differ from what they believed. One is told he has four days fewer than he counted, because a public holiday that fell during an earlier leave period had been counted twice. Another learns that carried-over days expire at the end of the year, a rule in the policy that he had never been shown. Neither case involves bad faith. Both leave the employee feeling that the company's records, not his own, decide his time off.

The HR administrator, meanwhile, spends close to a day each week answering balance queries, and in December, the busiest month, the queries double. She dreads the emails as much as the employees dread sending them.

## The human tax: confidence

The cost is confidence: the assurance that one can plan one's own time and rely on the figures. When it is missing, the employee becomes slightly dependent on someone else for something that should be under their control. They may be reluctant to ask again, afraid of looking as though they are checking up on the company, or of seeming to want too much time off.

The cost also falls on managers, who approve leave without seeing the balance, and on colleagues, who cover without knowing whether the absence is within entitlement. The related piece on [why leave requests turn into coverage puzzles](/moments/why-leave-requests-turn-into-coverage-puzzles/) explains how missing information makes every request harder to approve.

## What could realistically change

The fix is not complicated. It is to put the same figures the HR person already uses in front of the employee.

1. **Write down the rules.** Set out in plain language how leave accrues, what carries over, when it expires, and how sick and family responsibility leave are treated. A one-page summary that every employee has seen beats a policy no one reads. Keep a record that each employee received it, as discussed in [why policy acknowledgements need a reliable system record](/discovery/why-policy-acknowledgements-need-a-reliable-system-record/).
2. **Give every employee a view of their own balance.** This can be a self-service page in an HR or payroll system, or, at a minimum, a monthly statement sent to each person. The article on [why self-service can feel like respect](/moments/why-self-service-can-feel-like-respect/) explains why this matters beyond saving HR time.
3. **Show how it is calculated.** A balance that lists accrued days, days taken, days pending and days that will expire is easier to trust than a single number.
4. **Let requests and approvals live in one place.** When a request, its approval and the balance sit together, errors such as double-counted holidays are less likely and easier to trace.
5. **Offer a route to query.** Even with a clear balance, mistakes happen. Make it easy to raise a query and promise a reply within a set time.

The same principle applies elsewhere in the business. When people can see the numbers that affect them, disputes drop. The piece on [what changes when people can see their own numbers](/moments/what-changes-when-people-can-see-their-own-numbers/) and the note on [why fair rosters need transparent systems](/moments/why-fair-rosters-need-transparent-systems/) trace the same pattern in other settings.

## The business outcome

HR time spent answering balance queries drops. Leave gets planned earlier and spread better across the year, which makes coverage easier. Disputes become rarer and quicker to settle, because both sides are looking at the same figure. In a country where employment records matter if a dispute reaches the CCMA or a labour court, an accurate and accessible record also protects the employer. Handling employee records carefully also fits the obligations that the Protection of Personal Information Act places on employers as responsible parties.

## The human dividend

The dividend is autonomy and predictability. Autonomy because the employee can decide when to take leave without asking permission to know what they have. Predictability because the figure on the screen is the figure that will be honoured. For the person planning a December trip, it means booking with confidence. For the one who had been quietly worried about the days they might have lost, it is the relief of seeing the number and knowing the rule.

## The trade-offs

- **Set-up effort.** Moving from a spreadsheet to a self-service system takes time and money, and existing records may need cleaning first.
- **Transparency exposes errors.** Once balances are visible, past mistakes will come to light, and the business must be ready to correct them fairly.
- **Not every employee has easy access.** Staff without a work email or smartphone, such as some factory or driving roles, need a printed statement or a supervisor-supported option.
- **Rules still apply.** A visible balance does not guarantee approval of every request. Operational limits remain.

## Why it matters

A leave balance is a small piece of information with large effects on trust. When employees can see and understand their own entitlement, they plan better, ask less often and assume less bad faith. When they cannot, a minor administrative gap becomes a steady reminder that the company's records matter more than their own knowledge. Making the figure visible is a cheap way to say otherwise.
