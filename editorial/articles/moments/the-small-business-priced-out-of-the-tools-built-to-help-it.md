A five-person business knows exactly which software would help. It would stop the double-capturing of invoices, tidy up stock, and give the owner one view of who owes what. The product exists, it is well reviewed, and the pricing page makes the decision for them. The plan that includes the feature they need is priced per user, billed in dollars, and starts at a level that would take a noticeable bite out of a month's margin. They go back to the spreadsheet.

This piece looks at that experience: how pricing structures can leave small businesses outside the tools that are meant to help them, what that costs in financial breathing room, and what an owner can sensibly do about it. It is a hypothesis drawn from how software is generally priced and sold, not a finding about any particular South African business or product.

## How a good tool ends up out of reach

Most business software is now sold as a subscription, where the customer pays a recurring fee for access instead of buying a licence outright. Software as a service, as the model is commonly described, offers real advantages: lower upfront cost, automatic updates and no servers to maintain. It also moves the cost from a one-off purchase to an ongoing commitment, which changes how a small business has to plan.

Several features of typical pricing can work against small firms.

- **Tiering that hides the needed feature.** The capability that matters, such as multi-currency invoicing or an approval step, often sits in a higher plan than the entry level.
- **Per-user fees.** A business with five people, each needing occasional access, pays as if all five were heavy users.
- **Foreign-currency billing.** A price quoted in dollars or euros moves with the Rand, so a cost that looked manageable in January can look different by June.
- **Minimum commitments.** Annual contracts or minimum seat counts suit larger buyers who can predict their needs.

The subscription model is widely used across industries, and the general reference on it notes the trade-off between predictable recurring revenue for the seller and a continuing obligation for the buyer. For a business whose own income arrives unevenly, that obligation is a different kind of risk from a one-off purchase.

## What it costs a small business

The visible cost is the fee. The hidden cost is the opportunity cost of not having the tool: the hours spent on manual work, the errors that creep in, and the decisions made with partial information. Economists define opportunity cost as the value of the best alternative given up, and for a small business that alternative is often an owner's evening spent reconciling by hand.

In South Africa, small businesses also sit within a particular tax and compliance environment. SARS publishes guidance for small business taxpayers covering registration, returns and record-keeping, and good records are the foundation for all of it. A business that cannot afford the tools to keep tidy records may find compliance harder, which in turn makes it harder to access finance or tenders that require clean paperwork.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Port Elizabeth bakery and catering business with six staff. The owner wants software that links orders, stock and invoices. The best fit she finds charges per user per month, in dollars, with the order-management feature only on the middle tier. At the current exchange rate the total would be a material fraction of what she takes home in a slow month.

She decides to wait. Over the next year she continues to take orders by WhatsApp, copies them into a spreadsheet and invoices on Fridays. Twice a customer is invoiced the wrong amount, and once an order is missed entirely. A larger competitor in the same town uses an integrated system and quotes faster. Her margin shrinks, and the cost of the missing tool grows each month she avoids paying for it.

## The human tax: access and financial resilience

The first cost is access. Small businesses, which often have the most to gain from tools that remove admin, are the least able to buy them at the price the market sets for its typical customer. The second is financial resilience. When a business is reluctant to take on any recurring cost, it carries more manual work and more risk, and has less cushion when something goes wrong.

There is a human side to this too. The owner who is priced out of a tool does not stop needing it. The work moves to her evenings and weekends, and sometimes to a family member who is helping out. The note on [the Saturday morning spent on invoices](/moments/the-saturday-morning-spent-on-invoices/) describes how that unpaid labour accumulates.

## What could realistically change

An owner cannot change a vendor's pricing page, but there is more room to manoeuvre than it first appears.

1. **Price the problem before the product.** Estimate how many hours a month the manual work consumes, what an error costs, and what a late invoice costs the cash-flow position. The piece on [why knowing your margins changes what you can choose](/moments/why-knowing-your-margins-changes-what-you-can-choose/) explains why this step makes the decision calmer.
2. **Check what you already pay for.** Existing subscriptions often include features that nobody switched on. [The subscription bills nobody reviews](/moments/the-subscription-bills-nobody-reviews/) is a useful starting point before adding anything new.
3. **Ask for what you need, not the whole bundle.** Some vendors will offer a lower tier, a reduced seat count or a local-currency price if asked. The answer may be no, but asking costs nothing.
4. **Consider a narrower tool.** A simple product that does one job well, such as invoicing alone, may cover most of the need at a fraction of the price of a full platform.
5. **Stage the move.** Start with the process that costs the most time or causes the most errors, and add others only when the first has paid for itself.
6. **Plan for the exchange rate.** If a tool is billed in a foreign currency, add a buffer to the budget so that a weaker Rand does not turn a manageable cost into a squeeze.

## The business outcome

A business that prices the cost of not having a tool alongside the cost of having one makes better decisions in both directions. It avoids tools it cannot sustain, adopts the ones that pay for themselves and keeps cash in hand for the weeks that need it. The related piece on [the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake](/moments/the-cash-flow-week-that-keeps-the-owner-awake/) shows why even a modest recurring cost needs to be seen against the shape of the month.

## The human dividend

The dividend is financial breathing room: the ability to adopt a useful tool without putting the month's wages at risk, or to decline one with a clear head because the numbers say it does not yet make sense. Either way, the owner is choosing from knowledge, not locked out by a price built for someone else. That is a modest form of freedom, and it will matter differently to different owners.

## The trade-offs

- **Cheaper tools may do less.** A narrow product can leave gaps that later require another purchase or a migration.
- **Waiting has its own price.** Delaying adoption keeps manual work and its error risk in place.
- **Negotiation is uncertain.** Smaller buyers have less leverage, and some vendors will not move.
- **Switching later costs effort.** Data and habits built around one tool can be hard to move if the business outgrows it.

## Why it matters

When software pricing assumes a customer with a finance department, small businesses carry the difference in unpaid hours and avoidable errors. Treating the purchase as a calculation instead of a verdict gives owners a way to decide, and a way to keep some breathing room while they do.
