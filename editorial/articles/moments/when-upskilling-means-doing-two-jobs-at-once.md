A business tells a capable employee to get better at something. Learn the new accounting package, take the project-management course, pick up basic data analysis. The intention is good and the employee is keen. Then Monday arrives and the original job is exactly the size it was on Friday. Nothing was taken off the plate to make room for the learning, so the learning happens at the kitchen table at nine at night.

This piece looks at that arrangement: upskilling as a second job. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on workload and fatigue, not a finding about any particular South African employer.

## How learning turns into a second shift

Most upskilling requests are made in good faith and without a plan for the hours. The manager sees the benefit: a more capable team member, a skill the business lacks. The employee sees the benefit as well, because new skills are what raise earning power and widen choices. What neither of them sees is the arithmetic. A four-hour module, an assessment and a practice project do not fit into the gaps of a week that is already full.

The gaps do not exist, so the hours come from somewhere else. They come from the evening, the weekend, the lunch break and the sleep that follows a late night with a textbook. Three patterns tend to sit behind this:

- **Learning is treated as a perk.** Because it benefits the employee, it is assumed to belong to their own time.
- **Output targets do not move.** The monthly numbers stay the same, so any hour spent learning at work looks like an hour stolen from the targets.
- **Nobody names the hours.** Without a stated allowance, each person guesses what is acceptable, and the conscientious guess low.

## What the research says about load and hours

The World Health Organization's [fact sheet on mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) lists heavy workloads, long or inflexible hours and low control among the working conditions that can harm wellbeing. Learning on top of a full job combines all three: more total work, a longer effective day and little say over when the learning has to be done.

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance on fatigue adds a practical point. Tiredness reduces alertness and judgement, and long or irregular hours are among its main causes. A person studying until late and then performing a full day is also, in effect, making their day-job errors more likely. The business pays for the learning twice: once in the course fee and again in the slips that tired people make.

In South Africa, the Skills Development Act is the national framework for improving workplace skills, and it exists because the country treats skills as an economic priority. Learnerships, skills programmes and training levies sit in that framework. The Act does not say how many hours a given employee should be released, and it is not a substitute for a conversation about the workload. Working-time rules, including those for overtime and rest, sit in the Basic Conditions of Employment Act, so a business that expects study to happen after hours as unrecorded extra work should check how the rules apply to the role.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Pretoria bookkeeping practice with six staff. The owner asks a senior bookkeeper to become competent in management reporting, so that the practice can offer monthly packs to clients. The bookkeeper agrees at once, enrols in an online course, and keeps her full client list of twenty-two monthly files.

For two months she works her normal hours, then studies from eight to eleven on weeknights and most of Saturday morning. Her month-end work stays accurate, but she stops taking lunch away from her desk and stops answering the family group chat. In the third month a client file is filed late. The owner is surprised, because nothing about the bookkeeper's work had changed on paper. What had changed was that she was running two jobs, and only one of them was on the schedule.

## The human tax: time and energy

The first cost is time. Evenings and weekends are where people recover, see family, exercise, run errands and do the many small jobs that keep a household going. Learning that occupies those hours does not free time later in any simple way. It borrows it.

The second cost is energy. Learning is demanding work in itself, and it is harder when done at the tail of a long day. People retain less, take longer and feel worse about their progress, which can lead to the false conclusion that they are not suited to the new skill, when the real problem was the hour.

There is a third, quieter cost. When upskilling is always done in private time, the people who can afford to give it are those without caring duties, long commutes or second jobs. The business then draws its future capability from a narrower slice of its people than it intended.

## What could realistically change

The aim is not to make learning free of effort. It is to count the hours honestly and to take something off the plate while the learning happens. Practical options include a fixed weekly learning block that is protected in the diary, a temporary reduction in the person's targets, moving a recurring task to a colleague for a quarter, or pausing a low-value report. The note on [the employee who never has time to learn](/moments/the-employee-who-never-has-time-to-learn/) describes how the absence of such a block shows up across a team, and [why documented processes help people grow into roles](/moments/why-documented-processes-help-people-grow-into-roles/) shows how written procedures make it easier to hand work over while someone learns.

## Practical next actions

1. **Estimate the real hours.** Before agreeing to a course, ask the provider for the expected study time and add practice and assessment. Write the total next to the course name.
2. **Choose what comes off.** Agree one task, target or report that is reduced or reassigned for the duration of the learning. If nothing can come off, treat that as information about whether the timing is right.
3. **Put the learning block in the diary.** Fix a recurring slot of, say, two hours a week, marked as busy, and tell the team that it is protected.
4. **Agree a use for the skill.** Name the first real task where the new skill will be applied, with a date, so that the learning has a destination and does not drift.
5. **Review after six weeks.** Ask the learner how many hours the learning really took and where they came from. Adjust the plan. The note on [when training is forgotten because the system changed again](/moments/when-training-is-forgotten-because-the-system-changed-again/) is a useful check on whether the training itself will last.

## The business outcome

A business that releases hours for learning gets the skill sooner, with fewer errors along the way, and keeps the person who is learning it. It also avoids a common unhappy ending, in which a keen employee finishes the course exhausted, takes the qualification to another employer and leaves the original business with the cost and none of the benefit. Where the evening has become the default place for catching up, [why "I'll do it tonight" became the default plan](/moments/why-ill-do-it-tonight-became-the-default-plan/) explains how that habit forms.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time and optionality. Time, because the learning sits in the working week and the evening stays the person's own. Optionality, because a new skill that can actually be practised widens what the person can choose to do next, whether that is a promotion, a different role or a better offer elsewhere. What they choose is theirs to decide.

## The trade-offs

- **Protected time lowers short-term output.** The business accepts a slower quarter in exchange for a stronger year, and that is a real cost for a small firm.
- **Some people prefer to study in their own time.** A learner may want the evening quiet or the weekend structure, so the arrangement should allow choice without making it the default.
- **Backfilling costs money.** Reassigning work moves the load to someone else, so it must be shared fairly and not simply pushed down the line.
- **Small teams have little slack.** A practice of three cannot release anyone for long, and may need shorter blocks, a quieter season or an external contractor.

## Why it matters

Asking people to grow is one of the better things an employer can do. Asking them to grow without giving them the hours turns the invitation into a cost that they pay privately. When the hours are named and protected, the same invitation becomes what it was meant to be, a shared investment, and the person making it can finish the course rested enough to use it.
