The phone does not have to ring for on-call duty to cost something. By the second night, a person on stand-by is sleeping lightly, with the phone on the pillow and the volume turned up. By the fourth day they have declined a friend's invitation to a braai forty minutes away, and have not gone for a run in the hills because there is no signal. They are, officially, off duty. They are not quite resting.

This piece looks at that quiet cost and at what a business can do to reduce it. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on fatigue and working hours, not a finding about any particular South African employer, and the example is illustrative.

## The hours that do not show on the roster

On a roster, being on-call looks like a convenience. A name is written beside a date, and if nothing happens, nothing is recorded. In practice, a person on stand-by is working in a particular way: they must stay reachable, stay sober enough to act, stay close enough to a laptop or a site, and stay prepared to lose an hour of sleep at any moment.

HSE's guidance on fatigue describes it as a decline in mental or physical performance that results from prolonged exertion, sleep loss or disruption of the internal clock. It lists slower reactions, memory lapses, reduced attention and underestimation of risk among its effects, and it states that the incidence of accidents and injuries is higher on night shifts and after a succession of shifts. It also makes a point that applies directly to on-call: the legal duty in the UK is on employers to manage the risks from fatigue, regardless of an individual's willingness to work extra hours. A person who is happy to be on-call is not therefore well rested.

The risk is larger because the call, when it comes, is usually a problem: the outage, the failed batch, the angry customer. That is the moment when sound judgement is most needed, and it is requested of the person who has had the least sleep.

## What waiting does to a person

Waiting is not neutral. A mind that expects an interruption does not settle in the same way. The person checks the phone more often, takes shorter breaks, and cannot enter the kind of long, absorbed leisure that makes time off feel like time off. The World Health Organization's guidance on mental health at work lists long or inflexible hours, excessive workloads and low control among the working conditions that can harm wellbeing. On-call duty touches each of them: the hours are unpredictable, the load arrives without warning, and the person has little say over when it comes.

HSE's management standard on demands asks employers to make sure that employees can cope with the demands of their jobs and that working patterns are matched to their capabilities. An on-call arrangement that is only counted when the phone rings does not measure the demand at all.

The people around the on-call person also feel it. A family that cannot plan an evening, a partner who handles the school run alone, a team that never quite knows whether the person will be sharp tomorrow. Our piece on [the school run interrupted by a system alert](/moments/the-school-run-interrupted-by-a-system-alert/) shows how that looks from the doorstep.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Cape Town software support firm with six engineers. One engineer is on-call each week for the booking platform that its clients use around the clock. A monitoring tool sends alerts to a phone. Most weeks the phone buzzes about thirty times. Roughly four of those are real problems; the rest are disk-space warnings that clear on their own, a slow response from a partner service, and a test that fails every Sunday.

The engineers do not complain loudly. They are well paid, the work is interesting, and being on-call is part of the job. But one of them quietly moves house to be closer to the office. Another starts arranging weekends away only on weeks when someone else is on duty. A third, by the end of the week, is making mistakes in the code she writes during the day.

When the manager finally reviews the alerts, he finds that most can be removed or turned into a morning report. The weekly burden is not the four real problems; it is the twenty-six that train people to treat the phone as noise and to stay on edge for the one that is not.

## Making on-call lighter

Several changes help, and most of them are about the quality of alerts, not the people:

- **Page only on action.** If an alert does not need a human before morning, send it to a daily summary instead of waking someone.
- **Fix repeat offenders.** An alert that fires every week without anyone acting on it is a defect to be repaired or removed.
- **Share the rota.** Rotate fairly and publish the schedule weeks ahead, so people can plan their lives around it.
- **Recognise the time.** Give time off after a night with calls, and consider a stand-by allowance. Do not leave goodwill to cover it.
- **Write the first steps down.** A short runbook means the person who is half awake does not have to remember how to restart a service.

South Africa's [Basic Conditions of Employment Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/basic-conditions-employment-act) includes provisions on working time, overtime and rest, which apply to employees it covers. Coverage and thresholds depend on the role and on any sectoral determination, and stand-by arrangements raise their own questions. A business should take advice on how its own arrangements fit the Act, and should not assume that an unpaid availability expectation sits outside it.

## The trade-offs

Reducing alerts takes engineering time that has to come from somewhere, and the benefit is not visible until the nights go quiet. A rota needs enough people to share it; with only two engineers, the week-on, week-off rhythm is hard to soften. A second responder costs money that some small businesses do not have. And some staff prefer on-call pay or the flexibility it brings during the day, so a blanket change may not suit everyone. The right balance is a conversation with the people involved.

## Practical next actions

1. List every alert that woke or interrupted someone in the last month. Mark each as actionable now, actionable in the morning, or noise.
2. Remove or reroute the noise, and move the "morning" items into a daily summary that someone reads at the start of the working day.
3. Publish the on-call rota at least a month ahead, and agree how swaps work so that people can plan family and rest.
4. Set a rule for recovery: after a night with a call, the person starts later or finishes earlier the next day, without needing to ask.
5. Write a one-page runbook for the three most common incidents, so the first ten minutes do not depend on memory.

The same thinking applies when work travels home in other ways; see [the inbox that follows you home](/moments/the-inbox-that-follows-you-home/), [why rest days start with a status check](/moments/why-rest-days-start-with-a-status-check/), and [when flexible work comes with unwritten after-hours rules](/moments/when-flexible-work-comes-with-unwritten-after-hours-rules/).

The goal is not an on-call arrangement with no calls; some businesses really do need someone reachable. It is one in which the person on duty can trust that when the phone rings it matters, and that when it is quiet, they are allowed to rest.
