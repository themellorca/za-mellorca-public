It is seven in the evening. The food is on the table. The phone, face down beside the plate, vibrates. Then again. Then several times in a row.

It is the work group. A customer has asked whether tomorrow's delivery can move to the afternoon. A colleague is asking who has the keys to the second vehicle. The owner has posted a photo of a damaged pallet and a question mark. Nobody has asked this particular person anything. But everyone in the group can see who has read the messages, and who has not replied.

Whether the meal is with family, flatmates or alone with a book does not matter much. What matters is that the person has chosen how to spend the evening, and the group chat has quietly entered the room.

## How group chats became the operating system

In many small South African businesses, the work group chat is not a side channel. It is where the business runs. Jobs are allocated in it. Customers' requests are forwarded to it. Photos of completed work, delivery notes and problems are posted in it. It costs nothing, everyone already has it, and it is fast.

Consider an illustrative case: a Johannesburg events and equipment hire business with about fifteen staff. It has one main group for everyone, one for drivers and one for the office. The owner posts customer changes as they arrive, often in the evening after site visits. Staff answer when they see them, because the next morning's work depends on it.

The mechanism has a few parts:

- **Everyone is in every conversation.** A question for one person reaches fifteen, and each of them must read it to find out whether it concerns them.
- **Urgent and non-urgent look the same.** A change to tomorrow's first delivery and a general reminder arrive in the same stream.
- **The chat is the only record.** If a change is not read tonight, it may be missed tomorrow, because it exists nowhere else. The same problem in email is described in [when business knowledge lives in inbox history](/patterns/when-business-knowledge-lives-in-inbox-history/).
- **Visible reading creates visible silence.** Read receipts and quick replies from others make not replying feel like a choice that colleagues can see.

In the illustrative hire business, nobody set out to make evenings part of the working day. The group was created years ago to share photos of set-ups, and gradually became the place where everything happened. Because instructions now live in a fast-moving stream rather than a list, a message read late can easily be buried by the morning, which is how work gets lost, as described in [why work gets lost between teams](/insights/why-work-gets-lost-between-teams/). Staff learn that the safest thing is to keep reading.

## What the evidence says about work after hours

Microsoft's 2025 [Work Trend Index](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/breaking-down-infinite-workday) analysis of anonymised Microsoft 365 data found that the average worker in its data received more than 50 messages outside core business hours, and that 29% of active workers were back in their inboxes by 10 pm. The data covers Microsoft's own workplace products rather than consumer chat apps, and is not specific to South Africa, so it can only indicate a broader pattern of work continuing into the evening.

Some countries have responded with guidance on what is called the [right to disconnect](https://www.mercer.com/en-us/insights/law-and-policy/ireland-publishes-code-on-right-to-disconnect-from-work/). Ireland introduced a code of practice in 2021. As summarised by Mercer and by the law firm Mayer Brown, it recognises employees' right to switch off from work outside normal hours, including not having to respond immediately to emails, calls or messages, and not being penalised for that.

It also expects employers to develop policies with their staff, and accepts that some roles occasionally need out-of-hours work for legitimate reasons. This is Irish guidance, not South African law, and the code is not binding even in Ireland. It is useful here because it frames the issue as one of how work is organised, not of individual willpower.

## The human tax: presence and attention

The tax is presence: the ability to be mentally where one physically is. A person can be at the table and still half in the chat, reading, deciding whether to reply, thinking about tomorrow's changes.

This should not be overstated. Some people are comfortable dipping into a work chat in the evening, and some prefer it to arriving at a surprise the next morning. Mellorca does not suggest that checking a phone at dinner damages families or relationships, and it would be wrong to imply that. The narrower point is that when the business's operations run through an always-on group, the choice of whether to engage in the evening is shaped by the system rather than made freely by the person.

A related piece, [the inbox that follows you home](/moments/the-inbox-that-follows-you-home/), looks at how unfinished tasks stored in email travel home. Group chats add something extra: the real-time, social pressure of a conversation that everyone can see.

## What could change

1. **Give urgent changes a defined route.** If tomorrow's schedule changes in the evening, it should go to the one person responsible for it, by a direct call or message, not to everyone.
2. **Move routine work out of the chat.** Job allocation, customer requests and delivery changes can live in a simple job list or schedule that people check at the start of the day. The chat becomes for conversation, not the record.
3. **Agree what can wait.** A shared understanding that messages after a certain time do not need a reply until morning, unless they are marked urgent, removes much of the pressure.
4. **Use rotation for genuine after-hours cover.** If the business needs someone available in the evening, make it a known, rotating responsibility, so that everyone else can switch off.
5. **Lead by timing.** Owners can schedule evening messages to send in the morning, so that thinking about work late does not oblige others to do the same.

The wider question of letting the working week actually end is explored in [Friday evening should start on Friday evening](/moments/friday-evening-should-start-on-friday-evening/).

## Trade-offs

- **Speed and familiarity.** Group chats are fast and everyone knows how to use them. A more structured route can feel slower and more formal.
- **Some work really is urgent.** An equipment hire business may genuinely need someone to respond to an evening change. The aim is to concentrate that responsibility, not to pretend it does not exist.
- **Rules can feel restrictive.** Some staff value the flexibility to answer when it suits them. Agreements should leave room for that.
- **Social connection.** Work chats also carry jokes, encouragement and a sense of team. Moving operations out of the chat should not mean losing that.

## Why it matters

For the business, the change means fewer missed instructions, a clearer record of who agreed to what, and less dependence on people reading messages at night. For the person, it is about the evening belonging to them again. They can put the phone in another room, or not, knowing that anything genuinely urgent will reach the right person by another route.

That is the dividend: presence. The freedom to be fully with whoever, or whatever, they have chosen for the evening, without the quiet pull of a conversation that never closes.
