For many owners, the business is the largest asset they will ever hold, and a sale is the way they expect to turn it into retirement, a new venture or a life with fewer obligations. The sale usually comes with a surprise. A buyer, or the buyer's accountant, asks to see the records. The owner opens the drawer, or the shared drive, or a very long email thread, and discovers that the story of the business is harder to prove than to tell.

This piece looks at how messy records can slow or reduce a sale, and at why tidying them is as much about the owner's freedom as about the buyer's confidence. It is a hypothesis drawn from general record-keeping obligations and how buyers examine businesses, not a finding about any particular South African transaction.

## What a buyer is really asking

When a buyer asks for records, they are not collecting paper for its own sake. They are trying to answer a few practical questions. Do the profits claimed actually exist? Which customers generate them, and will they stay? What does the business owe, and what has it promised? Who does the work, and under what terms? Could the business run next month without the person selling it?

Each answer needs evidence. Financial statements need supporting ledgers. Customer claims need contracts or a history of orders. Employee costs need agreements and payroll records. When the evidence is missing, the buyer does not assume the best. They discount for the risk, ask for protections in the contract, or walk away.

## Why records end up messy

Messy records are rarely the result of neglect. They build up because the business was busy, small and trusted its owner. Typical patterns include:

- **Knowledge held by the owner.** Special prices, informal credit terms and long-standing promises that were never written down.
- **Several versions of the truth.** A spreadsheet maintained by one person, another by someone else, and an accounting system that matches neither.
- **Overrides without notes.** Figures changed by hand to make a report balance, with no record of why. The discovery piece on [manual overrides indistinguishable from source data](/discovery/manual-overrides-indistinguishable-from-source-data/) explains how this makes numbers untraceable.
- **Personal and business mixed together.** Business costs paid privately and private costs paid through the business, which makes the real profit hard to see.

## What the rules already require

Part of the problem is that some of these records are required anyway. SARS sets out what businesses must keep in its [record-keeping guidance](https://www.sars.gov.za/client-segments/record-keeping/), including the period for which records need to be retained. The [Companies Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/companies-act) also contains obligations for companies to keep specified records, and retention periods vary by type of record. A business that already meets these requirements has a head start. A business that does not may find that a sale exposes gaps that were present for years.

Records in a sale often include personal information about customers and staff. The [Protection of Personal Information Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/protection-personal-information-act) sets conditions on how such information is processed and shared, so an owner should take advice on what can be disclosed to a prospective buyer and at what stage. That is another reason to know exactly where personal data sits before a sale begins.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Port Elizabeth owner of a commercial cleaning company with 60 staff and a dozen long-standing contracts. He is 63 and wants to sell within two years. A buyer is interested. In due diligence, the buyer asks for the contracts with the three largest clients. Two exist as signed copies. The third is an email exchange from 2019. The pricing for one client includes a verbal discount that only the owner knows about. Staff overtime was covered informally and recorded in a notebook.

None of this suggests wrongdoing. But the buyer cannot confirm what the owner says, so the offer drops and a part of the price is deferred until the client contracts are confirmed. The owner, who expected a clean exit, now faces months of reconstructing the past while still running the business. His timetable is no longer his own.

## The human tax: optionality

The main cost is optionality. An owner whose business can only be understood through them has few ways out. They cannot sell on short notice, cannot step back for a long illness, and cannot let a successor take over without a long and stressful handover. Their choices narrow to staying or accepting whatever terms are offered.

The same weakness hits managers. The Moments on [the founder who wants to step back but can't](/moments/the-founder-who-wants-to-step-back-but-cant/) and [the manager who can't act without the founder](/moments/the-manager-who-cant-act-without-the-founder/) describe the daily version of this dependency. A sale simply puts a price on it.

## What tidy records change

Tidy does not mean perfect. It means that the important facts can be found, traced to their sources and understood by a stranger. The article on [data lineage reconstructed manually during audits](/discovery/data-lineage-reconstructed-manually-during-audits/) describes the effort of tracing numbers backwards after the fact. Doing a little of that work continuously is far cheaper than doing it all in a few frantic weeks.

With records in order, a buyer's questions get quick answers, the process moves faster, and the owner keeps more of the negotiating power. Even if no sale happens, the same records help a successor, a lender or an owner who falls ill.

## Practical next actions

1. **Make a list of what a buyer would ask for.** Include financial statements, customer contracts, employee agreements, licences, property and vehicle papers, and tax submissions. Mark each as complete, partial or missing.
2. **Write down every informal promise.** Special prices, credit terms, verbal agreements and favours owed should be recorded and, where appropriate, confirmed in writing with the other party.
3. **Pick one source for each key figure.** Decide where customer lists, price lists and balances officially live, and retire the duplicate spreadsheets.
4. **Add a reason to every manual change.** Whenever a figure is overridden, record who changed it, when and why, in the same place as the figure.
5. **Ask your accountant for a gap review.** A short review against SARS and Companies Act requirements shows what to fix first and what can wait.

## The business outcome

A business with traceable records is easier to value, easier to finance and easier to hand over. It also runs better in the meantime, since the same records support better decisions and fewer arguments. For the owner, the work done before a sale is a form of insurance against the unplanned events that make sales urgent.

## The human dividend

The dividend is optionality. An owner whose business can be understood by someone else can choose when to sell, when to step back and when to stay. That might mean retiring at 60 or working happily until 75. The point is that the choice belongs to the owner and does not depend on how long it takes to dig a contract out of an old inbox.

## The trade-offs

- **It costs effort before any sale exists.** Time spent tidying competes with running the business, and the payoff is uncertain.
- **Buyers discount for many reasons.** Even excellent records will not guarantee a price, since market conditions and customer concentration still matter.
- **Some knowledge is hard to write down.** Relationships and judgement do not fit neatly into a file, so a handover period may still be needed.
- **It can feel uncomfortable.** Making oneself replaceable on paper can seem like a loss of status, even when it is the thing that grants freedom.

## Why it matters

A business sale is one of the few moments when an owner's years of work are weighed in public. Records are what let that weighing be fair. Keeping them in order early costs little, and it keeps the decision about the owner's future in the owner's own hands.
