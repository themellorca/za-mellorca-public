People who work for a cause often accept lower pay, longer hours and fewer comforts than they would in other jobs. They do it because the work matters to them. That bargain holds as long as the work they do each day feels connected to the reason they came. It tends to fail when the day is spent on spreadsheets, forms and funder templates instead.

This piece looks at how administrative load wears down staff in nonprofits and social enterprises, and what leaders can do to lighten it without dropping the accountability that funders and regulators expect. It is a hypothesis drawn from general guidance on burn-out and working conditions, not a finding about any particular South African organisation.

## Where the paperwork comes from

Mission-driven organisations carry a heavier reporting load than many commercial businesses of the same size. A single programme may have several funders, and each wants its own report, in its own format, on its own schedule. Donors ask for evidence of how money was spent. Regulators require submissions. Boards ask for assurance. All of these are reasonable. Together they add up to a second job that sits on top of the first.

In South Africa, the [Nonprofit Organisations Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/nonprofit-organisations-act) provides for the registration of nonprofit organisations and sets out reporting duties for those that register. Organisations should check the current requirements, since they depend on how the organisation is set up and what it chooses to register for. Funders add their own layers of requirements on top.

The load is also uneven in who carries it. In a small organisation there is rarely an administrator. The work falls to the programme lead, the founder or whoever is best with numbers. These are the people with the most knowledge of the mission, and the ones whose time is hardest to replace.

## What burn-out actually is

The World Health Organization describes [burn-out as an occupational phenomenon](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases) resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, characterised by feelings of exhaustion, mental distance from or cynicism about one's job, and reduced professional efficacy. The description is useful because it places the cause in the workplace, not in the individual's resilience. It also names a feature that mission-driven staff know well: the cynicism arrives not because they stopped caring but because the work stopped looking like the thing they cared about.

The WHO's guidance on [mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) lists excessive workloads and low control among the working conditions that can harm wellbeing. A compliance calendar set by others, with deadlines that cannot move, fits that description closely.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a small Eastern Cape organisation that runs after-school reading programmes in eleven schools. It has three funders and a staff of seven. The programme coordinator, who trained as a teacher and joined to work with children, now spends two days a week on reports. She reconciles attendance sheets to budget lines, rewrites the same results in three formats and chases receipts from volunteers who were reimbursed in cash.

She still loves the work. But she has begun to dread Mondays, and in meetings she is quicker to say "we cannot afford that" than "let's try". The director sees a good employee becoming flat and does not know why. The reason is that her week no longer contains much of what she came for.

## The human tax: dignity and energy

The first cost is energy. Administrative work is rarely hard, but it is draining when it is relentless, fragmented and hostage to deadlines. People finish the week with little left for the family, friends or rest that would help them recover. The mission, meanwhile, gets the leftovers.

The second cost is dignity. Skilled people, such as teachers, social workers and community organisers, are asked to spend large parts of their time on clerical tasks that do not use their training. The Moments on [the teacher who became a data-entry clerk](/moments/the-teacher-who-became-a-data-entry-clerk/) and [why purpose gets lost in administrative noise](/moments/why-purpose-gets-lost-in-administrative-noise/) describe the quiet loss in this. It is the sense that one's judgement is not needed and one's time is not valued.

There is also a risk to the organisation. Burn-out leads to departures, and in a small team a departure takes years of relationships and knowledge with it. The costs of replacement land on an organisation that can least afford them.

## Giving time back

The goal is not to remove accountability. It is to make the necessary administration lighter and put it with the right people. A few approaches tend to help.

- **Capture once, report many times.** Keep one set of activity and spending records, and generate each funder's report from it, instead of rebuilding data for each template.
- **Negotiate the reporting.** Many funders accept a shared format or a reduced schedule when asked, particularly for small grants.
- **Move routine work off specialists.** Receipt collection, attendance capture and reimbursement can use simple forms and a regular schedule, so that skilled staff only review exceptions.
- **Protect mission time.** Block the week so that admin has its own periods, instead of leaking into every day.

The analysis of [high-value employees doing low-value admin](/impact/high-value-employees-doing-low-value-admin/) sets out the financial side of the same argument. For organisations where every rand is donated, the cost of using a trained specialist's time for clerical work is hard to defend.

## Practical next actions

1. **Time one week of admin.** Ask each staff member to note the hours they spend on reports, reconciliations and compliance, separately from programme work. The totals are usually larger than leaders expect.
2. **List every report you owe.** For each funder and regulator, record the format, the deadline and which data it draws on. Look for overlaps that could be served by one dataset.
3. **Ask two funders for a change.** Request a shared template, a longer interval or acceptance of an existing report. Funders are often more flexible than assumed.
4. **Move one routine task.** Pick the most repetitive job, such as chasing receipts, and give it a simple form, a fixed day and a named owner other than the programme lead.
5. **Check in on energy, not only output.** In one-to-ones, ask what drained people this month, and treat repeated answers about paperwork as a business problem, not a personal one.

## The business outcome

Lighter administration lets an organisation do more of what funders are paying for. It retains experienced staff, cuts errors in reporting and makes the organisation easier to audit. It can also help in funding conversations, since an organisation that can show tidy records and a lean reporting process is easier to trust with larger grants. The Moment on [the Saturday morning spent on invoices](/moments/the-saturday-morning-spent-on-invoices/) shows how small administrative burdens spill into personal time.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time. Hours returned to the work a person joined for bring back more than productivity. They restore the link between effort and purpose, and leave people with some energy at the end of the day. How each person spends that time is their choice: more time with the children in the programme, a full lunch break, or leaving at a reasonable hour.

## The trade-offs

- **Accountability is real.** Funders and regulators have good reasons to ask for reports, and cutting corners risks the organisation's standing.
- **Tools cost money.** Small organisations may not have the budget or skills to set up shared records, and a poor setup can add work.
- **Some admin is valuable.** Careful recording often improves programmes, so the aim is to remove duplication, not measurement.
- **Lightening one role loads another.** If admin moves to a volunteer or a part-timer, check that the load is fair and the person has the training.

## Why it matters

Mission-driven organisations run on commitment, and commitment is not unlimited. When the daily work stops resembling the cause, people leave or stay in name only. Giving them back time for the work they came to do is a way to protect both the people and the mission.
