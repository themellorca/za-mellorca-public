A creditors clerk who spent fifteen years capturing supplier invoices arrives one Monday to find that the new accounting system reads them automatically. Nobody has lost a job. The clerk is told the change will free up time. But by Wednesday the same question keeps coming back: if the system does what I did, what am I here for?

This piece is about that question. It is less about whether automation removes jobs and more about what happens to a role when its defining tasks are taken over. It is a hypothesis informed by international labour research and workplace health guidance, not a study of any particular South African business.

## Augmented, not replaced, but still changed

The International Labour Organization's analysis of [generative AI and jobs](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-global-analysis-potential-effects-job-quantity-and) concluded that the main effect of the technology is likely to be augmenting work rather than automating whole occupations, while noting that clerical work is among the most exposed. That finding is reassuring at the level of employment figures. At the level of one person's working week, augmentation can still feel like a loss. The tasks that remain may be the ones the clerk liked least, and the new tasks may be unclear.

A role is not just a list of tasks. It carries an identity: the person who knows every supplier, the one who catches the duplicate invoice, the one others come to. When a system takes over the capture, the visible markers of competence disappear, even if the knowledge behind them is still valuable.

## How roles drift after automation

- **Nobody rewrites the job.** The system goes live, the old tasks shrink, and the job description stays as it was. The person fills the gap with whatever comes along.
- **Exception handling becomes the job by default.** The system handles the clean invoices and passes the odd ones to a person. The clerk ends up dealing only with problems, all day.
- **Expertise goes unrecognised.** The clerk's knowledge of supplier habits is exactly what the system lacks, but nobody has said so, so it is not used deliberately.
- **Silence breeds worry.** When managers do not talk about the future of the role, staff assume the worst.

## The human tax: dignity and confidence

The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standard on [role](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/role.htm) describes a workplace where people understand their role and responsibilities, and where the organisation provides information to help them do so. After automation, that understanding is often exactly what is missing. The World Health Organization's guidance on [mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) lists an unclear job role and job insecurity among the risks to wellbeing.

The confidence cost is real. Someone who was the acknowledged expert at a task becomes a beginner at whatever replaces it. Being a beginner at fifty, in front of colleagues, is not the same as being a beginner at twenty-two. Without support, some people withdraw or cling to the old method, which can look like resistance but is often a form of self-protection.

The dignity cost is in the message the silence sends. If nobody explains what the person is now for, they may reasonably conclude that the business has not thought about it, and therefore that they do not matter much.

## What a deliberate redesign looks like

An illustrative example, not a real case: a Pietermaritzburg wholesale distributor introduces automated invoice capture. Before go-live, the finance manager sits down with the creditors clerk and lists what the system will do, what it will not do, and what the business needs that nobody currently has time for. Together they agree that the clerk will own supplier data quality, resolve exceptions, review payment terms with the top twenty suppliers and train the system's matching rules. The new role is written down, with a three-month review. The clerk is still uncertain, but now has something specific to be uncertain about, and a date to talk again.

## Practical next actions

1. **Talk before go-live.** Before any automation goes live, meet each affected person and explain which of their tasks will change and which will not.
2. **List the work nobody has time for.** Ask the team what useful work is currently neglected. That list is the raw material for the redesigned role.
3. **Write the new role down.** Update the job description with the new responsibilities and the expertise the person is expected to bring, and agree a review date.
4. **Budget for learning.** Allocate time and, if needed, training for the new tasks, and make it acceptable to be slow at first.
5. **Name the expertise.** Say explicitly which parts of the person's knowledge the business still needs, such as supplier history or judgement on exceptions.
6. **Be honest about uncertainty.** If the future of a role is genuinely unclear, say so and say when you will know more, rather than offering reassurance you cannot back.

## The business outcome

A deliberate redesign turns freed capacity into useful work instead of letting it dissipate. The business keeps the knowledge of experienced staff, which automation cannot replicate, and adoption tends to go more smoothly when people know where they fit. It also reduces the risk of losing good people who leave because they could not see a future in the changed role.

## The human dividend

The dividend is optionality: a wider set of things the person can do and grow into. A clerk who becomes the owner of supplier data quality has a skill that travels, a clearer case for development, and more say in how the finance function works. Not everyone will want the new shape of the role, and that is legitimate too. What matters is that the choice is made openly.

## The trade-offs

- **Redesign takes management time.** Conversations, rewritten roles and reviews compete with the pressure to show savings quickly.
- **Not every role can be rebuilt.** Sometimes there is not enough new work, and honesty about that is better than invented tasks.
- **Some people prefer the old work.** A person may have liked the routine and may not want analytical responsibilities.
- **Exception work can be draining.** A role made only of problems needs variety built in, or it becomes its own source of strain.

## Why it matters

Automation decisions are usually framed around cost and accuracy. The question of what the role is now for is left to the person in it, who has the least information and the most at stake. Answering that question openly, and writing the answer down, is a small amount of management effort that protects both the person's confidence and the value the business hoped to gain.
