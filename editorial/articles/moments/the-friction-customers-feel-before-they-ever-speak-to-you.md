By the time a customer finally speaks to someone at your business, they have usually already done some work. They searched for a phone number, filled in a contact form, waited for a reply that did not come, and tried again. Much of what they think of you was formed during that stretch, and nobody inside the business saw it happen.

This piece looks at that pre-contact friction: the effort a customer spends simply trying to reach you or follow up. It is a hypothesis drawn from consumer protection law, accessibility standards and common small-business practice, not research on any particular company.

## The part of the journey nobody owns

Inside most businesses, responsibility starts when a request arrives. Sales owns the lead once it is logged. Service owns the query once a ticket exists. The steps before that, the website form, the phone menu, the WhatsApp number that nobody monitors after five, often belong to no one in particular. They were set up once and left alone.

An illustrative example, not a real case: a Johannesburg plumbing and maintenance company with three office staff. A customer whose geyser has burst fills in the website's quote form at 7pm. The form sends an email to a general mailbox. The next morning, that mailbox has forty messages, the form submission sits among supplier statements, and nobody replies until mid-afternoon. Meanwhile the customer has phoned twice, reached a voicemail once and an engaged line once, and has booked someone else. Inside the business, nothing visibly went wrong. Outside it, the customer had a bad day and remembers who caused part of it.

## Common sources of pre-contact friction

- **Forms that ask too much.** Long forms with mandatory fields that do not matter yet, such as a company registration number for a first enquiry.
- **No acknowledgement.** A form or email disappears with no confirmation, so the customer does not know whether to wait or try again.
- **Channels that are not watched.** A listed WhatsApp number or social inbox that staff check only occasionally.
- **Repeat explanations.** A customer who follows up has to explain the problem from the beginning because the first message cannot be found.
- **Inaccessible pages.** Contact pages that do not work with screen readers, on small phones, or on slow data connections.

## The human tax: time and energy on both sides

For the customer, the cost is time and energy spent before any help arrives. For someone dealing with a burst pipe, a sick pet or a payroll problem, that energy is already in short supply. Chasing a reply turns a practical problem into an emotional one.

The tax also lands on staff. When the follow-up call finally comes in, the person answering it meets a customer who is already frustrated and may not be able to find the original message. They absorb the irritation for a delay they did not cause. Over time, staff can come to dread the phone, which is a poor outcome for people who usually chose service work because they like helping.

## What the rules and standards say

South Africa's [Consumer Protection Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/consumer-protection-act) sets national standards for how businesses treat consumers, and while it does not prescribe response times for enquiries, its spirit of fair and responsible dealing is a reasonable test for how first contact feels. For accessibility, the W3C's [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/) set out how web content can be made usable by people with disabilities, organised around four principles: perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. A contact form that cannot be completed with a keyboard or read by a screen reader is an obstacle before the conversation has started.

Contact forms also collect personal information, which brings the [Protection of Personal Information Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/protection-personal-information-act) into view. Asking only for what you need at first contact is both kinder to the customer and easier to justify.

## What could realistically change

The fixes are usually small. Shorten the first form to what is needed to respond. Send an automatic acknowledgement that says when a person will reply, and keep that promise. Route each enquiry to a named queue with someone responsible for it during business hours, and say clearly when channels are not monitored. Keep the original message attached to the customer's record so a follow-up starts where the first contact ended.

## Practical next actions

1. **Be your own customer.** From a personal phone, use every contact channel you advertise, after hours and during the day, and note how long each takes to get a human response.
2. **Cut the first form.** Remove every field that is not needed to give a first reply, and make the rest optional.
3. **Acknowledge every enquiry.** Set up an automatic reply that confirms receipt and states when a person will respond.
4. **Give each channel an owner.** Name who watches each inbox or number and when, and remove channels you cannot staff.
5. **Test accessibility basics.** Check that your contact page works on a small phone, with a keyboard only, and on a slow connection.

## The business outcome

Fewer enquiries are lost between channels, and fewer customers book a competitor while waiting. Staff handle calmer conversations, and duplicate contacts drop because customers are not chasing. The business also gains a clearer picture of demand, because enquiries are captured in one place instead of scattered across inboxes.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time, mostly the customer's, though staff gain some too. A customer who gets a prompt acknowledgement and a reply within the promised window can stop thinking about the problem and get on with their day. Staff who receive enquiries with context attached spend their time helping instead of reconstructing what happened.

## The trade-offs

- **Promises create obligations.** An acknowledgement that promises a reply within four hours must be kept, or it adds to the frustration.
- **Fewer channels can feel like less choice.** Closing an unmonitored channel is honest, but some customers preferred it.
- **Short forms mean more questions later.** Collecting less at first contact can mean a follow-up call to gather details.
- **Automatic replies can feel cold.** Keep them brief, human in tone, and clear about what happens next.

## Why it matters

Customers judge a business partly by what it is like to reach. The friction they feel before the first conversation is invisible from the inside, which is why it persists. Looking at contact from the outside, and fixing the few points where effort is wasted, is one of the cheaper ways to make both customers and staff feel better about the same interaction.
