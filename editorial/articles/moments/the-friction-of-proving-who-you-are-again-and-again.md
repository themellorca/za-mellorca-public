A customer has been with the same insurance broker for nine years. She phones to update her cover and is asked for her identity number, her date of birth and the answer to a security question she set long ago and has forgotten. She is asked to email a certified copy of her identity document, as she did last year and the year before. When she visits the office to sign, the receptionist asks to see her identity document again. She leaves feeling that, after nine years, the business does not know her at all.

This piece looks at why existing customers are asked to prove who they are again and again, and how a business can verify properly once and then recognise people safely. It is a hypothesis drawn from identity standards and South African regulatory guidance. It is not compliance advice; some re-verification is legitimately required.

## Why proof is repeated

Repeated proof is usually a sign that verification is not being remembered. Each department, channel or staff member verifies the customer from scratch, because there is no reliable record that verification already happened, or no trusted way to recognise the customer next time.

Sometimes it is caution after a fraud incident, applied to every customer forever. Sometimes it is a form designed for new customers being used for existing ones. And sometimes it is simply that documents were collected but filed where nobody can find them.

## Verify once, then recognise

Identity professionals separate two ideas: proving who someone is, and recognising them later. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology's guidelines on [identity proofing and enrolment](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63a.html) describe proving a person's identity to an appropriate level, then binding authenticators, such as a login or a device, to that proven identity so the person can be recognised without repeating the proof. The guidelines also caution against re-collecting and re-verifying evidence already processed, except in defined situations such as long inactivity, signs of fraud or evidence that has become invalid.

In a South African context, firms that are accountable institutions must identify and verify their clients. The Financial Intelligence Centre's [compliance page](https://www.fic.gov.za/compliance/) explains that the level of identification and verification must match the client's risk profile, under a risk-based approach. Ongoing due diligence is part of that, but proportionate checks are not the same as asking a low-risk, long-standing customer for the same documents every year without reason.

Privacy law points the same way. The [Protection of Personal Information Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/protection-personal-information-act) sets conditions for processing personal information. Collecting the same identity documents repeatedly, and leaving copies in email inboxes, increases the risk of that information being lost or misused.

## An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a ten-person short-term insurance brokerage in Kimberley. Identity documents are stored as email attachments, so staff cannot easily confirm what is on file. Phone verification relies on a security question many customers forget. Every policy change triggers a request for a fresh certified copy.

When the broker reviews the process with its compliance adviser, it sets up a client record that notes when and how each client was verified, stores documents in one secure place, and introduces a verification step by one-time PIN to the client's registered cellphone for routine changes. Fresh documents are requested only when the record shows they are due or something has changed. Calls become shorter, and long-standing clients notice.

## The human tax: time and dignity

The time cost is easy to see: finding documents, getting them certified, scanning, emailing, waiting on hold while answers are checked. The dignity cost is subtler. Being treated as a stranger by a business you have trusted for years can feel like being suspected. Our story [why customers want to talk to someone who knows them](/moments/why-customers-want-to-talk-to-someone-who-knows-them/) describes how much recognition matters.

Staff pay a price too. Asking a regular customer for the same document again is awkward, and handling the frustration that follows wears people down. The neighbouring story [the relief of a password you don't have to reset](/moments/the-relief-of-a-password-you-dont-have-to-reset/) covers the parallel friction inside organisations, and [the time tax of re-explaining the same thing](/moments/the-time-tax-of-re-explaining-the-same-thing/) shows how repetition drains both sides of a conversation.

## Practical next actions

1. **Map every verification step.** List each point where a customer is asked to prove identity, by channel and by department.
2. **Record verification once.** Keep a single client record showing when and how verification was completed and what is on file.
3. **Store documents securely, in one place.** Move identity documents out of inboxes into a secure, access-controlled store.
4. **Introduce recognition for routine contact.** Use a safe method, such as a one-time PIN to a registered number, for low-risk changes.
5. **Define when to re-verify.** Agree, with compliance advice, the specific triggers for fresh documents rather than asking every time.
6. **Explain to customers.** When re-verification is needed, explain why, so it reads as care rather than suspicion.

## The business outcome

Verifying once and recognising afterwards shortens calls and visits, reduces administrative effort and improves customer loyalty. It can also improve security, because a well-managed record and a consistent recognition method are harder to exploit than ad hoc checks by different staff. Compliance reviews become easier when verification history is in one place.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time, and the feeling of being known. The customer of nine years makes her change in a few minutes, confirmed by a code to her phone, and is greeted as someone the business remembers. Staff spend their time helping rather than checking. The relationship reflects the trust that has already been built.

## The trade-offs

- **Some checks must be repeated.** Regulation, fraud indicators or changed circumstances can legitimately require fresh verification.
- **Recognition needs security.** One-time PINs and logins must be protected against SIM swaps and account takeover.
- **Risks change.** Verification rules must be reviewed as fraud patterns evolve.
- **Stored documents carry duties.** Holding identity documents requires careful access control and retention rules.

## Why it matters

Being asked to prove who you are, again and again, by a business that should know you is a small indignity repeated many times. Verifying properly once, recording it well and recognising people safely afterwards respects both the customer's time and the trust they have placed in the business.
