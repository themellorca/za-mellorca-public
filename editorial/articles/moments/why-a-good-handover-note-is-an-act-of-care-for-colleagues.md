On a Thursday afternoon, a colleague leaves for two weeks of leave, a new baby, a funeral or a long-planned holiday. They leave a handover note. In some businesses the note is a single line: "Everything is in the shared folder." In others it is a page that tells the next person what is urgent, what is waiting, who to call and what to leave alone. The difference between them is not only efficiency. It is a quiet statement about how the person who is leaving thinks of the person who is staying.

This piece argues that a good handover note is a form of care, for the colleague who covers, for the customers who depend on the work, and for the person who leaves and wants to leave in peace. It is a hypothesis drawn from general research on handovers and wellbeing, not a finding about a named South African employer.

## What a handover is for

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance on [shift handover](https://www.hse.gov.uk/humanfactors/topics/shift-handover.htm) describes the goal as the accurate and reliable communication of task-relevant information, so that work continues safely and effectively when responsibility passes from one person or team to another. It describes effective handover as having several elements, including preparation by the person who is leaving and a conversation in which outgoing and incoming people exchange the information that matters.

That guidance was written with shift work in mind, but the principle applies to any transfer of responsibility: a few days of leave, a long absence, a change of role. In every case, the person who stays has to act on information that someone else collected. The quality of that information decides whether they feel capable or stranded.

## Why a note is more than a document

A handover note is a short piece of writing, but it carries three kinds of message.

- **It says what is true.** The status of open items, deadlines, promises made to customers and traps to avoid.
- **It says what is trusted.** By setting out what the covering person may decide alone and what must wait, it gives them a clear mandate, which helps them act without second-guessing.
- **It says that the cover matters.** The effort of writing the note tells the colleague that their time and peace of mind were thought about before the leaving person went.

When these messages are missing, the cost is borne by the person who stays. They hunt for context, interrupt the absent colleague on leave, and carry the worry that they may do something wrong. The article on [what a reliable handover gives back on a Friday afternoon](/moments/what-a-reliable-handover-gives-back-on-a-friday-afternoon/) looks at the benefit from the departing person's side.

## An illustrative case

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: a Bloemfontein property-management office with six staff. A senior administrator, who handles tenant queries, deposits and contractor bookings, takes ten days of family leave after a bereavement. Her handover is a hurried message in a group chat: "Call the plumber about unit 14. Everything else is in my inbox."

The colleague who covers has no idea which of eighty emails is urgent. A tenant calls about a deposit that the administrator had promised to refund by the end of the week. The colleague does not know about the promise. On the administrator's return, there is an angry tenant, an apology to write and a colleague who feels she was set up to fail. The administrator, who was grieving, feels she has failed too. Nobody was careless. The note simply was not written.

## The human tax: presence

The cost here is presence, the ability to be wholly where you are. A person on leave who has not handed over properly is not fully on leave. They check messages, answer calls and rehearse the open items in their head, as the note on [the price of never fully switching off on leave](/moments/the-price-of-never-fully-switching-off-on-leave/) describes. A person covering without a note is not fully at their desk, because part of their attention is spent wondering what they have missed.

The World Health Organization's [fact sheet on mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) lists unclear roles and lack of control among the working conditions that can harm wellbeing. A covering colleague with no note meets both. They have been given a responsibility without the information to carry it.

The piece on [when parental leave means handing over an undocumented job](/moments/when-parental-leave-means-handing-over-an-undocumented-job/) shows how heavy the issue becomes for longer absences, and the note about [the returning employee who finds everything changed](/moments/the-returning-employee-who-finds-everything-changed/) describes the same gap from the opposite end: the person returning needs a handback as much as the cover needed a handover.

The HSE's [overview of safety critical communications](https://www.hse.gov.uk/humanfactors/topics/communications.htm) treats clear communication as a human factors topic, which fits: how information passes between people is a design question, not a matter of personal style.

## What a good note contains

A good handover note is not long. It is specific. It tends to cover six things.

- **What is urgent this week**, with dates and the consequence of missing them.
- **What is in progress**, with the current state of each item and the next step.
- **Promises made** to customers, suppliers or colleagues that someone must keep.
- **Who to contact**, including a backup when the first person is unavailable.
- **What the cover may decide alone**, and what should wait for the absent person or a manager.
- **Where to look** for files, logins and instructions, with a note on what not to touch.

The note works best when it is followed by a short conversation, even ten minutes, in which the cover reads it and asks questions. The HSE's guidance puts weight on this exchange for good reason: writing carries information, but conversation checks that it has been understood.

## What could realistically change

Handover quality improves when it is treated as part of the job and not as a favour. A one-page template removes the blank-page problem. Time set aside on the last working day removes the excuse that there was no time. A culture in which a manager reads the note, even briefly, tells people that it matters. The article on [why documented processes help people grow into roles](/moments/why-documented-processes-help-people-grow-into-roles/) shows how the same documents help newcomers, and [when the out-of-office reply does not stop the work](/moments/when-the-out-of-office-reply-doesnt-stop-the-work/) describes what happens when the handover never really happens.

## Practical next actions

1. **Create a one-page handover template.** Use the six headings above, and keep it in a shared place where anyone about to go on leave will find it.
2. **Block time on the last day.** Ask people to reserve thirty minutes before leave for writing the note and ten minutes for a conversation with their cover.
3. **Test the note.** Ask the covering person to say what they would do first, and fix any gap in the note that the answer reveals.
4. **Set a contact rule.** Agree in advance whether, when and how the person on leave may be reached, and for what. The default should be that they are not.
5. **Write a handback, too.** Ask the cover to leave a short summary of what happened, so that the returning person can pick up the thread without a day of detective work.

## The business outcome

Good handovers lower the number of dropped items, broken promises and avoidable customer complaints. They reduce the interruptions that follow people onto leave, and they make it easier to cover absences at short notice. Over time, the habit builds a business where knowledge is shared, which is more resilient than one where it sits with individuals.

## The human dividend

The dividend is presence and predictability. The person leaving can be fully absent, with the knowledge that their work is held. The person staying can be fully at work, with the confidence that they know what to do. What the departing colleague does with their time away, whether it is rest, family, grief, travel or nothing in particular, is their own business.

## The trade-offs

- **Writing takes effort.** The note costs time at the busiest moment, just before leave, so it must be short enough to survive that pressure.
- **Notes can go stale.** A note that is out of date is worse than none, so it should be written for the specific absence, not stored as a standing document.
- **Some knowledge is hard to write.** Judgement and relationships do not fit neatly in a list, and need a conversation or a joint visit.
- **Over-detailed notes are unread.** A five-page handover can bury the one urgent item, so the first lines should always say what cannot wait.

## Why it matters

A handover note is a small act, but it shows whether the people in a business expect to look after one another. Writing it well tells a colleague that their peace of mind counts, that the customers will not be let down, and that the leaving person's time away is theirs to keep.
