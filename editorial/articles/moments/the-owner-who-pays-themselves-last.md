Ask a small-business owner about the best part of payroll day and many will say it is the moment the last salary clears. Ask about the worst part and the answer is often the same moment, because it is also when they look at what is left and find that their own pay has become a question mark. The owner who pays everyone else first is a familiar figure, and often an admired one. This piece asks what it costs, and what a different rhythm might look like.

It is a hypothesis drawn from how statutory payments and payroll timing work, not a finding about any particular business. The aim is practical: to treat the owner's pay as a planned cost with a date, not a remainder.

## How the remainder model works

Consider an illustrative owner of a forty-person electrical contracting company in Pretoria. Each month she pays wages, the tax and levies on those wages, suppliers and rent. Only then does she look at the bank balance and transfer something to herself. In a good month the amount is generous. In a month when a large client pays late, it is zero, and in a bad month she lends the business money from her own savings. Her household budget therefore moves with the company's cash timing, not with any decision she has made.

Nobody forced this arrangement. It grew from a sensible instinct: staff have rent to pay and cannot wait, so they come first. The instinct is a good one. The problem is that it quietly turns the owner into the business's emergency reserve.

## The obligations that do not move

Part of why the owner goes last is that other payments have fixed dates and consequences. On the SARS pages for [PAYE](https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/pay-as-you-earn/), employers are described as responsible for deducting tax from employees' remuneration and paying it over to SARS. The same applies to the [Skills Development Levy](https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/skills-development-levy/), which is a separate employer obligation. The Unemployment Insurance Act places contribution duties on employers too, and the Basic Conditions of Employment Act contains rules on how and when covered employees must be paid.

Taken together, these mean that most of the payroll bill is not negotiable in a lean month. What can flex is the owner's draw, because the owner is the only person who can agree to be paid later. That is how the cushion forms, and it is why it is easy to mistake a sacrifice for a plan.

## The human tax: financial resilience

The cost is borne by the owner's household. A bond or school fees do not shrink in a thin month. An owner who cannot predict their income cannot comfortably commit to a medical aid, a retirement contribution or a family holiday, and may postpone all three for years while the business takes priority. This is financial resilience eroding at the very person the business depends on most.

There is a second, quieter cost. An owner who is anxious about personal cash may make business decisions from that anxiety: discounting to collect quickly, delaying a necessary hire, or avoiding a conversation with a slow-paying client. The reasoning of the business and the reasoning of the household have become tangled, and neither is well served.

It would be wrong to assume every owner wants a comfortable salary above all. Some knowingly accept volatility for the sake of building something. The concern here is for the owner who has never chosen the arrangement, and for whom "last" simply happened.

## What could realistically change

The change is mostly one of structure and visibility, and less about earning more. The first step is to treat owner pay as a line in the payroll, with a date, an amount and a place in the monthly cash plan, set at a level the business can reliably afford. Related stories describe the pressure points on either side: [when payroll week means late nights for one person](/moments/when-payroll-week-means-late-nights-for-one-person/) shows the workload around the date, and [the cash-flow week that keeps the owner awake](/moments/the-cash-flow-week-that-keeps-the-owner-awake/) shows what the uncertainty feels like.

The second step is a buffer that belongs to the business, held apart from the owner's own savings. A common rule of thumb is to build towards covering one payroll cycle, though the right size depends on how predictable receipts are. The third is to shorten the gap between work done and cash received, since late payment is what most often squeezes the owner's draw. The note on [when late invoices become late salaries](/moments/when-late-invoices-become-late-salaries/) follows that chain in detail.

## Practical next actions

1. **Calculate the true cost of payroll.** Add salaries, PAYE, UIF, the Skills Development Levy and any benefits, and compare the total with the bank balance on the day before it is due.
2. **Set an owner salary on the payroll.** Choose an amount the business can pay in an ordinary lean month, and pay it on the same date and through the same process as everyone else's.
3. **Open a separate buffer account.** Move a fixed amount into it each month until it covers a defined portion of one payroll run, and agree the rules for when it may be used.
4. **List who owes you money.** Review the debtors that are more than thirty days overdue and decide who will chase each one, and by which date.
5. **Review once a quarter.** Compare actual owner pay with the plan, adjust the salary up or down with the numbers in front of you, and take any larger profit as a separate, deliberate decision.
6. **Talk to your accountant.** Ask how owner remuneration, dividends and tax interact for your type of business, as the best structure depends on the legal form and the owner's circumstances.

## The business outcome

A business that plans owner pay sees its real costs. It stops subsidising itself with the owner's goodwill, which means prices, margins and collection practices get judged against honest numbers. It also reduces the key-person risk that comes from an owner who is financially stretched and personally exhausted, a theme explored in [the owner who wants choices, not just more revenue](/moments/the-owner-who-wants-choices-not-just-more-revenue/).

## The human dividend

The dividend is financial breathing room: a household that knows roughly what is coming in each month, and an owner who can plan a school year, a medical procedure or a rest without first checking whether a client has paid. What the owner does with that room is their own business. It may be saving, a holiday, further study or investing in the company. The gain is the ability to choose.

## The trade-offs

- **A smaller cushion in lean months.** Every rand paid to the owner on schedule is a rand the business no longer has as a buffer, so the salary has to be modest and honest.
- **Slower reinvestment.** Some owners will have less to put back into growth, at least until the buffer is built.
- **Uncomfortable numbers.** Putting owner pay on the payroll may show that the business cannot yet afford the owner's actual living costs, which is hard to learn but better learned early.
- **Seasonal limits.** Where income is lumpy, pay may need to be smoothed over a year, and some months will still be tight.

## Why it matters

An owner who always pays themselves last is subsidising the business with their own security, usually without having decided to. Making owner pay a planned, scheduled cost does not make anyone less generous. It gives the person at the centre of the business the same predictability they work so hard to give everyone else.
