Ask a physiotherapist, a site engineer or a compliance officer what they did yesterday, and the honest answer often has two halves. There was the part they trained for, and there was the part where they described that work, several times, in several places, for several readers. For a growing number of professionals the second half is now the larger one.

This piece looks at why documentation expands until it crowds out the work it is meant to record, and what can be done without weakening the records a business genuinely needs. It is a hypothesis built from published research and workplace guidance, not a study of any South African firm.

## What the shift looks like

The clearest measurement comes from medicine, where electronic records leave a detailed trail. A 2017 study in the Annals of Family Medicine, [Tethered to the EHR](https://www.annfammed.org/content/15/5/419), used system event logs and direct observation of primary care physicians. It found that they spent close to six hours a weekday in the electronic record, including more than an hour after clinic hours, and that clerical and administrative tasks, documentation among them, made up a large share of that time. Doctors are not the only professionals in this position; they are simply the ones whose screen time has been counted carefully.

Consider an illustrative example rather than a real case: a structural engineer at a mid-sized Durban consultancy. A site inspection takes ninety minutes. Afterwards she writes an inspection report for the client, enters the same findings into the firm's project system for billing, updates a shared tracker for the project manager, and emails a summary to the contractor because the contractor cannot open the tracker. Each version is slightly different in format and emphasis. By the end, the write-up has taken longer than the inspection, and none of the four documents told anyone anything the first one did not.

## Where the extra writing comes from

Documentation rarely grows because someone decides it should. It accumulates through a few ordinary habits:

- **Each reader gets a copy in their own format.** The client, the finance team, the manager and the regulator all want the same facts, but in different templates and systems, so the professional becomes the translator.
- **Defensive writing.** After a dispute or an audit finding, people learn to record everything, just in case. Nobody later removes the extra fields when the risk has passed.
- **Systems that do not share.** When the booking system, the job system and the billing system do not talk to each other, a person carries the information between them by typing it again.
- **Status requests on top of records.** Even when the record exists, people ask for an update by email because they cannot see the record or do not trust that it is current.

## The human tax: dignity and time

The time cost is easy to see on a timesheet. The dignity cost is quieter. A professional's sense of worth at work is tied to using judgement and skill. The World Health Organization's guidance on [mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) lists under-use of skills and excessive workloads among the risks to wellbeing. Hours of repetitive write-ups combine both: the skilled part of the job shrinks, and the total load grows.

Documentation also follows people home. Unlike the inspection, the write-up can be done at night, so it often is. The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standard on [demands](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/demands.htm) says workloads should be achievable within agreed hours. A role in which the paperwork routinely spills into evenings is not meeting that test, however rewarding the core work may be.

It would be wrong to say documentation is the enemy. Good records protect clients, support handovers and allow a business to learn from its own work. The concern is with duplicated and defensive writing that serves nobody in particular.

## What could realistically change

The aim is to write once and let the record travel. That can mean one structured form whose fields feed the client report, the billing entry and the tracker, so the engineer captures findings once and each reader receives the view they need. It can mean giving the contractor read access to the record instead of a separate email. It can also mean removing fields that nobody has read in a year.

Not every fix is technical. Sometimes the improvement is an agreement that the project tracker is the single place for status, which removes the need for update emails. Related stories cover the neighbouring pressures: [the nurse whose shift is half paperwork](/moments/the-nurse-whose-shift-is-half-paperwork/) describes the clinical version, and [what changes when status updates stop being a chore](/moments/what-changes-when-status-updates-stop-being-a-chore/) looks at the reporting side. Where the same facts are typed into several screens, [the teacher who became a data-entry clerk](/moments/the-teacher-who-became-a-data-entry-clerk/) shows how that drift happens.

## Practical next actions

1. **Measure one week honestly.** Ask two or three professionals to note, roughly, how long they spend on the core task and how long on writing it up. Use the figures to start a conversation, not to judge anyone.
2. **Map every copy.** For one common job, list every place the same facts are recorded and who reads each one. Mark the copies that exist only because a system cannot share.
3. **Remove one field or report.** Find a form field or a routine report that nobody has used recently, confirm with its supposed reader, and stop producing it.
4. **Give readers access instead of copies.** Where a manager or client only needs to see status, give them read access to the existing record rather than a separate summary.
5. **Check what the law actually requires.** Separate the records that a regulator or contract requires from those kept out of habit, and keep the first group complete and well organised.

## The business outcome

When documentation is captured once, the firm's records become more consistent, because there are fewer versions to disagree with each other. Billing tends to be faster and more accurate. Professionals spend more paid hours on the work clients value, and the business is less dependent on late-night catch-up to stay current.

## The human dividend

The dividend is time, returned to the part of the job that drew the person to the profession in the first place. An engineer who finishes the write-up in twenty minutes instead of two hours may use that time for another inspection, for mentoring a junior colleague, for thinking, or for going home on time. The choice is theirs. What matters is that the work they trained for is no longer the smaller half of their day.

## The trade-offs

- **Structured forms can feel rigid.** Capturing findings in fields saves time but may lose nuance, so leave room for free-text judgement.
- **Integration costs money.** Connecting systems so the record travels may need configuration or development effort before it pays back.
- **Removing records carries risk.** A report that looks unused may matter once a year, so confirm before you stop it.
- **Access needs care.** Sharing a record more widely means thinking about who should see personal or commercially sensitive details.

## Why it matters

Most professionals accept that recording their work is part of the job. What wears them down is recording it four times for four readers who could have shared one record. Reducing that duplication is a modest change with a large effect on how the work feels, and on how much of the professional's skill the business actually gets to use.
