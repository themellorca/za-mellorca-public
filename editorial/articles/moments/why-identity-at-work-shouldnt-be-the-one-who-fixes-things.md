Every workplace has one. When the server goes down, the payment run fails or the production line stops, people look to the same person. They arrive, stay calm, and fix it. They are thanked, sometimes praised in front of everyone. Over the years, that role becomes who they are at work: the one who fixes things.

There is real skill and generosity in that role. This piece argues that it is nonetheless a fragile thing to build an identity on, both for the person and for the organisation, and that there is a better foundation available. It is a hypothesis informed by engineering practice and workplace-health guidance, not a psychological study.

## How the identity forms

The fixer identity forms through recognition. Emergencies are visible. Everyone knows when the system was down and who brought it back. Prevention is invisible. Nobody notices the outage that did not happen because someone patched a weak point last month. So the organisation, without meaning to, rewards rescue over prevention.

The person on the receiving end of that recognition naturally comes to value it. Being needed feels good. Being the only one who can do something feels important. The neighbouring story [when status is measured in how many fires you put out](/moments/when-status-is-measured-in-how-many-fires-you-put-out/) looks at this from the organisation's side, and [why recognition rarely reaches the people who prevent problems](/moments/why-recognition-rarely-reaches-the-people-who-prevent-problems/) describes who loses out.

## An illustrative example

Consider an illustrative example, not a real case: the IT support person at a fifty-person logistics firm in Johannesburg. He has been there twelve years and knows every quirk of the systems. When something breaks, he is the hero of the day. When the firm moves to managed cloud services that rarely fail, his phone goes quiet.

He is relieved, at first. Then uneasy. He finds himself wondering whether he is still valued. When asked to write down how the old systems work, so others can support them, he delays. It is not obstruction. Writing it down feels like giving away the thing that made him matter.

## The human tax: dignity

The tax is dignity, and it shows up in several ways. A person whose worth depends on emergencies is, in a quiet way, dependent on things going wrong. They may feel unneeded when things go right, which is a strange and uncomfortable position. They may feel anxious about sharing knowledge, because sharing reduces their uniqueness. And they carry a constant load of being on call for the next crisis.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on [mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work) lists excessive workloads, unclear roles and low control among the psychosocial risks to wellbeing. The fixer role often combines all three: unpredictable demand, a job description that does not mention the real work, and no control over when the next emergency arrives. The UK Health and Safety Executive's standard on [role](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/role.htm) expects organisations to make requirements clear and to give people ways to raise role conflicts. A role defined by rescue is rarely clear.

## A better foundation

The alternative is not to stop valuing skill. It is to value it differently. Google's site reliability engineering book, in its chapter on [blameless postmortem culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/), argues that the purpose of reviewing an incident is to find the contributing causes and fix systems and processes rather than people. It also notes that blame makes people hide problems. Applied to the fixer, the lesson is that the most valuable thing an expert can do after an emergency is make sure it does not need them next time.

That gives the expert a different identity: the person who makes things work reliably, who teaches others, and whose knowledge lives in the organisation rather than only in their head. That identity grows rather than shrinks as systems improve. The story [the employee who dreads being the only one who knows](/moments/the-employee-who-dreads-being-the-only-one-who-knows/) shows how heavy the alternative can become.

Managers carry most of the responsibility for this shift. People build identities around what is recognised. If prevention, documentation and teaching are noticed and rewarded, people will build their worth on them.

## Practical next actions

1. **Recognise prevention.** In team meetings, name the work that stopped problems happening, not only the work that fixed them.
2. **Review emergencies for causes.** After each incident, ask the expert to lead a short review of why it happened and how to prevent it.
3. **Make teaching part of the role.** Write knowledge sharing and training into the expert's job description and goals.
4. **Pair the expert with a learner.** Assign a colleague to learn the critical fixes, so the expert is not the only one on call.
5. **Talk about the change.** When systems improve, discuss openly how the expert's role is changing and what new value they bring.
6. **Protect their time off.** Arrange cover so the expert can be genuinely unreachable on leave.

## The business outcome

An organisation that rewards prevention has fewer emergencies, shorter outages and less dependence on one person. Knowledge spreads, so absences and resignations cause less damage. The expert's energy goes into improving systems, which compounds over time, instead of into repeated rescues.

## The human dividend

The dividend is predictability, and a steadier sense of worth. The expert can enjoy a quiet week without wondering whether they still matter. Their value is visible in systems that work, colleagues they have taught and problems that no longer happen. That is a working identity that survives good days as well as bad ones.

## The trade-offs

- **Prevention is hard to see.** Rewarding work that leaves no visible trace requires deliberate effort from managers.
- **Some people love emergencies.** For some, the energy of a crisis is part of what they enjoy, and that preference deserves respect.
- **Sharing feels like loss.** Moving knowledge out of one head can feel threatening until the new role is clear.
- **The shift needs support.** Telling someone to change their identity rarely works; it has to be built together.

## Why it matters

People deserve to feel valued at work on the good days as well as the bad. An identity built on rescue makes calm feel like a threat. An identity built on craft, prevention and teaching makes calm feel like success. Helping people make that shift is one of the kinder things a workplace can do.
