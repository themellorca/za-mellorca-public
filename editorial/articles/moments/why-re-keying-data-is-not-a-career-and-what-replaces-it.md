Somewhere in most offices, a capable person spends part of the day copying numbers from one screen into another. An order confirmation arrives by email and is typed into the accounting package. A spreadsheet from a supplier is reformatted and pasted into the stock system. The work is not difficult, and it is not meaningless, because the numbers have to end up in the right place. But nobody chose a career in order to do it.

This piece looks at re-keying data as a question of dignity and choice, not only of efficiency. It is a hypothesis built on general research into job design and on public guidance about data quality, not a finding about any one South African employer, and the example is illustrative.

## What re-keying actually is

Re-keying means typing, by hand, information that already exists in digital form somewhere else. It happens wherever two systems do not talk to each other: a web form and a ledger, a booking tool and an invoice template, a bank statement and a reconciliation sheet. The task takes minutes each time, which is exactly why it survives. No single instance is bad enough to justify a project, and so the instances accumulate. The article on [how manual data transfer becomes an operational bottleneck](/insights/how-manual-data-transfer-becomes-an-operational-bottleneck/) describes that accumulation from the process side.

From the person's side, the problem is different. Re-keying uses almost none of what they know. A trained administrator understands customers, suppliers, exceptions and consequences. While re-keying, they are asked to act as a very slow cable between two programs.

## Why it wears people down

Self-determination theory, a long-established body of psychology research, names competence as one of the basic needs that support motivation: the feeling of using and growing one's abilities. Work that offers no use for those abilities, hour after hour, tends to work against that need. The UK Health and Safety Executive's management standards for work-related stress make a related point about control and role: people do better when they have a say in how their work is done and when the work matches what they were hired for. The HSE page on [control in the management standards](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/control.htm) sets out that thinking in plain language.

Three features of re-keying make it especially draining.

- **It is repetitive and exacting at once.** The task is dull, but one wrong digit still causes a problem later. Boredom and vigilance are an uncomfortable pairing.
- **It is invisible.** When the numbers arrive correctly, nobody notices the effort. When one is wrong, the person who typed it is the first to be asked.
- **It has no end.** Tomorrow brings another batch. There is no feeling of having finished anything, only of having kept up.

## The human tax: dignity and the loss of options

The first cost is dignity. Being asked to do, every day, something a machine could do reliably sends an unspoken message about how the business values the person's judgement. Often the message is unintended. The person in the role may feel it anyway.

The second cost is optionality, the room to do something else. The hours consumed by re-keying are hours not available for learning a new skill, handling a complex customer issue or moving towards a more senior role. In a country where many people work hard to progress from entry-level administration, a role that quietly consumes the time needed for growth is a barrier that does not appear on any organogram. For a closer look at skilled people stuck in this position, read [the employee whose expertise is trapped in data entry](/moments/the-employee-whose-expertise-is-trapped-in-data-entry/).

There is also a data-quality cost. South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act includes a condition that information be complete, accurate and not misleading where personal data is involved. Every manual copy is a chance for a transposed digit or a misspelt surname to enter a customer record. The Government's published text of the [Protection of Personal Information Act](https://www.gov.za/documents/protection-personal-information-act) is worth a read for any business that holds customer details in more than one place.

## An illustrative case

Consider a hypothetical Gqeberha parts supplier with a team of twelve. Customers place orders through a web form. An administrator copies each one into the accounting package, then types the delivery details into a separate courier booking site. It takes about six minutes per order, and the business handles around forty orders a day, which is roughly four hours of typing. The administrator is good at the job and has been offered nothing else to do.

One month, a courier address is mistyped and a pallet goes to the wrong depot. The customer is cross, the administrator is blamed and the manager reminds everyone to be careful. Nobody asks why a skilled person is typing the same address twice. The caution requested is another demand on a task already needing constant attention.

## Practical next actions

1. **Count the re-keying.** For one week, ask each person to tally how many times they type something that already exists on another screen. Even a rough count makes the issue concrete.
2. **Find the two systems that should talk.** Most re-keying sits between one pair of tools. Identify the pair, then check with the vendors whether an export, an import, or a built-in connection already exists. The guidance on [deciding which system owns which data](/insights/how-to-decide-which-system-owns-which-data/) helps settle which record is the master.
3. **Remove before automating.** Ask whether the second entry is needed at all. Sometimes a copy exists only because of an old habit.
4. **Ask the person doing the typing.** They usually know which step is the worst and what a fix should look like. Involving them also signals that their time is valued.
5. **Agree what the freed time is for.** Decide in advance whether it goes to customer follow-up, learning, a shorter day or a different role, so the saving is not silently absorbed by more typing.

## The business outcome

Cutting re-keying reduces transcription errors, shortens the gap between an order and its fulfilment, and frees capable people for work that needs a human. It also tends to surface hidden costs. When four hours of daily typing is counted, the case for a modest integration or a cleaner form often becomes obvious. The wider habit of measuring is described in [how much manual work is hiding inside your business](/insights/how-much-manual-work-is-hiding-inside-your-business/).

## The human dividend

The dividends are time and optionality. Time is the plain return of hours that were spent on transcription. Optionality is the more valuable one: the freedom to choose what to do with those hours, whether that is a harder problem, a course, a promotion path, or an earlier finish. What the person chooses is theirs to decide, and the business should resist the urge to refill the gap at once.

## The trade-offs

- **Integration has a cost.** Connecting systems takes money, testing and upkeep, and for very low volumes manual entry may remain the sensible choice.
- **Automated errors scale too.** A faulty connection can copy a wrong value into thousands of records faster than a person could.
- **Roles may need redesign.** If most of a job was re-keying, the business must be honest about what the role becomes, and about whether the person is being offered growth or being quietly made redundant.
- **Human checks have value.** Typing sometimes catches a bad number. Where it does, that check should be rebuilt on purpose and not lost by accident.

## Why it matters

Re-keying is a small task with a large shadow. Removing it is less about speed than about telling capable people that their time is worth more than the gap between two programs.
