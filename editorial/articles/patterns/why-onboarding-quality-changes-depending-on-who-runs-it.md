## Observable condition

Two new clients sign with a Stellenbosch accounting and payroll practice in the same month. One is onboarded by a senior manager who has done it for years. Within a fortnight the client's records are set up, access to their bank feeds is arranged, the first payroll run is agreed and the client knows exactly who to call.

The other is onboarded by a capable but newer colleague. Documents are requested in three separate emails. The payroll cut-off date is not explained. Bank feed access is set up a month late. The client starts the relationship slightly confused and slightly irritated.

Both staff members worked hard. The difference is that one carries a complete mental checklist and the other is assembling theirs as they go.

## Realisation

The realisation is that the business does not have one onboarding process. It has as many processes as people who run onboarding. Each person's version reflects their experience, memory and preferences. Some are excellent, some are adequate, and the client has no say in which they get.

This matters because onboarding sets expectations. A client or employee who starts well tends to stay longer and trust more. One who starts badly spends months recovering confidence.

**Realisation prompt:** ask two people who run onboarding to write down every step they take. Compare the lists. How many steps appear on only one?

## Identification

This is a pattern of person-dependent process. The visible symptom is variable outcomes for new clients or employees. The common assumption is that some staff are simply better at onboarding than others. The structural cause is that the steps, their order and their standard have never been written down, agreed and made the same for everyone.

It is closely related to [when the onboarding checklist exists only in one person's memory](/patterns/when-the-onboarding-checklist-exists-only-in-one-persons-memory/). That page looks at the risk when one person holds the process. This pattern looks at what happens when several people each hold a different version.

## Diagnosis

- **No agreed sequence.** Steps are done in whatever order the person running onboarding prefers, so some are forgotten until they cause a problem.
- **Templates differ.** Each person has their own welcome email, document request and checklist, with different wording and different gaps.
- **No definition of done.** Nobody has agreed what a fully onboarded client or employee looks like, so nobody can tell when onboarding is complete.
- **Learning is not shared.** When one person discovers a better way, it stays with them.
- **No feedback from the new client or employee.** The business does not ask how the start went, so it cannot compare outcomes.

## Commercial impact

The commercial value at stake is early retention, time to first value, staff time and reputation.

Early retention is the most important. Clients who have a confused start are more likely to leave in the first year, and that loss is rarely traced back to onboarding.

Time to first value varies too. A client whose payroll goes live in two weeks starts paying fees and seeing benefits sooner than one whose setup drags for six.

Staff time is wasted on rework. Missed steps are discovered later and fixed at greater cost, often by the senior person who should have been doing other work. The Impact note on [onboarding that depends on manager memory](/impact/onboarding-depends-on-manager-memory-checklists-inconsistent-employee-starts-cost/) describes the same cost for new employees.

Reputation suffers through comparison. Clients talk to each other. When one says their start was smooth and another says it was chaotic, both hear that quality depends on luck.

## Common misidentification

One misreading is that newer staff need more experience before they run onboarding. Experience helps, but it is a slow and expensive way to teach a process that could be written down.

Another is to buy onboarding software. Software can guide people through steps, but only once the steps have been agreed. Automating five different versions produces five different automated versions.

A third is to rely on the best person to do all onboarding. That works until volume grows or they take leave, and it turns the business's best people into a bottleneck. See [when client onboarding becomes a growth constraint](/patterns/when-client-onboarding-becomes-a-growth-constraint/).

## Possibility

The possibility is one agreed onboarding sequence, built from the best practice of the people who already do it well. Each stage has defined steps, a clear owner and a definition of complete. Anyone running onboarding follows the same path, and the tools they use show them where they are.

[Microsoft's overview of business process flows](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-automate/business-process-flows-overview) describes defining stages and steps so people follow the same process every time, and notes that this helps new staff get up to speed and avoid mistakes. A small practice can apply the same idea with a shared checklist and a tracking list. Repetitive steps such as sending welcome packs and document reminders can be automated with [cloud flows](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-automate/overview-cloud).

In that business, quality no longer depends on who picks up the new client. Experienced staff improve the shared process rather than holding a private one. The Moment [why documented processes help people grow into roles](/moments/why-documented-processes-help-people-grow-into-roles/) captures the benefit for newer staff.

## Intervention

Ask everyone who runs onboarding to list their steps independently. Merge the lists into one, keeping the best version of each step and adding anything only one person remembered.

Group the steps into four or five stages, such as agreement signed, information gathered, systems set up, first delivery and review. Define what must be true to leave each stage.

Create shared templates for every client-facing message. Retire personal versions.

Track each new client through the stages in one list. After thirty days, ask the client a few simple questions about how their start went, and compare results across the people who ran it.

## Practical next actions

1. Ask each person who runs onboarding to write down every step they take, independently.
2. Merge the lists into one agreed sequence and group it into four or five stages with clear exit criteria.
3. Replace personal welcome emails and document requests with shared templates.
4. Track every new client or employee through the stages in one visible list.
5. Collect brief feedback from each new client or employee at thirty days and compare outcomes.
6. Review the sequence every quarter and add improvements discovered by any team member.

## Diagnostic questions

- If two of our people onboarded the same client, would the client have the same experience?
- What does fully onboarded mean in our business, and is it written down?
- How many of our first-year client losses had a difficult start?
- When someone finds a better way to onboard, how does the rest of the team learn about it?

## Bottom line

When onboarding quality depends on who runs it, the business has habits rather than a process. Gather the best of what experienced staff already do, agree one sequence with clear stages, share the templates and track every new start. Clients and employees get the same good beginning, whoever is on duty.
