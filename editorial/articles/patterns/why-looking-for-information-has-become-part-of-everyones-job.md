## Observable condition

A sales administrator needs the current price for a product that was repriced last month. She checks the price list on the shared drive, finds two versions, messages a colleague to ask which is right, and waits. A technician on site needs the installation notes from the last visit. He phones the office, and someone stops what they are doing to look through a job folder and an email thread. A new bookkeeper needs to know how a particular supplier's rebate is handled. Nobody has written it down, so she asks three people and gets two different answers.

None of these moments is dramatic. Each one takes a few minutes, perhaps a quarter of an hour. Nobody records them, and nobody's job description mentions them. Yet across a working day, almost everyone in the business is doing some version of the same thing: looking for information that already exists somewhere, so they can do the work they are actually paid to do.

## Realisation

Searching is usually treated as a personal skill or a minor irritation. Some people are good at finding things; others are not. The realisation is that searching is a workload, and it is spread so thinly across so many people that it never shows up as a cost. It does not appear in a budget, a timesheet or a management report. It shows up indirectly: in slower turnaround, in interruptions, and in decisions made on whatever information was easiest to reach rather than what was correct.

There is a second, less obvious cost. Every search that ends in a question to a colleague transfers the cost to someone else. The person who knows where things are becomes the business's informal search engine, and their own work is interrupted many times a day. A related pattern is examined in [why customer service becomes a human search engine](/patterns/why-customer-service-becomes-a-human-search-engine/), where the same dynamic plays out at the customer front line.

Realisation prompt: ask five people in different roles how many times yesterday they had to look for, or ask about, information they needed for a routine task. Then ask how many times someone asked them. The two answers together describe the search workload the business is carrying.

## Identification

This is an information findability problem: the business stores information, but not in a way that makes the right version reachable at the moment of work. It overlaps with the knowledge-retrieval pattern in [why nobody can find the document they know exists](/patterns/why-can-nobody-find-the-document-they-know-exists/), which focuses on documents that are stored but unusable. This pattern is broader. It concerns every kind of operational information: prices, terms, customer history, procedures, decisions and the knowledge of who knows what.

Research on how people seek information helps explain the behaviour. The [Nielsen Norman Group's summary of information foraging theory](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-foraging/) describes people weighing the value of information against the cost of obtaining it, including the time spent and the alternatives given up. When finding the right answer is costly, people rationally settle for a nearby answer, or ask someone, or guess.

## Diagnosis

The visible symptom is time spent looking. The common assumption is that staff need better search habits or that a better search tool would fix it. The structural causes usually sit elsewhere:

- **No designated home for each type of information.** Prices live in a spreadsheet, a quoting tool and old emails. Customer history lives in the accounting system, a CRM and personal inboxes. When information has several possible homes, every search has to check all of them.
- **Versions without an authority.** Copies multiply, and nothing marks which one is current. People then have to ask, because finding a document is not the same as knowing it is right.
- **Information organised by person, not by process.** Folders reflect who created them rather than the work they support, so finding something requires knowing who made it.
- **Knowledge that was never written down.** Procedures and exceptions live in long-serving staff, so the only search method is to ask them.
- **Search that cannot reach where the information lives.** Workplace search tools generally only show people what they already have permission to see. Microsoft's [overview of Microsoft Search](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoftsearch/overview-microsoft-search), for example, notes that it does not change permissions and that users only see results they already have access to. That is correct behaviour, but it means a search tool cannot compensate for information kept in private folders, personal mailboxes or systems it does not index.

The common thread is that the business has never decided where each kind of information belongs and who keeps it current. Search fails because there is no reliable answer to search for.

## Commercial impact

The commercial value at stake is productivity, speed, decision quality and resilience.

- **Productivity.** Skilled people spend part of every day on retrieval rather than on the work customers pay for. The cost of that time is explored in [the cost of paid time spent searching for files](/impact/employees-searching-for-files-lost-productivity-cost/).
- **Speed.** Quotes, answers to customers and on-site decisions wait while someone looks or asks.
- **Decision quality.** When the right information is hard to reach, people use what is easy to reach. An outdated price or a superseded procedure costs more than the search would have.
- **Capacity of key people.** The colleagues who know where things are lose focus to a steady stream of questions.
- **Resilience.** When knowledge can only be found by asking specific people, their absence or departure removes the business's ability to find it.

The size of the effect varies by business and should be estimated from its own evidence, such as a short sample of how often staff search or ask, and how long it takes.

## Common misidentification

The usual response is to buy a better search tool, or more recently, an AI assistant that promises to answer questions from company content. Both can help, but only where the underlying information is organised, current and accessible. An assistant pointed at duplicate price lists and contradictory procedures will find them quickly and present them confidently. The limits of that approach are covered in [when AI search fails because knowledge is chaotic](/patterns/when-ai-search-fails-because-knowledge-is-chaotic/).

The opposite error is a one-off clean-up of the shared drive. Without ownership and rules about where new information goes, the drive returns to its previous state within months.

## Possibility

In a stronger state, each important type of information has one home and one owner. Prices are maintained in one place and everyone else links to them. Customer history sits on the customer record rather than in individual inboxes. Procedures are written where the work happens and are reviewed by someone accountable for them. Search then becomes reliable, because there is one current answer to find.

People still ask each other questions, but they ask about judgement and context rather than about where things are.

## Intervention

1. **Sample the search load.** For one week, ask a few people in different roles to note each time they look for or ask about routine information. Group the results by type of information.
2. **Decide the home for the top categories.** For the five or six most-searched types of information, agree a single authoritative location. The reasoning is set out in [how to decide what should be the system of record](/insights/how-to-decide-what-should-be-the-system-of-record/).
3. **Assign owners.** Each category needs a named person responsible for keeping it current and retiring old versions.
4. **Move information to the point of work.** Link procedures and reference data from the systems where tasks are done, so people do not have to leave the task to search.
5. **Capture the knowledge that lives in people.** Start with the questions most often asked of key staff and write the answers down where others will find them.
6. **Then improve search.** Once the content is organised, search tools and AI assistants have something reliable to work with.

## An illustrative example

The following is illustrative, not a client case. A Durban industrial supplies distributor has twelve staff. The internal sales team keeps being asked for current prices and stock lead times, and the warehouse supervisor is asked several times a day where a customer's special delivery instructions are kept. When the owner asks the team to note their searches for a week, prices, delivery instructions and supplier terms account for most of them. Each lived in at least three places. The business agrees one price list maintained by the buyer, moves delivery instructions onto the customer record and writes up supplier terms in a single shared page. The questions do not stop entirely, but most of them now have an answer that anyone can find.

## Practical diagnostic questions

- Which types of information do people most often look for or ask about?
- For each, is there one place that is definitely current?
- Who is responsible for keeping it current?
- Which colleagues are interrupted most often with "where is" questions?
- If a long-serving employee left tomorrow, which information would become unfindable?

## Bottom line

When everyone spends part of the day looking for information, the problem is rarely the people or the search box. It is that the business has not decided where each kind of information belongs and who keeps it right. Fixing that turns a hidden, shared workload into time returned to productive work, and makes later investments in search and AI far more useful.
